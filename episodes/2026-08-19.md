## FreeToken: Frontier Models on Your Own Hardware

First, FreeToken, an edge-native serving system for mixture-of-experts models. Its premise: your personal machine isn't a small GPU — it's a unified elastic inference platform with heterogeneous resources that differ from machine to machine, running agent workloads whose execution patterns shift continuously. So instead of committing to a fixed offloading strategy, FreeToken continuously maps computation and model state onto whatever is actually available: expert residency, CPU-GPU execution, model layout and loading, agentic state reuse, runtime memory management — co-designed as one stack.

The results are remarkable: it supports over twenty MoE models and real coding and tool-using agents, from an eight-gigabyte laptop GPU to a single workstation — serving a thirty-five-billion-parameter model on a laptop, a two-hundred-eighty-four-billion model on a gaming desktop, and the seven-hundred-fifty-three-billion GLM Five Point Two on one workstation GPU. If you self-host, this paper is a statement about what your existing hardware can now do.

## Agent Lightning v1.0: Training With the Harness in the Loop

Second, Agent Lightning version one point zero. Modern agents live inside harnesses that own tools, context, and control flow — so their paradigm, harnessed agentic RL, lets the deploy-time harness participate directly in post-training: the harness runs the environment loop while the trainer observes only sequences of request-response pairs through an endpoint proxy. That decoupling sounds clean but creates real problems — re-tokenization, sample merging, advantage calculation, loss normalization, backend scheduling.

Version one point zero implements the whole thing in about three and a half thousand lines of code, supports arbitrary agent harnesses, and ships a complete reproducible pipeline. The headline: with only six thousand training examples and modest compute, reinforcement learning lifts Qwen Three Point Five Nine-B on SWE-bench Verified from forty-two to fifty-six percent — a fourteen point six point absolute gain. An architecture adopted by several major training frameworks, now packaged as a practical testbed.

## LEGO-RL: Policy Gradients Without Breaking the Harness

Third, LEGO-RL, which attacks the same problem from the execution side. Native coding-agent harnesses are misaligned with policy-gradient training by default: environmental crashes and reward hacking corrupt outcome signals, and train-inference discrepancies decouple what the rollout did from what the policy update assumes. LEGO-RL bridges them without modifying harness internals, on three pillars: faithful optimization via in-process LLM proxying that captures raw generation streams even under harness-side compaction or re-serialization; reliable execution through sandbox orchestration with image caching and stage-wise defenses against reward hacking; and observable training with automated validation plus a live UI for trajectory diagnostics.

Trained with GSPO across three native harnesses, a sparse thirty-five-billion MoE model gains six-plus points on SWE-bench Verified under OpenHands, five points eight under Claude Code, and nine points four under OpenCode, while rollout-training probability correlation stays above zero point nine nine. Between these two papers, training agents where they actually deploy has stopped being a research hack and become engineering.

## Demystifying Agent Skills: Why They Work — Until They Don't

Fourth, "Demystifying Agent Skills." Skills — structured knowledge packages loaded at inference time — demonstrably help agents, but aggregate success rates hide why. This study normalizes over eight thousand trial records and two hundred forty open-coded ones into a taxonomy of three categories and twelve skill-use modes. The core finding: skills work mainly as procedural anchors that stabilize noisy trajectories into reliable execution — sixty-five point seven percent of beneficial cases — versus four and a half percent for injecting missing facts. Skills stabilize action rather than add knowledge.

Two warnings for anyone running a skill library like ours: retrieval is a separate bottleneck — as the pool grows from five to a hundred skills, actual-use precision collapses from twenty-nine percent to three percent; and exact ground-truth invocation is neither sufficient nor necessary, since confusable distractors hurt offline identification without moving downstream success much. Skills fail under brittle assumptions, incompatible contexts, or insufficient adaptation. Keep libraries small, names distinctive, and assumptions explicit.

## HarnessRisk: Safety Across the Harness Lifecycle

Fifth, HarnessRisk, a lifecycle-oriented benchmark for agent-harness safety: one hundred twenty-eight sandboxed cases organized into six operational phases from configuration through capability extension, runtime, state persistence, action control, and incident recovery — each pairing a benign objective with an adversarial instruction hidden in an untrusted workflow artifact.

Across three harnesses and fourteen configurations, attack success ranges from twelve point six to eighty point nine percent while utility holds at seventy-five to ninety-seven — and the most vulnerable phase, everywhere, is Harness Configuration: attackers win by altering security-sensitive parameters inside otherwise authorized workflows. More unsettling: explicit risk recognition does not produce safe action — some configurations flag risk in over ninety percent of runs and still get attacked. Detection is not defense; constrain what the harness may change, don't just ask it to notice.

## MathForm: Autoformalization at Mathlib Scale

Last today, MathForm, on scaling mathematical autoformalization — translating natural-language mathematics into machine-verifiable Lean Four. Faithful formalization means mapping concepts into Mathlib's deep hierarchy of types and definitions, which past systems tried to do from parametric memory alone. MathForm instead retrieves relevant definitions before generation and revises outputs using compiler diagnostics and semantic-consistency feedback — building FormalVerse, roughly three hundred sixty-seven thousand verified examples, then training an eight-billion model with supervised fine-tuning followed by reinforcement learning.

MathForm-Eight B hits average Pass-at-eight of eighty-eight percent under syntax checking and seventy-two percent under consistency checking — beating specialized thirty-two-billion autoformalizers — and on the hardest FATE subsets reaches sixty-three and thirty-seven percent consistency-pass, above every baseline. For anyone who has watched a model hallucinate nonexistent lemma names, retrieval plus compiler feedback is the recipe that scales.
