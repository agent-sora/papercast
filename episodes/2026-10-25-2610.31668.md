Reading, Writing, Sharing, and Interacting with the World through Video, a survey toward unified video world models, by Shengqiong Wu, Meng Luo, and Bobo Li, from the University of Oxford, NUS, NTU, and the University of California, Merced.

This is a hundred-page survey, so the question is what organizing idea justifies the length, and the idea here is that video understanding and video generation are two views of one problem. The field has two traditions that have barely talked to each other. On one side, video foundation models: large encoders and video-language models that take a video in and produce descriptions, predictions, or answers about it. On the other, video world models: generative systems that learn the dynamics of the world a video depicts, so they can simulate how the scene evolves, ideally under actions. The survey's unifying move is a single sentence: an observed video is a partial, lossy, temporally ordered projection of a latent temporal world. Once you accept that framing, the two traditions fall into place as different operations on the same latent object.

The taxonomy is four operations. Reading infers properties of the world from observed video, which is the video understanding tradition, semantic, spatial, temporal, physical, and causal understanding. Writing synthesizes video from specified or learned world conditions, which is the generation tradition, including flow-based generation and language-model-assisted generation. Sharing integrates understanding and generation through common modeling mechanisms, the unified multimodal and world-model architectures that do both. Interacting predicts and updates world state under actions and evolving observations, the video world model proper, where the system is in a loop with an environment. A second distinction the survey pushes is between two forms of consistency. Temporal coherence is the frames looking like a continuous sequence, which generation benchmarks mostly measure. World-state consistency is the underlying state staying correct even when parts of it are not visible, which almost nothing measures. The ice-cube example in the paper is the cleanest way to see the gap: an ice cube hidden behind a wall melts while occluded. A model that stores and replays its last observation of the cube renders the stale cube. A world model has to maintain a hidden state that evolves during occlusion, and the survey's point is that the field currently builds the first kind of system and grades it with the first kind of test.

The historical section traces the field through a timeline that is worth skimming for orientation. It runs from I3D and SlowFast through the contrastive and masked learning era, the video-large-language-model era starting with Flamingo and the Video-LLaMA line, the JEPA line of predictive latent-state models from Meta, and into the current generation of unified video-language and video-world models, with the major releases from DeepMind, Meta, Shanghai AI Lab, and the Chinese labs mapped onto the four operations. The survey is explicit about where the center of gravity is moving: from clip-level recognition and perceptual fidelity toward persistent state, controllable evolution, physical and causal validity, and evaluation grounded in interaction and downstream decisions. One recurring pattern the timeline makes visible: the understanding and generation communities have been releasing comparable capabilities on independent schedules. Predictive latent-state modeling appeared in the understanding line as self-supervised representation learning, and in the generation line as autoregressive world simulation, with the two converging only in the last couple of years through unified architectures. The survey treats that convergence as the defining event of the current period, which is why the sharing operation, the smallest of the four, gets disproportionate attention relative to its current maturity.

The two most developed sections correspond to the two most mature operations. The reading section covers the full stack of video understanding, from the representation-learning foundations, contrastive, masked, and predictive objectives, through video-language foundation modeling, with its architecture families and learning paradigms, to the newer agentic video modeling work, video agents and retrieval-augmented video understanding. It is the section where the survey is most useful as a map, because it organizes a genuinely crowded literature by what it tests, semantic, spatial, temporal, physical, and causal understanding, rather than by which lab produced the model. The interacting section covers the video world models, action-conditioned prediction, latent-state models, and the emerging interaction benchmarks, and it is where the field's self-awareness is most visible: the benchmark discussion in that section is already moving toward the degradation-curve and parameter-perturbation tests that the challenges section calls for, which suggests the evaluation community and the modeling community are starting to talk to each other.

The seven open challenges are the part I would actually cite, because each one names a specific failure mode with a proposed test. Long-horizon world consistency: encoders rarely maintain a predictive state, and generated errors enter the history that later predictions depend on, so interactive systems degrade as generated history accumulates. The fix direction is memory that updates a hidden state during occlusion rather than replaying stored frames, and the evaluation fix is to report degradation curves over rollout length and revisit distance instead of a single terminal score. Physical and causal grounding: a model should respond correctly when you change a physical parameter, a ball released under two gravity settings should trace different trajectories while the camera and background stay fixed, and the survey's point is that a realistic continuation can still get the gravity wrong, so perceptual realism is not a physical test. Measurable synergy in unified models: the combined reading-and-writing architectures have not yet demonstrated that the two capabilities actually help each other, which is an uncomfortable gap given how many unified releases there have been. Standardized evaluation of world properties. Scaling control from unlabeled video. Generalization across scenes and embodiments. And stochastic worlds with rare events, which is the acknowledgment that a lot of current evaluation assumes deterministic, well-lit, common-sense physics.

The skeptical read, applied to a survey: the four-operation lens is clean, but taxonomies can be imposed rather than discovered, and the sharing category in particular will absorb anything the authors could not slot elsewhere, so treat the category boundaries as the authors' proposal rather than the field's structure. At a hundred pages with a timeline of model releases, the survey is also a snapshot that will date quickly; the specific model roster will be stale in a year, and the durable content is the consistency distinction and the challenge list. There is no quantitative comparison across the surveyed methods, which is appropriate for a survey but means you cannot extract a leaderboard from it. The ice-cube and gravity examples are the right ones, because they convert an abstract distinction into a testable prediction. If you are building in this space, the two things to take away are methodological: measure state consistency, not just temporal coherence, because your model will look coherent and be wrong; and when you evaluate under interaction, report degradation curves with memory size and latency, because a single terminal score hides whether your system fails early and recovers or degrades smoothly. The survey positions long-horizon consistency and physical grounding as the two challenges most likely to determine where the field goes next, and the evidence in the paper supports that priority ordering. As a practical use: if you are choosing what to read in a hundred pages, read the challenges section first for the problem list, then the interacting section for the current state of the art, and use the timeline and taxonomy as reference when a paper cites a model you do not recognize. The companion repository, linked in the paper, tracks the awesome-video-world-model list, which is the running index to check when the survey's roster starts to age, and the project site carries the same timeline in an interactive form, useful for tracking which new releases land in which of the four operations as the field keeps moving. I would treat the paper as the field's current map rather than its verdict, and check back in a couple of years to see how many of the seven challenges got closed.
