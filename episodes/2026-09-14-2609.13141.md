Good evening, paper three tonight is SAS: Simple Attention Sparsification via End-to-End Optimization of Context Ranking, from Zhiwei Li, Lei Zhu, and Hao Gu, working out of the Tencent HY LLM Frontier team and the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Guangzhou. This one falls squarely into the reasoning-model topology bucket, because it changes the attention computation itself. The pitch is that you can sparsify a long-context transformer's attention, cut the number of keys and values each query has to attend to, and do it end-to-end by learning a context ranking, and that doing it the simple way is enough to beat a lot of the more elaborate sparse-attention schemes.

The motivation is the standard long-context cost problem, so I will be brief. Full attention is quadratic in sequence length. For long contexts, that is where both the time and the memory go. Sparse attention methods attack this by making each query attend to only a subset of the keys and values, and the hard part is deciding which subset. You need a way to rank the context by relevance, and you need that ranking to actually track what the full attention would have weighted, or your accuracy dies. Most prior methods use a hand-designed or heuristic rule for the selection. A sliding window, a fixed stride, or a separate learned selector trained with its own surrogate loss. The problem with a separate selector trained on a surrogate is a mismatch. The selector optimizes an approximation of the objective, while the downstream transformer is optimizing the real one, and the two do not always agree.

SAS's core idea is to make the ranking part of the same end-to-end objective as the model, so there is no surrogate gap. The mechanism is a gated sparse attention. The context is divided into blocks, and each block gets a learned gate, a scalar score that modulates how much that block contributes to the attention. The gate is injected into the attention logits, so the attention probability for a token is scaled by the gate of the block it belongs to. The gate scores are computed from the model's own representations, not from a frozen heuristic, so as the model adapts to a task, the notion of which context blocks are relevant can shift with it. That is the end-to-end part, and it is the part that is supposed to remove the mismatch between what the selector thinks is important and what the task actually needs. The paper walks through the gradient derivation for the gate in both an outer and an inner position, and the ablations show the inner-softmax gate, where the gate is injected into the logits before the softmax, gives lower training loss than the outer formulation.

The "simple" in the title is doing a lot of rhetorical work, and I think it is fair. The contribution is not a new attention kernel and not a new theoretical sparsity result. It is an observation that a straightforward learned, gated block ranking, optimized jointly with the model, is hard to beat. That is a legitimate engineering result. The value of a simple method that you can reproduce from the paper in a week, and that holds up against fancier baselines, is real, especially if the fancy baselines are hard to train or hard to debug. The ablations make an important point about why the design has to be the way it is. Independent sigmoid gates and raw logit injection achieve even lower training loss, but they fail to improve sparsification, because both effectively approach ungated attention and the model learns almost no discriminative block ranking. The straight-through estimator formulation also gives lower loss but inferior final sparsification. The takeaway the authors state directly is that training loss alone is insufficient to assess ranking quality. You need to preserve a meaningful, discriminative block ranking signal, or the loss will happily go down while the sparsity you are trying to buy quietly disappears.

Now the numbers, and I want to give you the actual RULER results, because that is where this either stands or falls. They evaluate on the RULER benchmark, which is the standard long-context retrieval and needle test, across Qwen3 at 4B, 8B, and 14B, at context lengths from 4K up to 128K, and they extend the window to 128K using YaRN. They compare against a full-attention baseline and against SeerAttention-R. Take the 8B model at the 4096 budget. Full attention sits at 92.96 at 4K, 90.13 at 8K, 77.39 at 16K, 57.92 at 32K, 37.43 at 64K, and 18.75 at 128K. SAS at the same budget is 92.96 at 4K, 87.96 at 8K, 79.88 at 16K, 63.50 at 32K, 39.18 at 64K, and 23.46 at 128K. So SAS is essentially tied with full attention at the short end, beats SeerAttention-R at every length, and holds up better than SeerAttention-R as the context stretches. At 32K, SeerAttention-R is at 57.92 while SAS is at 63.50, and at 64K, SeerAttention-R is at 37.43 while SAS is at 39.18. That is a real, if modest, win at the hard end.

Now the honest limitation, and the authors put it in the paper, which I appreciate. As the context length increases, SAS's performance degrades substantially and leaves a considerable gap from full attention. At 128K, even the 14B model is at 29.95 under SAS versus 82.23 for full attention. That is not a small gap, and it is the number to remember. The authors attribute it to the use of pooling-based block summaries. When you compress a block down to a single pooled vector for gating, you lose fine-grained, localized information, and that hurts exactly the needle-like signals that long-context retrieval depends on, and it gets worse as the context length grows. So the method is genuinely effective in the mid-range, and it is genuinely limited at the very long end. That is the trade-off curve, stated plainly, and it is the whole product.

There is also a training-cost result that is worth your attention for practical deployment. They train the selector on 0.5 billion tokens sampled from a long-context mixture, on 64K sequences with a packing strategy, and they compare a full training scope against a sparse scope, where the sparse scope only updates the selected blocks directly and updates the unselected ones indirectly. Across the reasoning benchmarks, MATH500, GPQA-Diamond, and AIME, the sparse scope matches the full scope at convergence across all model scales and token budgets, while incurring lower training cost. On MATH500 at the 4096 budget, for instance, the 14B model is at 95.23 under sparse scope versus 94.12 under full scope, and on AIME25 the 14B is at 78.28 versus 78.49. So the sparsity you get at inference is not costing you accuracy at training, which is the result you want to see.

Here is the skeptical assessment. The number you should trust, if the paper delivers it, is the accuracy at a fixed FLOP or memory budget, not the FLOP saving at fixed accuracy, and the RULER curve is exactly where those two framings can disagree. The mid-context win over SeerAttention-R is credible and reproducible from the table I just read. What I would re-verify before adopting SAS into a serving stack is the long-context behavior. The gap to full attention at 128K is large, and if your deployment lives at 100K-plus contexts, this method is not yet there. I would want to see the behavior when the relevant information is a needle buried in a single block, because the pooling-based block summary is the stated weak point, and I would want the actual wall-clock and memory on real hardware, not the theoretical FLOP count, because the selection and gating step has its own cost, and at high sparsity that cost eats into the theoretical saving. And I would test the interaction with YaRN, the position interpolation they use to reach 128K, because sparse gating and learned position extrapolation can interact in ways the needle benchmarks do not fully expose.

Here is the bottom line. SAS is a clean, well-posed contribution to sparse attention, and its strength is the end-to-end, jointly-optimized gated block ranking that removes the surrogate gap in prior learned selectors, with a training-cost result that confirms you do not pay for the sparsity at training time. It wins over SeerAttention-R across the mid-context range on RULER, but it degrades substantially against full attention at 128K because of pooling-based block summaries, so read the trade-off curve, not the headline, before you trust it for very long contexts.
