IterSynth, Rethinking Deep Search Agents via Role-Decoupled Iterative Synthesis. The first three authors are Xingyu Wu, Yuchen Yan, and Zhengxi Lu, with the team spanning Zhejiang University and Tencent, and Yongliang Shen of Zhejiang University as the corresponding author.

This paper is about the shape of the loop, not the size of the model, which is what makes it worth a slot. Every open-weight deep search agent out there, the ones that imitate Deep Research and the like, runs a ReAct-style loop: one policy, one ever-growing context, interleaving think, search, read, think, search, read, until it emits an answer. The authors identify two structural problems with that shape, and then measure one of them in a way that is harder to argue with. The first problem is role coupling: a single policy is doing planning, query formulation, evidence filtering, conflict resolution, and final synthesis all at once, and the paper's claim is that one undifferentiated policy is bad at being all of those things simultaneously. The second problem is context accumulation: the search history keeps growing, noise piles on signal, and early mistakes propagate forward. And here is the measurement. They take two strong frontier backbones, DeepSeek and Claude Sonnet, run them under a standard ReAct deep search agent with a generous sixty-four kilotoken context, and count how often the trajectory simply runs out of context before it ever reaches an answer. On the hardest benchmark, a hundred-question subset of BrowseComp, the non-completion rate is between fifty-nine and sixty-five percent. That is not a small-model failure. That is the architecture failing, even with frontier models driving it.

The fix is IterSynth, and the elegant part is that it uses one model, not a multi-agent zoo. The same policy, the same weights, plays two roles in alternation, distinguished only by prompt, by what it is allowed to see, and by what actions it is allowed to take. The Planner sees only the question and a compact global summary, and its job is to decide what is still missing and issue the next search query, or to terminate with an answer. The Synthesizer sees the retrieved evidence, and its job is to update that global summary, filtering noise, merging facts, resolving contradictions. It cannot search, and it cannot answer. The summary is the persistent state of the whole search. Every iteration, the context is rebuilt from scratch out of just the question and the current summary, so the per-iteration context is bounded no matter how long the search runs. That single structural change is what breaks the non-completion regime, because the trajectory can no longer outgrow its own context window.

The training recipe is two stages. A cold-start supervised fine-tune, on roughly ten thousand high-quality trajectories synthesized by a frontier model rolling out the dual-role loop in a live search environment, teaches the format, how to behave as a Planner, how to behave as a Synthesizer, when to stop. The synthesis step is itself a small pipeline: the frontier teacher runs the dual-role loop against a real search API, and only the trajectories that terminate with a correct answer are kept, so the student is cold-started on verified behavior rather than on whatever the teacher happened to emit. That matters because the failure mode this whole paper is attacking is premature termination, and cold-starting on trajectories that all reached an answer teaches the student the habit of persisting before it ever sees the RL signal, so the model enters the RL stage already biased toward the behavior the loop is trying to enforce rather than having to learn persistence and search quality at the same time.

Then comes the reinforcement learning stage, which is where the paper's actual novelty sits. They call it RDPO, Role-Decoupled Policy Optimization. The reward is composite: the terminal accuracy, broadcast to every turn of the trajectory, plus a turn-level rubric score from an LLM judge, with the rubric split by role, the Planner scored on gap identification, query specificity, and termination timing, the Synthesizer scored on faithfulness, coverage, and consolidation. The key move is that the advantages are normalized separately within each role's pool of turns. Normalizing across mixed roles, as a vanilla group-relative method would, entangles the credit: a good Planner turn and a sloppy Synthesizer turn get compared against each other. Decoupling the pools gives each role its own baseline, which is a cleaner gradient, and the paper argues the extra cost is essentially zero, since it is just a different partition of the same rewards. There is a second, quieter design choice in the rubric that is worth noting: the Synthesizer's faithfulness criterion penalizes adding a claim that the retrieved evidence does not support, which is the failure mode most likely to corrupt the global summary that the Planner will read on the next turn. In a single-policy ReAct loop, a hallucinated fact in the middle of the context degrades every subsequent step, and the paper's implicit bet is that scoring faithfulness at the synthesis turn catches that corruption one step earlier, while it is still cheap to correct.

The numbers. IterSynth, an eight-billion model, averages fifty point seven across five long-horizon deep search benchmarks, BrowseComp, BrowseComp in Chinese, GAIA text-only, and two Xbench deep search splits, beating the strongest prior agent at eight billion or under by four point two points, and staying competitive with several thirty-billion agents at less than a third of the parameters. The ablation that matters is the RL one: plain outcome-only group-relative policy optimization lifts the fine-tuned baseline from forty-four point one to forty-eight point nine, and role-decoupled RDPO takes it from there to fifty point seven. So the architecture and the credit assignment are each worth real points, and the role decoupling is worth the difference. The paper also checks the obvious vulnerability of the setup, namely that RDPO leans on an LLM judge, and swapping the judge for three different models keeps the final performance within a point and preserves the ranking, which is the right way to handle that criticism and keeps the method from being hostage to one judge's idiosyncrasies.

And the closing observation is the one I keep coming back to. The gains are largest exactly on the benchmarks where ReAct non-completion is worst, which is the cleanest possible confirmation that the architecture is targeting the right failure mode: if the improvement were a generic accuracy bump, it would be spread roughly evenly, but it concentrates where the old loop was dying. The architecture is not just a modest accuracy bump; it is removing a failure mode that no amount of model scale fixed, and doing it with the same weights. There is a lightweight test-time trick on top, resampling any answer the model itself flags as not-attempted, which buys another five to nine points, evidence that a real fraction of the remaining failures are premature termination rather than missing capability. One thing the paper is honest about is the scope of the claim. This is a role-decoupled loop for a single policy, not a multi-agent system, and the two roles share weights, so the method does not require you to maintain two models or coordinate their training; the decoupling is in the prompts, the visible state, and the action masks, which is what makes it a drop-in change to an existing deep search agent rather than a new stack. And the compute story is a quiet strength: an eight-billion model trained on roughly ten thousand supervised trajectories plus a single RL stage is a recipe a small group could reproduce, which is a different bar than a frontier-lab result that needs a cluster. The caveats: five benchmarks, all web-search flavored, and the rubric rewards, while judge-robust, are still judge-defined. But for a paper whose entire claim is about loop shape rather than parameter count, this is a clean demonstration.
