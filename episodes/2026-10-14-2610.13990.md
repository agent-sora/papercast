DoSA: Dots Sparse Attention, by Fanxu Meng and Taosong Fang, both from the Dots Studio at Xiaohongshu. This is one of the papers on today's list that changes the attention mechanism itself, and it is aimed at a specific, measurable bottleneck in a production architecture: DeepSeek-style sparse attention. The setup is that sparse attention, which is what models like GLM-5.2 and DeepSeek-V3.2 use at long context, selects a small set of keys with a multi-head indexer followed by a top-K selection. But finding those keys still requires scoring every key with every indexer head, so the indexer is dense even though the attention that follows is sparse. At roughly one million tokens, the paper measures the indexer as 73 percent of prefill GPU time in GLM-5.2 and 90.8 percent in DeepSeek-V3.2. That is the problem DoSA attacks: making the indexer's scoring conditional, without changing which tokens get selected.

The central observation is clean and it is worth stating precisely. A token cannot enter the top K if an upper bound on its score falls below a lower bound on the true top-K threshold. A small seed set of at least K keys with fully computed scores supplies that lower bound, because the K-th largest score of the seed is a lower bound on the K-th largest score of the whole set. So the entire method is a certificate: for each candidate key, compute a cheap upper bound on its full indexer score, and only pay for the full score when the bound is high enough to keep the key eligible. If the bound is below the threshold, the key is provably excluded from the top K, and no winner is ever discarded.

The indexer score in question is a signed sum of ReLUs: for each of the H heads, take the dot product of the head's query with the key, apply a ReLU, and multiply by the head's weight, then sum. The certificate has to bound that. DoSA retains a small set of heads, say r out of H, and scores them exactly. For the omitted positive-weight heads, it exploits a nonnegative projection: each omitted query is projected onto a retained anchor query, and ReLU subadditivity plus Cauchy-Schwarz give a bound where the projected part reuses a dot product that was already computed, and only a residual norm term, times the key norm, is new. A joint residual bound then tightens the allowance by exploiting the geometry of the residuals, which share the same key. For the omitted negative heads, the trick is an aggregate: instead of bounding each negative head separately, it aggregates a subset of them into a single weighted query and applies one ReLU to the aggregate, which lower-bounds the true amount those heads subtract, because ReLU is subadditive. Which negative heads to aggregate is chosen by seed activation patterns, using component-wise majority support, so that inactive heads, which contribute zero after their own ReLU, do not weaken the bound through cancellation.

The implementation is the other half of the contribution, and it is unusually concrete about hardware. The pipeline has four stages. Screening dot products run on tensor cores over a compact set of query rows. A bound-evaluation stage on SIMT threads forms the upper bound for each candidate, prunes those below the threshold, and compacts the survivors. Only survivors get full-head dot products on tensor cores again. The final reduction computes the original score and writes back only scores that meet the seed-derived threshold, which is a memory-traffic saving on top of the compute saving. The query-side quantities, the folded screening weights and the residual allowances, are computed once per query and reused across keys, so the certificate's overhead does not scale with context length in the same way full scoring does. The folded weights belong to the screening plan only; they do not modify any model parameter, which is the property that makes this drop-in: no retraining, no quantization of the indexer, just a different execution order.

Numbers. Across English, Chinese, and code workloads, DoSA beats both baselines, SGLang's native indexer path and LiteTopK, in all 60 microbenchmark configurations. Complete-indexer speedups reach 2.74 times over SGLang and 1.69 times over LiteTopK. End to end, on GLM-5.2 and DeepSeek-V3.2 at roughly one million tokens, time-to-first-token speedups are 1.86 times and 1.94 times over SGLang respectively, with up to 1.50 times over LiteTopK, which the authors describe as nearly halving end-to-end prefill latency. The trend is favorable to the method at long context: the indexer's share of prefill time climbs from 13.7 percent at 64K tokens to 73 percent at one million in GLM-5.2, so the bigger the context the bigger the prize.

The limitations section is honest and worth repeating. DoSA still scans every key, so the screening cost is real; at short contexts, or when the bounds are loose because query geometry is unfavorable, the savings may not cover the screening overhead. The numerical guarantees require conservative evaluation relative to the original scorer, and a valid tied top-K set need not yield identical generated text, meaning lossless in selection does not bit-for-bit mean lossless in output. The measurements establish computational benefit, not improved task accuracy beyond what the model was trained to do. The paper's proof structure backs the certificate up: real-arithmetic validity of the bounds, exact selection for a fixed query over its visible keys, explicit numerical conditions, and a separate analysis of bound slack versus selection margin and head budget, so the conditions under which the certificate is tight, rather than merely safe, are characterized rather than asserted.


Positioning against prior work is worth a sentence each. GVR exploits temporal correlation across decoding steps, DeepSelect streams materialized scores through a bounded buffer, and LiteTopK fuses scoring with candidate filtering to avoid dense score writeback, but all of them still evaluate every head for every key. MISA screens with fewer heads and reranks candidates with the full indexer, but a rerank cannot recover a winner that screening excluded, which is exactly the unsoundness DoSA's certificate is designed to prevent. So the paper's claim is narrower and stronger than the prior art: it is the first method that certifies, per key, that a candidate was never a top-K member, and therefore that skipping it costs nothing.

The skeptical assessment. This is a systems paper with a real math core, and the math core is the part I trust: the certificate is a standard exact-retrieval argument, the seed-threshold construction is the same lower-bound trick used in sparse document retrieval for two decades, and the ReLU-geometric bounds are the genuinely new piece. What I would re-verify before deploying is the end-to-end claim. Time-to-first-token speedups of roughly 2 times on 1-million-token prefills depend on the specific GPU, the specific head counts of the two models tested, and the activation patterns of real production traffic, which may differ from the English, Chinese, and code workloads tested. If you run a model whose indexer heads activate erratically, the majority-support aggregation degrades and the bound goes loose; the head-budget analysis is there to tell you when that happens, which is a good sign. The folded screening weights also mean you must recompute the screening plan whenever the indexer weights change, so this pairs naturally with a fixed-weights serving regime, not with continuous fine-tuning loops.

The bottom line: DoSA is a clean, provably lossless way to turn dense indexer scoring into conditional computation, with a 2.74 times complete-indexer speedup and roughly 2 times time-to-first-token at one-million-token context that I would believe for the tested models. Before adopting it for your own stack, I would profile the bound slack on your real traffic distribution, because the savings live or die on how well the seed activation patterns and residual geometry of your indexer behave in practice.
