The paper is EdgeWise, from Zeru Shi, Jindong Jiang, and Ryo Hachiuma, from NVIDIA and Rutgers University, with Ayush Agarwal of NVIDIA as a co-author. The system is a step-level edge-cloud collaboration framework for long-horizon agents, and the observation that drives it is simple enough to be annoying: if you run a small local model as the primary solver on a long coding or terminal task and only delegate the genuinely hard steps to a frontier cloud model, you keep most of the accuracy at a fraction of the cost, because the cloud model's benefit is concentrated in a small subset of critical steps. The interesting question, and the one the paper actually answers, is how the local model learns to know which steps are critical without a separate router or judge sitting in the loop at inference time.

The setup is a coding agent running a long trajectory, dozens or hundreds of tool steps, the sort of task on SWE-Rebench or Terminal-Bench where you are inspecting a repository, repairing dependencies, and iterating until a hidden verifier passes. In the standard design, a frontier model generates every step, and the serving cost grows with the length of the trajectory. The obvious economy is that most steps are routine: read a file, search for a symbol, run a test, wait for a build. A small local model can do those. The hard steps are the writes and edits that need real reasoning, the diagnosis of a failure, the navigation of an unfamiliar codebase. The prior art here falls into two camps. Routers pick one model per query, so the decision is coarse. Collaborative-agent methods and step-level speculative schemes keep the strong model as the generator and only use the weak model when it can be checked cheaply. EdgeWise inverts the relationship: the small model is the author of every step, and the cloud model is consulted selectively, on demand.

There are two delegation mechanisms, and they answer different questions. The first is external-judge-based delegation, where a judge model looks at the current context and decides whether this step should go to the cloud. The authors use this mechanism mainly as a probe: it lets them systematically study when the cloud model is actually needed, what context should be shared between the two models, and how often delegation should happen. The finding from that study is the paper's most useful artifact. The benefit of the cloud model is not spread evenly across the trajectory. On Terminal-Bench, roughly half the cloud-handled steps are write and edit steps, a quarter are read steps classified as complex, and the rest are search, diagnosis, and validation. The hard steps are a recognizable category, and that means the decision to delegate is learnable from the trajectory itself. The second mechanism is self-delegation, where there is no external judge at inference time. The local model is trained, with step-level reinforcement learning, to jointly optimize two objectives: solving the task, and deciding when to escalate. The credit assignment is fine-grained, so that the reward for a successful task is attributed to the specific delegation decisions that mattered, not smeared across the whole run. That is what makes the whole thing deployable, because the inference-time system is just a local model that occasionally emits an escalation, with no additional service to serve.

The training recipe has a detail that explains a lot about why the RL stage is non-negotiable. The local model is first fine-tuned on trajectories where delegation labels were produced by the external judge, teaching it the format of an escalation and, broadly, when to use the cloud model. But the supervised-only version underperforms noticeably on pass at one, even though it does reduce cloud usage. The authors' reading is that the supervised labels are mostly format supervision. They tell the model what an escalation looks like, but they do not explicitly separate the critical steps from the routine ones in a way the reward signal can use. So the model learns to delegate less, not to delegate well. The reinforcement learning stage is where the model learns the actual decision boundary, because the task outcome, filtered through fine-grained credit assignment, is the only signal that says this particular escalation was the one that saved the run. That is a useful negative result in its own right, because it says that distilling a router's decisions with supervised fine-tuning is not the same as learning to route, and the gap shows up in exactly the metric you would care about.

The results are the reason to take the paper seriously. On SWE-Rebench, using a nine-billion local model with a frontier cloud model, self-delegation actually improves accuracy over always using the cloud model, by about two points on pass at one, while cutting cost by about fifty-two percent. On Terminal-Bench it is closer to a trade: a couple of points lower on pass at one, at a thirty-five percent cost reduction. The average across models and benchmarks is a forty-six percent cost reduction at competitive performance. The numbers that deserve attention are the deltas in the table: the self-delegation rows show accuracy at or near the vanilla cloud baseline while the cost drops by a third or more, and the SWE-Rebench improvement over the cloud-only baseline is the most interesting cell, because it suggests the local model is not merely a cheaper substitute but is sometimes adding value, perhaps because the local model has better context on the specific machine state it is operating in. The authors also deployed the system on a local workstation in a real edge-cloud setting and got comparable savings, which matters because the whole premise is that the local model runs where the data is.

What I would trust is the measurement of where cloud capacity is actually needed. The step-category analysis is the kind of result that generalizes beyond this paper: if you are building any long-horizon agent with a cost constraint, the fact that hard steps concentrate in writes, edits, and diagnosis tells you where to spend your model budget, and it holds whether or not you adopt EdgeWise itself. The two-mechanism design is also sound. Using the external judge as an analysis instrument and then distilling its behavior into the local model via RL is a clean way to get the benefit of a trained router without paying for the router at inference time, and the fine-grained credit assignment for delegation decisions is the right call, because a coarse trajectory-level reward would teach the model to delegate either always or never.

What I would re-verify is the generality and the accuracy deltas. The benchmarks are coding and terminal tasks, where the step structure is fairly regular, and I would want to see this on tasks with more varied step types, like web agents or research agents, where the hard steps may be less predictable. The couple of points lost on Terminal-Bench at pass at one is real, and the cost saving has to be weighed against the actual deployment constraint, because if the cloud model is already rate-limited or the task is latency-bound, the savings matter more than the accuracy, and if the task is accuracy-bound, the trade may not close. The SWE-Rebench accuracy improvement over cloud-only is the headline, but it is a single configuration, and I would want it replicated across more cloud models and more seeds before treating it as a robust effect. The external judge's own cost is included in the reported numbers for the judge-based rows, which is the honest accounting, but it also means the self-delegation advantage is partly an advantage over a baseline that carries that overhead.

Two-sentence verdict: EdgeWise is a practical, well-measured answer to the cost problem of long-horizon agents, with the step-level RL self-delegation as the deployable core and the hard-step concentration analysis as the transferable insight. The cost savings are large and consistent, the accuracy trade is small and configuration-dependent, and the one number I would want replicated before believing it is the SWE-Rebench case where self-delegation beats the cloud-only baseline on accuracy while costing half as much.
