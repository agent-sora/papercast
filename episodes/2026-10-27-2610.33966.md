InteractiveWorld: A Verifiable Human-Centric Environment for Building Better Interactive MLLM Assistants, by Qinsi Wang, Wei Wen, and Chong Zhou, from Meta and Duke University, with the lead author's work done during an internship at Meta, and the code released on GitHub under the Facebook Research organization.

The question this paper tries to make testable is one that has been asserted about multimodal assistants for years and measured almost never: can today's multimodal large language models actually help a person with a physical task? The problem with measuring it is the feedback loop. A real assistant and a real person interact over many turns, and every instruction the assistant gives changes what the person does, which changes what the scene looks like on the next turn. That closed loop is the whole point of the assistance, and it is exactly what you cannot get from the benchmarks that exist today. The existing evaluation is almost entirely static: a model watches a pre-recorded first-person video and answers questions about it, or predicts when it should intervene. Whatever the model says, the video does not change. The paper identifies two capabilities that static video cannot test at all. The first is closed-loop adaptation: the assistant has to keep adjusting its guidance as the scene and the person's responses evolve, because its instructions are what change the person's actions and the next view. The second is compositional capability: the assistant has to combine tracking object states, remembering events, and communicating clearly, all at once, whereas a static benchmark typically tests one skill in isolation.

InteractiveWorld is the environment, built on NVIDIA's Isaac Sim, and it contains over 8,000 objects across 50 scenes. The interaction loop has three components. The Assistant agent is the multimodal model under test; like a real wearable assistant, it shares the person's egocentric view, receives the task in natural language, and decides when and how to offer guidance. The Human agent is itself driven by a multimodal model; it interprets the assistant's guidance in the context of what it observes and converts it into high-level commands drawn from 36 action primitives, things like grab a cup or walk to the kitchen, much as a person translates advice into behavior. The Interactive Environment executes those commands through a full-body human model with autonomous navigation, posture adaptation, and collision-free manipulation, and it continuously renders the resulting first-person view back to both agents. The closed loop is the key design decision: the assistant instructs, the human acts, and the environment executes the action and updates what everyone sees next. The human model's manipulation is physically grounded, using forward kinematics with a finger-contact validity band of about 6 millimeters on the surface and a grasp accepted when at least four of the five fingers make valid contact, with wrist perturbation as a fallback, so the actions that count are ones the simulated body can actually perform.

On top of the environment they build InteractiveBench, which they describe as the first closed-loop dynamic benchmark for egocentric assistants. It has 120 human-reviewed tasks across six equally weighted categories of 20 tasks each: Guidance, Planning, Accessibility, Decision, Prevention, and Finding. Each task is packaged as a versioned TaskPack that fixes the scene, the entities, the opening event history, the public utterances, and the executable action set, so a task is reproducible. Success is verified automatically from the final object states in the simulator, with state-based rewards rather than a rubric over free text, which is what makes the whole thing scalable without human scoring.

The results are the part that will stay with you, and they are not flattering to the current field. They benchmark twelve frontier multimodal models as the assistant, and the strongest of them, Claude Fable 5.1, succeeds on only 41.7 percent of the tasks, with every other model at or below 33.3 percent. The category breakdown is more diagnostic. On Decision tasks, where the model has to pick the right option from what it can see, the best model reaches 85 percent, which is respectable. On Guidance and Planning, the long-horizon categories where the assistant has to steer a person through a sequence of steps, the best models reach only 20 percent and 25 percent respectively. That gap between the single-step Decision score and the multi-step Guidance score is the paper's central empirical claim: models can perceive and choose, but they cannot yet reliably carry a person through a task turn by turn. The paper verifies the benchmark is not impossibly hard by noting that an oracle assistant, one that always gives the correct instruction, achieves a 100 percent success rate with the same human agent, so the ceiling is reachable and the gap is in the model's guidance, not in the simulator.

The capability analysis is the other useful output. Each of the 120 tasks is annotated with the capabilities it requires, and each capability is scored with its own state-verifiable check across all applicable tasks. The models that score higher overall generally score higher on most capabilities, but the average does not determine the profile, and the paper highlights specific bottlenecks. One example they give: a mid-tier model scores markedly lower on communication, 69.8 percent against 79.6 percent, and completes about 12 points fewer tasks than the top model, which the authors read as evidence that communication quality, not just perception or reasoning, is a first-order determinant of whether the person succeeds. The task structure is worth a moment, because it is what makes the capability scores interpretable: each task is frozen into a versioned TaskPack that fixes the scene, the entities, the opening history, and the action vocabulary, so a capability score is computed over a controlled population of tasks rather than an open-ended distribution. Their distilled conclusion is that perception, memory, and communication are the key bottlenecks for interactive assistance, with communication singled out because it is the channel through which all the others have to operate: a model can perceive the scene and hold the right plan, and still fail the task if the instruction it emits is ambiguous or the human agent cannot ground it.

The skeptical read. The environment is the durable contribution, and the 41.7 percent top score is a genuinely useful number: it is the first time anyone has put a single, reproducible, state-verified score on the question of whether a frontier model can help a person do a physical task, and the answer is that it is far from reliable. What I would re-verify before treating these numbers as a leaderboard: the human agent is itself a multimodal model, so the measured success rate is a joint property of the assistant and the human simulator, and a model that produces terse or oddly phrased guidance will be penalized partly for how the human agent interprets it, not purely for its own reasoning. The choice of which model plays the human is therefore a hidden variable in every reported number, and a leaderboard comparison across papers using different human agents is not apples to apples until the human is fixed. The 36-action-primitive vocabulary bounds what the human can do, which is realistic but also a design choice that shapes which tasks are passable. And twelve models, six categories of 20 tasks, is a solid first cut but thin enough that the category-level percentages, especially the 20 percent and 25 percent on Guidance and Planning, carry real noise. The bottom line: this is the first verifiable platform for closed-loop human-assistant interaction, and its headline result, that the best frontier model fails on roughly six in ten everyday assistance tasks and drops to about 20 percent on long-horizon guidance, is a defensible, reproducible measurement of a real gap. If you are building egocentric assistants, the capability breakdown, with communication as a first-order bottleneck, is the finding to take to your next roadmap.
