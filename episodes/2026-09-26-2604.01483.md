Type-Checked Compliance, by Devakh Rashie, an independent researcher based
in Centreville, Virginia, and Veda Rashi, a student at Thomas Jefferson
High School for Science and Technology, which is a detail worth holding
onto, because the combination of the two, an independent adult researcher
and a high school student, is itself a data point about the accessibility
of this kind of formal-verification work, and it also sets the expectations
for how the paper should be read, as a design and a proof-of-concept rather
than a deployed system.

The setting is a specific tension that has not been resolved, and the
paper frames it as an architectural crisis. Large language models are
probabilistic and non-deterministic, they produce outputs that are
plausible and sometimes wrong, and they are being deployed in financial
systems, where the compliance requirements are not probabilistic, they are
absolute, they are the multi-variable regulatory constraints mandated by
bodies like the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Financial Industry
Regulatory Authority, and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency,
and the paper's argument is that the existing guardrail solutions, it names
NVIDIA's NeMo Guardrails and Guardrails AI specifically, are built on
probabilistic classifiers and syntactic validators, and those two tools are
fundamentally inadequate for enforcing a constraint that has to hold for
every possible input, because a probabilistic classifier can be wrong, and
a syntactic validator only checks the shape of the output, not whether it
satisfies the regulatory logic. The paper's proposed fix is to change the
kind of check, from a probabilistic or syntactic check to a formal
verification check, where the check is a proof in a theorem prover, and the
proof either exists or it does not, there is no probability, there is no
plausibility, the Lean four kernel either accepts the proof or it does not.

The system is called the Lean-Agent Protocol, and its central design
principle is a single sentence that captures the whole architecture, every
proposed agentic action is treated as a mathematical conjecture, and
execution is permitted if and only if the Lean four kernel proves that the
action satisfies the pre-compiled regulatory axioms. The pre-compiled
regulatory axioms are the formalization of the compliance rules, and the
paper uses a specific tool to produce that formalization, a neural-symbolic
model called Aristotle, developed by a company called Harmonic AI, which
takes the natural-language institutional policy and auto-formalizes it
into Lean four code. So the pipeline is, the human writes the policy in
natural language, Aristotle formalizes it into Lean four, the Lean four
kernel compiles it into a set of axioms, and then every action the agent
proposes is checked against those axioms by the kernel, and the action only
executes if the kernel produces a proof. The design has a property that is
worth naming, because it is the part that distinguishes this from a
post-hoc audit, the check happens before execution, not after, so the
regulatory constraint is enforced as a precondition of the action rather
than as a log entry that a human reviews later, and that timing is what
makes the guardrail deterministic in the strong sense, the action either
has a proof or it does not happen, there is no window where the action has
already executed and the proof is being checked. The Aristotle component
is the part that does the auto-formalization, and the paper is specific about how
it works, Aristotle uses the Lean four compiler as an infallible, high-
speed reward signal within a reinforcement learning loop, so when the model
produces a formalization that does not compile, the compiler error, which
often includes an explicit counterexample, is fed back to the model, and the
model autonomously repairs the formalization until the kernel accepts it.
That is the neuro-symbolic core of the system, a neural model doing the
drafting and a symbolic kernel doing the checking, with the compiler error
as the training signal. The paper situates the design against the existing
deterministic approach, which is the rule-based world of robotic process
automation and hard-coded workflow engines, systems that execute
predefined logical paths with mathematical precision, and the paper's
point is that those systems are deterministic in exactly the way a
compliance system needs to be, but they are rigid, if a condition is not
explicitly defined, execution halts, which bounds operational risk but
also means the system cannot adapt to a case the rule writer did not
foresee. The Lean-Agent Protocol is positioned as the middle position
between that rigidity and the LLM's flexibility, the agent can reason and
adapt the way a language model can, but every action it proposes has to
clear a formal check before it executes, so the flexibility of the model
is contained inside a deterministic boundary. The paper's framing is that
this is the missing layer, the layer between the probabilistic model and
the deterministic regulator, and the Lean four kernel is what fills it,
because a kernel proof is a deterministic object with a probabilistic
model producing the candidate that has to satisfy it.

Now the skeptical read, and this is where the paper needs to be handled
carefully, because the architecture is sound in the abstract and the
specific claims need to be checked against what the paper actually shows.
The first question is the formalization step, which is the weakest link in
the whole pipeline. The Lean four kernel is infallible, it will never
accept a false proof, but the kernel is only as good as the axioms it is
checking against, and those axioms are produced by Aristotle, a neural
model, and the question is whether Aristotle's auto-formalization is
faithful to the natural-language policy. If the policy says something
subtle, a multi-variable constraint with an implicit exception, and
Aristotle formalizes it slightly wrong, the Lean four kernel will happily
prove or disprove actions against the wrong axioms, and the system will be
deterministically enforcing the wrong rule. The paper's defense is that the
compiler error catches the formalizations that do not type-check, but a
formalization can type-check and still be a misreading of the policy, and
that is a faithfulness gap, not a type error. The second question is the
scope of the deployment. The paper is a design and a proof-of-concept, and
the authors are an independent researcher and a high school student, and
the honest read is that this is not a system that is running in a
production financial environment, it is a paper that argues a system of
this kind should be built and sketches the architecture, and a reader
should check what the paper actually implements and evaluates, whether it
is a full pipeline run on a real policy or a design document with a small
worked example. The third question is the performance cost, because a Lean
four proof check is much slower than a probabilistic classifier, and the
paper should be read with the question of whether the proof-checking
latency is acceptable in the financial workflow it is targeting, because
the whole point of the guardrail is to run in the loop between the agent's
proposal and the execution, and if the proof check takes too long, the
guardrail becomes a bottleneck. And the practical question is the
regulatory acceptance, because the paper's claim is that a formal proof is
what a regulator should accept as a compliance guarantee, and that is a
reasonable argument, but it is also a claim about how regulators work, and
a reader should note that the paper is making a case to regulators as much
as to engineers, and the argument is stronger than the implementation at
this stage.

Bottom line: this is a clean architectural argument, that agentic financial
systems need a deterministic, formally-verified guardrail rather than a
probabilistic one, with a specific pipeline, Aristotle for auto-
formalization and the Lean four kernel for proof checking, that is
well-motivated and correctly identifies the limitation of the existing
probabilistic guardrails. The open question is the faithfulness of the
auto-formalization, because the kernel is only as good as the axioms it
checks, whether the paper implements and evaluates a full pipeline or
sketches the design, the latency cost of proof checking in the agent loop,
and whether the regulatory-acceptance argument holds in practice.
