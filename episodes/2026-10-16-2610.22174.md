The paper is Mosaic: Multi-Session Agentic Reinforcement Learning, from Yilong Xu, Minrui Xu, and Xinyu Zhou, with the corresponding authors Haochen Tan and Zhijiang Guo, from the LARK lab at HKUST Guangzhou, the Foundation Model Lab at Huawei, and the City University of Hong Kong. It is a method for reinforcement-learning LLM agents in a setting the field has been quietly ignoring: trajectories that span multiple sessions. The claim is that the standard single-session training assumption does not match how these agents actually run at inference, and that the obvious fix, broadcasting the trajectory reward to every session, gives a coarse and poorly targeted signal.

The motivation is concrete. Agentic RL trains a language model to do multi-turn reasoning, tool use, and interaction with an environment, and it has worked well. But recent agent stacks add inference-time techniques that break the single-session assumption. Context compaction compresses the execution history to free context space, and in doing so it rewrites the prefix that conditions the next actions, which starts a new session. Subagent forking delegates part of the goal to a subagent, which creates a branch session. So a single rollout can be a tree or a chain of sessions rather than one continuous context, and the trajectory no longer satisfies the assumption that the training objective is written for. That is a real train-inference mismatch, and it is getting more common, not less, as agent harnesses get more sophisticated.

The baseline they critique is broadcasting. Once you have multi-session rollouts, the simplest way to make them trainable under a group-relative objective like GRPO is to take the trajectory-level advantage and replicate it to every session and every token in the trajectory. This preserves equal weighting across rollouts within a prompt group and stabilizes the signal scale, which is why it is the default. But the authors' argument is that it provides only coarse supervision. Every session gets the same credit regardless of whether it contributed, so a trajectory with many unhelpful sessions and one decisive one gives the unhelpful sessions the same reward as the decisive one, and the method gives you no handle on how to allocate sessions within a trajectory.

The concrete failure is easy to construct. Take a browsing task where the agent compacts its context three times to stay within the window, spawns a subagent to verify a candidate answer, and then settles the final answer in the last session. Broadcasting hands all four sessions an identical advantage, so the gradient says the two exploratory compactions and the verification fork were just as valuable as the session that actually resolved the query, when the evidence in the log may say the opposite. The model is thus trained to treat every structural move as equally worth the tokens and latency it cost. That is the gap Mosaic is trying to close: it wants the credit to flow to the sessions that moved the outcome, while still keeping the global outcome signal intact so the policy does not start over- or under-using structure purely to game the shaping term.

Mosaic's fix is a critic-free hierarchical optimization with two levels. At the trajectory level, they make the advantage session-aware. The number of sessions in a trajectory is a proxy for how much exploration and structural complexity it used, but the paper is careful that the raw count should not become a reward in its own right, because the right direction depends on whether the task succeeded. They impose three ordering constraints: for successful trajectories, using more sessions costs more and should get a lower advantage, while fewer sessions should get a higher one; for unsuccessful trajectories, using too few sessions may mean insufficient exploration and should get a lower advantage, while more sessions on a hard failure should get a higher one; and the outcome reward must stay dominant, so that using more sessions alone can never let a failed trajectory outrank a successful one. They implement this as a bounded modulation of the standard group-relative advantage, calibrated by within-group rankings of session counts conditioned on the outcome, which preserves the outcome ordering while widening the relative advantage gaps inside a group.

At the session level, they add advantage shaping. For each session they estimate its marginal contribution to the outcome, treating the marginal outcome gain as an immediate reward, and they normalize that reward group-relatively, but separately by session type, because a compaction session, a subagent session, and a normal session have different distributions of marginal gain and normalizing them together would mix incomparable scales. They deliberately exclude the last session from the shaping signal, because its output state usually contains the final answer, and a correct answer can sharply spike the answer likelihood and confound the marginal-gain estimate. The final per-session advantage is the trajectory-level advantage plus a weighted shaping term, so the first term is the long-term, outcome-oriented global signal and the second is the short-term local signal that differentiates sessions.

The experiments are on BrowseComp, a hard benchmark of one thousand two hundred and sixty six questions that require an agent to persistently navigate the web to find entangled, hard-to-locate information, which is exactly the kind of long-horizon task that produces multi-session trajectories. The base model is Qwen 3.5 four billion, running a ReAct-style agent loop. They compare single-session GRPO and IGPO baselines, the broadcasting multi-session baseline, and Mosaic, evaluated under both context compaction and subagent forking. In the context-compaction setting, Mosaic reaches thirty-eight point eight six percent accuracy with two point three five sessions on average, against broadcasting's thirty-five point three one percent with three point one seven sessions. In the subagent-forking setting, Mosaic reaches thirty-two point four six percent with three point seven seven sessions, against broadcasting's thirty-one point four four percent with four point four three sessions. So it improves accuracy in both settings while using fewer sessions on average, which the authors frame as improving both the utility and the allocation of sessions. The session-count reduction is the more telling number: Mosaic is getting a better answer while doing less structural work, which is the opposite of the failure mode you would expect from a shaping term that simply rewarded more exploration.

What I would trust here is the problem framing, which is correct and increasingly relevant: the single-session assumption is a real mismatch, and the three ordering constraints are a clean way to encode "use enough sessions but no more than necessary" without letting the session count become a reward hacking target. The type-conditioned normalization is a sensible detail, and excluding the final session from shaping is exactly the right call to avoid the answer spike contaminating the marginal-gain estimate. What I would re-verify: the model is a single four-billion-parameter base on a single benchmark, so the generality of the method is not yet demonstrated, and the absolute accuracies are low, in the high twenties to high thirties, which means the relative improvements are being measured on a noisy, hard task. The gains over broadcasting are a few points and the session-count reduction is the more interesting result, but a few points on BrowseComp is within the range where a different random seed or prompt template could matter, so I would want the seed variance before I put weight on the exact margin. And the whole approach is built on a ReAct loop with a specific harness, so transferring it to a different agent architecture means re-deriving what a "session" even is.

Two-sentence verdict: Mosaic identifies a real and growing train-inference gap in agentic RL and offers a principled, critic-free way to give sessions differentiated credit, with the session-count reduction being the more compelling result than the accuracy gain. It is demonstrated on one small model and one hard benchmark, so treat it as a promising direction for multi-session agents rather than a settled state of the art.
