Depth Pro 2, by Peiyun Hu, Marko Mihajlovic, and Lyndon Duong, with the rest of the author team from Apple, is a method for estimating dense metric depth from monocular images and video at full resolution. The paper's opening observation is that prior monocular depth methods live on opposite corners of a trade-off: single-frame methods produce sharp occlusion boundaries but are temporally inconsistent, because each frame is processed independently, while multi-frame video methods are stable over time but smooth over fine detail, because they operate at reduced resolution to afford the temporal context. The paper's claim is that this trade-off is not fundamental, and it presents a single scalable network that processes a flexible number of frames jointly at native resolution, producing depth maps that are metrically accurate, sharp, and temporally consistent at the same time, and it releases a family of models from 24 million to 872 million parameters that it describes as a new Pareto frontier.

The trade-off the paper sets out to dissolve is worth stating concretely, because it is the design constraint that everything else follows from. A single-image method like InfiniDepth delineates fine detail well, the fur on the paper's example meerkat, but places the subject at different distances across frames, which breaks any application that needs a stable depth over time. A video method like pi-3 keeps the subject at a consistent distance across frames, but it operates below the input video's resolution, which blurs the fine detail. The reason the two goals have been in tension is that temporal consistency is usually bought with long temporal context, temporal attention over many frames, video diffusion priors, or video training data, all of which are expensive in memory and runtime, and the standard way to afford them is to drop resolution, which is exactly what erases boundary sharpness. Depth Pro 2's architectural answer is to process a flexible number of frames jointly, which stabilizes depth temporally, while operating at native resolution, which preserves sharp boundaries. The training recipe is deliberately simple relative to the multi-stage curricula of prior work: it trains on clips of one to 32 frames, mixing single images, video, and multi-view datasets in one setup. Training on single images alongside video is what lets the network infer metric scale from an individual frame, and training on video with a temporal consistency loss is what keeps the estimates stable across frames, so both objectives are learned from the same data rather than from separate stages.

The metric-depth mechanism is a direct carry-over from Depth Pro, the prior system this builds on. The network predicts inverse depth normalized by the focal length, and the metric depth is recovered using the input image's focal length. When the focal length is not provided, a separate focal length estimator supplies it, following the Depth Pro approach, but the estimator here is designed to be lightweight so it can run in a streaming pipeline. The paper reports that this focal length estimator runs at 118 frames per second at 1080p on an NVIDIA B200 and is more accurate than prior estimators, which is a useful number for a streaming use case, because the estimator is on the per-frame hot path.

The released family is the part that matters for adoption, because it is sized for different latency budgets. The models range from 24 million to 872 million parameters. The smallest, Depth Pro 2-S, runs at 53 frames per second at 1080p on the B200 in the streaming setting where frames arrive one at a time, which is faster than the 30 frames per second at which most video is recorded, so it can keep up with a real camera stream in real time. The largest, Depth Pro 2-H-plus, runs at 8 frames per second on 1080p video and, according to the paper, outperforms all prior work on metric depth, boundary sharpness, and temporal consistency, on both images and video.

The architecture's flexibility in the number of frames it processes is a design choice worth understanding, because it is what lets one network serve both the image and the video use cases. When you feed it a single frame, it behaves like a single-image depth model and must infer metric scale from that frame alone, which is the capability the single-image training data provides. When you feed it a clip, the joint processing over the frames gives it the temporal context to stabilize depth, which is the capability the video training data with the temporal consistency loss provides. The same weights handle both regimes, and the 1 to 32 frame training range means the network has seen everything from the single-frame extreme to a full clip, so it does not need a separate model or a separate code path for still images versus video. That unification is part of why the Pareto claim is plausible, because the network is not being asked to specialize for one regime and then lose the other. The evaluation is on standard high-resolution video datasets with dense ground truth, Sintel and Spring, for the sharpness-versus-consistency trade-off, and on metric depth benchmarks for accuracy, and the paper positions the released family as defining a new Pareto frontier across metric accuracy, sharpness, temporal consistency, and runtime.

Now the skeptical reading. The strongest claim in the paper is the Pareto frontier, that this family dominates prior work across all four axes at once, and the thing to verify is whether the frontier is being compared against the right set of baselines at the right operating points. A model family that spans 24 million to 872 million parameters can always beat a single fixed-size competitor at some point, because you can always pick the largest model to win on quality and the smallest to win on speed, so the meaningful comparison is whether, at a fixed compute or latency budget, Depth Pro 2 beats the best prior method that fits that same budget. The paper's framing, a family that defines a frontier, is harder to falsify than a single model beating a single model, and a skeptical reader wants the iso-compute comparison, not just the frontier plot.

The second caveat is the native-resolution claim in the streaming setting. Running at 53 frames per second at 1080p on a B200 is a strong throughput number, but the B200 is a datacenter-class accelerator, and the paper is from Apple, so the question of how these numbers translate to Apple silicon, which is presumably the intended deployment target for a consumer or device-facing depth product, is not directly answered. If the 53 frames per second figure is B200-only, the real-time claim for the streaming use case needs to be re-verified on the actual hardware you would deploy on, because the memory bandwidth characteristics of a streaming depth model are very hardware-specific.

For implementation, the transferable pieces are the mixed image-plus-video training recipe, which is simpler than the multi-stage curricula it replaces, the lightweight focal length estimator that runs on the per-frame path, and the model-size ladder, which lets you pick a latency-quality point rather than committing to one model. The training recipe is the most portable idea, because mixing single images and video in one training setup to get both metric scale and temporal consistency is a technique you can apply to your own depth model without adopting the full architecture. The thing to check before adopting is the iso-compute comparison and the target-hardware throughput, since those are the two numbers that determine whether the Pareto claim is real for your deployment rather than just on paper.

To summarize the bottom line. Depth Pro 2 is a clean architectural contribution to monocular metric depth, attacking the sharpness-versus-temporal-consistency trade-off with joint multi-frame processing at native resolution and a simple mixed training recipe, and releasing a parameter-count ladder that spans real-time streaming to highest-quality offline. Its limits are the frontier-style comparison, which is harder to falsify than an iso-compute head-to-head, and the datacenter-class throughput numbers that may not transfer to the Apple silicon a product deployment would actually use, so verify the iso-compute comparison and the target-hardware frame rate before trusting the Pareto claim for your specific deployment.
