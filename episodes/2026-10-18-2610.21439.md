The paper is Each Reward to Its Relevant Tokens, from Skylar Zhai, Haonan Wang, and Zhining Liu, from the University of Minnesota, the National University of Singapore, and the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, with Jian Kang from MBZUAI as the senior author. The system is MRTPO, a policy optimization method for multi-reward reinforcement learning, and the one sentence that captures it is this: when you train a language model against several verifiers at once, each verifier's score should move the tokens that actually decided that score, not every token in the response. That is a small-sounding claim, and it turns out to be the missing piece in a whole line of recent multi-reward methods.

Here is the setup. RLVR, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, is now standard post-training, and GRPO is the workhorse. But real objectives have multiple criteria. A text-to-SQL model must produce a query that is well formed, that actually executes, and that returns the right rows. A tool-calling agent must emit a syntactically valid call with the right function and the right arguments. Each criterion is its own verifier, and each produces its own reward. The naive move is to sum the rewards and run ordinary GRPO, which collapses different reward combinations into the same advantage. GDPO, the fix from last year, standardizes each reward within its rollout group before summing, so that the combination of format-pass plus execution-fail keeps a different advantage from format-fail plus execution-pass. That solves the reward collapse. But look at what GDPO does next, and here is the gap: it hands the summed advantage to every token of the response with equal weight. Every token pays the same penalty for the execution failure. Every token collects the same credit for the format success. And that is wrong in an obvious way, because the format verifier only checks a handful of structural tokens, the tags and the code fence. The execution failure is usually decided by two or three tokens, the wrong column name or the wrong comparison operator. Spreading that penalty over four hundred tokens means it gets diluted, and the tokens that actually caused the error get only a whisper of the lesson.

MRTPO's answer is a piece of counterfactual reasoning that the authors borrow from the self-distillation literature, and it is genuinely clean. For each reward, each verifier holds some piece of information that decides the reward: the reference query, the SQLite error message, the required format template. The model replays its own sampled response under two contexts that differ only in whether that verifier's information block is present. It reads the next-token distribution at every position under both contexts and takes the Jensen-Shannon distance between them, normalized so it lands between zero and one. If removing the reference query changes the model's prediction at the selected column, that token is relevant to the execution reward. If removing the format template changes the prediction at the thinking tag, that token is relevant to the format reward. Then the reward's advantage is redistributed across tokens in proportion to that relevance, with a floor, so that no token is fully starved. The beauty of it is that no new model is needed, no step-level annotations, no extra generation. The replay is forward passes over a response the model already sampled, and it reuses the exact parameters that produced the response, so there is no train-inference skew. And because the weights average to one across the response, the total credit a response receives is unchanged from GDPO. Only the placement changes. The method is also composable, because it acts on a different axis from the dynamic reward-weighting methods like SAW, and the authors demonstrate the combination.

One practical detail is worth naming, because it is the price of the mechanism. Every training step now runs the sampled response through the model several extra times, once per reward, plus one shared replay with all the verifier blocks present. For the three-reward SQL setting that is a handful of additional forward passes per response, no new sampling, no new model in the loop, and the authors report that MRTPO still converges to its final accuracy in fewer steps than GDPO on the same hardware, so the extra compute is more than paid back. There is also a stability story in the details: the allocation strength beta is ramped up from zero over the first ten steps, so training starts as plain GDPO and gradually hands authority to the token-level placement, and when several rewards pile onto the same token, the reallocated part of that token's advantage is capped at the magnitude of the uniform advantage, which prevents one crowded position from dominating the gradient. These are the kind of unglamorous engineering choices that decide whether a clean idea survives contact with real training runs.

The results are on three tasks, text-to-SQL, multi-constraint instruction following, and tool calling, with models from three billion to seven billion parameters. On text-to-SQL, measured by execution accuracy across Spider and BIRD, the three billion model goes from an average of about fifty-four for the base to about sixty-nine with MRTPO, against sixty-seven for GDPO, and the seven billion model goes from about sixty-nine base to seventy-three, against seventy-one and a half for GDPO. The gains are a couple of points, consistent, with low variance across seeds. The more interesting number is convergence: MRTPO reaches GDPO's final accuracy in fewer steps, which matters because these training runs are expensive and the step budget is the real constraint. On instruction following, the pattern holds, and the largest gains are on IFBench, where the constraints were not seen during training, which is the honest transfer test. The one caveat the authors themselves state is that the method only works for rewards whose verifying information can be written down as text, a reference answer, an error message, a rubric constraint. A reward like response length has no such block, and those rewards keep the old uniform allocation.

What I would trust is the mechanism. The counterfactual relevance signal is doing exactly what it claims. The Jensen-Shannon distance between predictions with and without the verifier's information is a direct, model-internal measure of which tokens that reward depends on, and it has no hyperparameters beyond the single allocation strength beta, which they fix at half. The leave-one-out context construction is the right choice, because comparing against a bare prompt would confound the verifier's information with the mere presence of extra text. And the composable design is the kind of contribution that actually accumulates, because any of the dynamic reweighting methods in the literature can be stacked on top.

What I would re-verify is the size of the wins and the transfer. Two to three points on execution accuracy, with the baselines already at seventy or above, is a real but modest gain, and I would want to see it on harder distributions where the baselines are weaker, because that is where credit assignment actually matters. The ablation question is whether the gains come from the token-level placement or from the extra forward passes, which are an implicit additional regularization, and the paper's OPSD comparison, which also does extra forward passes and does worse, helps but does not fully close it. The tool calling results should be checked against the full Berkeley leaderboard rather than the in-paper subset, and the whole framework's ceiling is set by that one requirement, that the verifier's deciding information be expressible as a text block, which rules out a large class of real-world rewards.

Two-sentence verdict: MRTPO is a clean, well-motivated fix for a real gap in multi-reward RL, giving each verifier's advantage to the tokens its own counterfactual replay identifies as responsible, and the consistent gains with faster convergence across three tasks and five models are exactly what the theory predicts. The modest absolute margins and the text-block requirement on verifiers are the honest limits, but as a component that composes with the existing reweighting stack, it is the kind of incremental method that tends to end up in production pipelines.
