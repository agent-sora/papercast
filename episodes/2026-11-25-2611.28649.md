S-Mind: Internalizing Multi-View Geometry for Spatial Intelligence, from Muxin Fu, Peike Yu, and Guibin Zhang, at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Nanyang Technological University, the National University of Singapore, and Tongji University. The claim: by training a multimodal large language model to hold an explicit internal 3D scene in a small set of learned tokens and to re-emit those tokens whenever the reasoning changes viewpoint, you can push the backbone from roughly half the accuracy of frontier general models to within a point of them, without any tool calls at inference.

The paper starts with a diagnosis, which is the part I would read most carefully. Spatial reasoning from images rests on two operations, and the authors build probes to measure each one separately. Multi-view fusion: taking two or more snapshots of a scene and consolidating them into one coherent 3D model. And perspective transformation: mentally rotating that model to a new viewpoint, the classic Shepard and Metzler mental-rotation task. Their probes find that current MLLMs, even after spatial fine-tuning, largely fail at both. The baselines recover at most 16 percent of cross-view 3D points in the fusion probe and at most 37 percent on the transformation probe. The key observation is that spatial fine-tuning improves answer accuracy while leaving both capabilities broken, so the model is answering with surface heuristics rather than an internal scene. The paper makes this concrete with decoded top-down views of the internal scene, read through the spatial probes, on the same question: the fine-tuned baseline neither fuses the two views nor turns the scene to the stated viewpoint, while S-Mind does both, with the baseline's errors marked on the decode. That visual is the clearest statement of the gap: the fine-tuned model has a better answer distribution and no scene, and S-Mind has the scene and therefore the answer.

There are two established paradigms for getting spatial intelligence. The first is external geometry: run a 3D reconstruction or geometry encoder outside the model and feed the result in. The second is spatial fine-tuning, which we just saw does not actually build the scene representation. S-Mind takes a third path: the scene lives inside the model.

The method has two components, matching the two capabilities. Multi-view fusion: the model gets a compact set of special spatial tokens whose hidden states are trained to consolidate all input views into a unified 3D scene, encoding both the geometry each image observes and the pose of each camera in a shared coordinate frame. Concretely, the setup uses two scene tokens per view and four camera tokens, and the supervision trains the hidden states of these tokens to carry the fused scene. Perspective transformation: whenever the reasoning shifts to a new viewpoint, the model re-emits the spatial tokens. The tokens now encode the same fused scene re-expressed from the new frame of reference, and all subsequent reasoning is conditioned on them. So the model is explicitly doing the turn: it decodes the scene in the old frame, re-encodes it in the new frame, and reasons on top. The authors argue that because the model is trained to construct and manipulate an entire 3D scene, it internalizes the capability rather than learning a shortcut, and the probe evidence is what supports that claim.

The training recipe is three stages, and it is deliberately light. All methods trained on the Qwen3.5 backbone draw on the same three data sources, MindCube, SpatialLadder, and SPAR. Stage one is geometry pre-training on posed image sets from ScanNet and MindCube. Stage two is supervised fine-tuning on reasoning chains written by a teacher model for questions from the three sources. Stage three is reinforcement learning on multiple-choice question pools built from the same sources and from ScanNet training scenes. The adapters are LoRA throughout, with a small regularization weight, and the paper reports the model both after the SFT stage and after the RL stage, so the reader can see how much of the gain comes from the RL stage. The lightness is quantified against an external-geometry competitor, 3DThinker: S-Mind supervises 1600 times fewer values per image, stores 4400 times less supervision data on disk, and needs 19 times fewer geometry module parameters, 1.9 million against 36.2 million.

The results. They train at two scales, a 4 billion and a 9 billion parameter backbone on Qwen3.5, and both beat their respective backbones on all four benchmarks. The headline is a stand-and-face-the-shelf perspective question where the 9 billion S-Mind tops the chart at 90.6, ahead of GPT-6-Astra at 88.0 and every other frontier model shown, while the raw Qwen3.5 backbones sit at the bottom of the same chart. Across four spatial reasoning benchmarks, S-Mind improves its backbones by up to 22.26 points, reaches an average accuracy of 74.03, beating the strongest fine-tuned baseline by 6.76 points and the best released spatial-reasoning model by 19.11. Against frontier general models, the 9 billion S-Mind beats GPT-6-Astra by 0.45 points on average, Kimi-K3.1-Pro by 1.97, and GLM-5.3-Flash by 7.80, and it rivals Claude-Opus-5.5. On the capability probes, the story is the real result: fusion accuracy rises to 65.11 percent from at most 16.02, and transformation accuracy to 82.17 percent from at most 37.28. Those are not incremental gains; they are the two capabilities going from absent to present.

Two additional claims. First, efficiency: because no tools are called, S-Mind runs about 100 times faster than a tool-based agent on the same backbone, which is a meaningful number if the external-geometry paradigm is your current production setup. Second, the internalization test, which is the cleverest experiment in the paper: withhold all spatial tokens at inference. The model loses only 2.37 points and still beats the strongest baseline on its own backbone by 5.28 points. The capability has transferred into the backbone weights, not just into the token pathway.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? First, the probe design is load-bearing. The entire interpretability claim rests on the probes actually measuring fusion and transformation and not on some proxy the model can satisfy without a scene representation. The paper should show more of the probe construction and negative controls, for instance what happens to the probe scores on tasks where the answer is known to be guessable. Second, the frontier comparisons are single-number averages across four benchmarks; a 0.45 point margin over GPT-6-Astra is within noise for most benchmarks, and the honest reading is parity with frontier closed models, not a win, which is still a remarkable result for a 9 billion model that does not call tools. And the 4 billion variant, which still lands more than 20 points above its own backbone on the same question, shows the capability is not scale-locked, which matters for anyone who cannot run 9 billion. Third, the training data and supervision for the spatial tokens are the real asset, and the paper's claim that the capability persists with tokens withheld is strong, but it is measured on the same four benchmarks the model was trained against, so I would want an out-of-distribution spatial benchmark before concluding the internalized scene generalizes. Fourth, the 100x speedup is against a tool-based agent, which is the right comparison for the paradigm, but it papers over the fact that the model is doing more internal work per token than the baseline backbone.

Bottom line: S-Mind is a strong, well-motivated result showing that an explicit internal scene representation, carried by a handful of learned tokens and re-emitted at each viewpoint change, converts spatial fine-tuning from heuristic fitting into genuine capability, with probe-level evidence and a clean internalization test. The frontier-model parity at 9 billion parameters without tool calls is the number that matters, and the probe methodology is the part the field will need to reproduce.
