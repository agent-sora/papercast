PlayWorld: Benchmarking World Models with Agent Players over Long-Horizon Objectives, by Kaixin Ding, Xi Chen, Minghong Cai, and colleagues at The University of Hong Kong, the Kling Team at Kuaishou Technology, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, and Zhejiang University. The paper attacks a real methodological gap in how interactive video world models get evaluated. Fixed action trajectories, the standard protocol, are a poor cross-model instrument because action granularity differs per model: the same three camera commands may complete a full 360-degree rotation in one system and only a partial turn in another, so whether a landmark leaves and re-enters view is an artifact of the controller, not a property of the world model. A human player instead pursues long-horizon objectives, turning fully around to check environmental consistency or walking into water to see if ripples form. PlayWorld operationalizes that judgment with an automated agent, producing what is currently the most complete like-for-like comparison of nine state-of-the-art world models.

The mechanism has three parts. First, an Agent Player: a replaceable multimodal model, Claude Haiku in the headline runs, wrapped in an interface that translates abstract controls into each world model's native input format over continuous W, A, S, D, arrow-key, and WAIT commands, including browser automation for web-only closed models like Genie 3. Each case provides a human-annotated basic action sequence as shared initial reference, and at every step the agent observes generated frames plus executed-action history and issues one of five decisions: keep the planned action, stop it early once the intended visual state is reached, extend its hold duration, correct or skip the next action when observed state diverges from reference, or end the case when the objective's completion condition is verifiably met. One rule shows careful design: in interaction-fidelity cases involving an obstacle, reaching the obstacle does not trigger end, because the rollout must continue forward to reveal whether the subject collides realistically or passes through. A forty-step interaction budget bounds each rollout, which lasts roughly ten to sixty seconds. Second, the benchmark itself: 171 human-annotated scenarios built from Pexels and Google Images starting frames across natural, urban, fantasy, aquatic, retail, kitchen, home, street, countryside, and specialized settings, with subjects spanning humans at 57 cases, environments at 49, objects at 32, vehicles, animals, and robots; Gemini 3.1 Pro drafts environment captions that humans verify and revise, annotators define objectives with visually verifiable completion conditions and write basic action sequences spanning fifty-plus action patterns from single actions up to five-action combinations, and sample-specific VQA rubrics are constructed per case, over 820 questions organized into four evidence families, identity and appearance at 317 questions, physics and dynamics at 236, temporal evolution at 203, and spatial and trajectory reasoning at 72, applied across more than 1,400 generated videos from nine models. Third, the VQA rubric verifier: Gemini 3.1 Pro answers weighted yes-no rubric questions aggregated to case scores on a one-to-five scale, gated by dimension-specific trajectory validation so instruction-following failures do not masquerade as memory failures; rollouts failing validation score the floor value of one on that dimension.

Four capability dimensions structure the evaluation. Geometry consistency covers identity preservation and spatial layout through orbit and revisit objectives, checking whether lettering stays legible, whether a wall mural keeps its color and shape after a full turn, and whether a red car that started to the right of a green car still sits on the correct side when the camera crosses the street and looks back. Interaction fidelity covers collisions, walking into water, fire, cliffs, and grass, asking whether contact produces plausible responses rather than pass-through, with validation confirming the controlled subject actually responds to actions and reaches the interaction region; a completely static subject fails validation because the intended interaction was never tested. Out-of-sight evolution tests whether a target keeps its identity and keeps changing while unobserved, for instance whether an apple being peeled shows more removed peel after leaving and re-entering the frame, or whether an in-progress drawing gains content while out of view. Insight evolution observes stationary scenes for sixty seconds, checking whether ongoing processes progress causally instead of freezing, resetting, or looping, such as whether a vendor stops filling a container that is already full. Alongside these sit seven video-quality metrics borrowed from VBench, Omni-WorldBench, MemoBench, and HyDRA, covering aesthetics, imaging, motion smoothness, flickering, temporal consistency, depth stability, and subject consistency, plus translation and rotation pass rates computed by VGGT camera-pose estimation against trajectories defined by executed actions including online adjustments.

The leaderboard is unflattering across the board. Genie 3 leads overall at 2.12, with geometry consistency 2.74, interaction fidelity 2.40, but out-of-sight evolution just 1.81 and insight evolution 1.51. Alibaba's HappyOyster follows at 1.92. The open-source field runs lower: LingBot-World 1.78, LingBot-World2 1.82 notable for leading insight evolution at 1.95 while collapsing to 1.16 on out-of-sight evolution, HY-World2 1.61, SANA-WM 1.48, Hunyuan-GameCraft 1.42, HY-WorldPlay 1.21, Matrix-Game-3.0 last at 1.14 with floor scores of one on two dimensions. On a one-to-five scale where five means flawless, the best system averages 2.12, which is the actual finding: no current model reliably sustains long-horizon interactive objectives. Trajectory-validation pass rates expose a control-versus-capability divergence: SANA-WM posts a solid 80.4 percent overall pass rate yet mediocre rubric scores, meaning it reaches target regions without preserving memory, geometry, or physical plausibility there. Basic-ability metrics tell the same cautionary tale inverted; HappyOyster wins the basic ability score at 76.4 percent without leading any rubric dimension, confirming that aesthetic quality and coarse controllability do not predict world modeling skill. Failure modes worth internalizing: during a 360-degree orbit of the Taj Mahal, models regenerate the monument at inconsistent locations, violating global scene topology while every frame looks plausible locally; first-person movement passes through solid obstacles that third-person characters correctly stop at; water entry often produces no ripples or depth change; and sustained processes freeze or repeat motions under continuous observation.

The ablations validate the agent design sensibly. Against executing the preset sequence alone or letting the agent plan everything online, the hybrid preset-plus-agent strategy wins on both Genie 3 and HappyOyster, reaching trajectory scores of 1.08 and 1.12 against baselines as low as 0.68, while modifying only 10 to 20 percent of actions, so adaptation is targeted rather than chaotic, and human preference of 65.6 and 67.4 percent against far lower baselines. Full online planning also runs slow because every action requires reasoning over the latest observation, which is why the shared reference exists at all. Agent-model choice barely matters: Claude Sonnet scores higher trajectory quality at 1.24 versus 1.08 for Haiku and Gemini 3.1 Pro, but Haiku delivers equal preference at 3.83 seconds decision latency versus Sonnet's 6.21, so they ship Haiku, and the interface deliberately treats the agent as replaceable as stronger models arrive. Human validation supports the metric: six hundred pairwise judgments show consistently positive Spearman correlation between rubric scores and human preference rankings per dimension, with a linear fit and confidence band reported for each.

Skeptical read. The verifier is Gemini judging Gemini-adjacent artifacts, single-judge VQA with no reported inter-rater reliability per question family, and rubric weighting choices that are not deeply audited; the human study is 600 judgments, thin relative to 171 cases times nine models. Validation gating sets failed-dimension scores to the floor of one, which conflates cannot-follow-trajectory with terrible-world-simulation and mechanically punishes weaker controllers twice, once in validation and again in score. Closed models were evaluated through web interfaces whose backend versions can drift, making the leaderboard a snapshot. And the agent budget of forty steps caps effective horizon below what long-context claims need. Before adopting this protocol, run the verifier twice with different judge models on a subset and report agreement. Bottom line: PlayWorld converts the subjective way players actually judge world models into a reproducible benchmark, and its central number, best-in-class 2.12 out of 5, is the most honest summary yet of where interactive world simulation stands. Treat rankings as indicative, treat the four-dimension decomposition as the durable contribution.
