Learning to Repair Lean Proofs from Compiler Feedback, by Yiran Wang,
Simon Chess, Daniel Lee, Siyuan Ge, Ajit Mallavarapu, and Vasily Ilin,
from a group that the paper lists under the Math AI Lab at the University
of Washington, with the senior authors at the Department of Computer
Science and Engineering at the same university, which is a detail worth
holding onto, because the paper is a systems paper about a specific
failure mode of neural theorem provers, and it comes from a group that
builds the provers rather than a group that only benchmarks them.

The setting is a gap in the training data that the field has been
working around, and the paper names it precisely. As neural theorem
provers become increasingly agentic, the ability to interpret and act on
compiler feedback is critical, because an agentic prover is not a model
that emits a proof in one pass, it is a loop, the model writes a proof,
the compiler says what is wrong, the model tries again, and the quality
of that loop is a function of how well the model reads the compiler's
diagnostic. The problem is that the existing Lean datasets, the corpora
the provers are trained on, consist almost exclusively of correct proofs,
which means the model has seen a great deal of right, it has seen very
little wrong, and the wrong it has seen is not paired with the compiler
message that explains why it is wrong, so the model has no supervision for
the exact skill the agentic loop needs, which is reading a compiler
diagnostic and acting on it. The paper's contribution is to study Lean
proof repair as a supervised learning problem, and the formulation is
specific, given an erroneous proof and the compiler feedback, predict both
a corrected proof and a natural-language diagnosis grounded in the same
feedback, and the two targets, the correction and the explanation, are the
part that matters, because a model that can repair the proof but cannot
say why it was broken is a model that has memorized the fix, and a model
that can say why it was broken has learned the structure of the failure.

The dataset the paper introduces is called APRIL, for Automated Proof
Repair in Lean, and it is two hundred sixty thousand supervised tuples,
and the structure of a tuple is the contribution. Each tuple pairs a
systematically generated proof failure with the compiler diagnostic and
with aligned repair and explanation targets, and the systematically
generated property is the part that distinguishes APRIL from a dataset of
real errors, because real errors are rare and noisy and hard to collect
at scale, and a systematically generated failure is a failure that the
authors construct by taking a correct proof and breaking it in a specific
way, and the paper is specific about the way it is broken, and the
methods are the part a reader should hold onto. The failure is produced by
corrupting the proof in controlled ways, swapping related theorems,
swapping similar tactics, and eliciting plausible but incorrect line and
multi-line completions from a language model, and the last of those is the
part that makes the failures realistic, because a corrupted proof that is
obviously broken is not a realistic failure, and a plausible but incorrect
completion is the kind of failure an agentic prover actually encounters,
and the aligned targets mean that each failure is paired with both the
corrected proof and the natural-language diagnosis that explains the
compiler message, so the model is trained to do both at once. The result
the paper reports is specific, and it is an ablation rather than an end-
to-end claim, which is the honest framing. Training a language model on
APRIL substantially improves repair accuracy and feedback-conditioned
reasoning, and in the single-shot repair evaluation, which is the
evaluation where the model gets one shot at the repair with no search and
no iteration, the fine-tuned model reaches twenty-seven point four percent
correction accuracy, compared to one point one percent for the base model
and twenty-six point eight percent for Goedel-Prover-V2 thirty-two B under
the same protocol, and the paper is careful about the interpretation, it
says that because Goedel is typically deployed with search and iteration,
this comparison should be read as an ablation of compiler-feedback-
conditioned repair rather than as an end-to-end proving performance, and
that is the right call, because a single-shot repair is a measure of the
repair skill in isolation, and an end-to-end prover uses the repair skill
inside a search loop, and the paper does not conflate the two.

Now the skeptical read. The first question is the systematicity, because
the failures are systematically generated, and the honest version of the
question is whether a model that has learned to repair systematically
generated failures can repair the failures it will actually see, because
the systematic failures are constructed by the authors, and the question
is whether the distribution of constructed failures matches the
distribution of real failures, and the plausible-but-incorrect-completion
component is the part that addresses this, because it is generated by a
language model, so it is in the same distribution as the model's own
mistakes, but a reader should check the breakdown of the dataset, how
many tuples are tactic-swap failures versus theorem-swap failures versus
language-model-completion failures, because the repair skill is a function
of the failure distribution, and a dataset that is mostly one kind of
failure is teaching one kind of repair. The second question is the single-
shot number, because twenty-seven point four percent is a real improvement
over one point one percent, but it is a small number in absolute terms,
and the honest version of the question is what the ceiling is, because the
paper frames the result as an ablation, and the question is whether the
repair skill, once it is there, actually composes with a search loop to
give an end-to-end improvement, and the paper does not report that, and a
reader should check whether the authors have plugged the repaired model
into the Goedel prover and measured the end-to-end pass rate, because the
value of the repair skill is a function of how it behaves inside the
agentic loop, not in isolation. The third question is the diagnosis
target, because the natural-language diagnosis is the part that is novel,
and the honest version of the question is whether the diagnosis is used,
because the paper trains the model to produce it, and the question is
whether producing the diagnosis improves the repair, or whether the
diagnosis is a byproduct, and a reader should check whether there is an
ablation that removes the diagnosis target and measures the repair
accuracy, because if the repair accuracy is the same with or without the
diagnosis target, the diagnosis is a nice output, not a mechanism, and if
the repair accuracy is better with the diagnosis target, the diagnosis is
a mechanism, and that is the difference between a tool that explains and
a tool that reasons. And the practical question is the scale, because two
hundred sixty thousand tuples is a real dataset, and the question for a
practitioner is whether the generation procedure is released, because the
value of the dataset is a function of whether a reader can generate more
of it, and a reader who wants to use this should check that the failure-
generation pipeline is open, because a dataset that is a snapshot is less
useful than a pipeline that can produce more.

Bottom line: this is a well-motivated and specific contribution, a
two-hundred-sixty-thousand-tuple dataset, APRIL, that pairs systematically
generated Lean proof failures with compiler diagnostics and aligned repair
and explanation targets, and the result is a fine-tuned model that reaches
twenty-seven point four percent single-shot repair accuracy against one
point one percent for the base model, framed honestly as an ablation of
compiler-feedback-conditioned repair rather than an end-to-end proving
claim. The open question is whether the constructed failure distribution
matches the real one, whether the repair skill composes with a search loop
to give an end-to-end improvement, whether the natural-language diagnosis
target is a mechanism or a byproduct, and whether the failure-generation
pipeline is released.
