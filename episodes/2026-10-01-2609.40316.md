Scaling Laws for Looped Mixture of Experts. The three authors are Yanbei Chen, Anirudh Goyal, and Raghuraman Krishnamoorthi, all at Meta AI, with Chen corresponding and Goyal and Krishnamoorthi as senior authors.

This is a scaling-law paper, and it sits squarely in the architecture-topology lane. The two axes it combines are recurrence and sparsity. Looped transformers reuse the same weights across multiple passes to increase computational depth at fixed stored parameters. Mixture-of-Experts sparsity expands total capacity at fixed active compute. Existing scaling laws model one axis or the other in isolation: looped-transformer laws model recurrence, often with linear or power-law forms that grow without bound, and MoE laws model sparsity through expert count but omit recurrence. Concurrent looped-MoE studies fix recurrence at two passes in their primary analyses, leaving the interaction unmodeled. This paper introduces the first scaling law to jointly model recurrence and sparsity alongside model size and data, and it is the kind of architectural result that is easy to miss on a shortlist, which is why it is being covered despite a small upvote count.

The core object is a bounded, sparsity-conditional recurrence mapping. The key empirical observation is that recurrence shows diminishing returns: loss drops sharply over the first few recurrent passes and then approaches a plateau. A second observation is that greater sparsity, varied through expert count, improves and sustains the gain from recurrence. The law captures both: the effective-parameter gain from looping is bounded rather than unbounded, and the bound is raised as sparsity increases. The formulation recovers the standard dense scaling law and the standard MoE scaling law as special cases, which is the right sanity check for a unified law.

The training setup for fitting the law is a small-model sweep: active sizes of 0.3, 0.6, and 1.0 billion, training tokens from 100 to 500 billion in steps of 100, expert counts of 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16, and varying recurrence. The fitted law is then used to predict held-out loss, and it beats prior alternatives. The held-out prediction errors are reported per axis, with an RMSE of 0.0656 when holding out model size and 0.0824 when holding out data, which are the numbers you would check against any reproduction.
The shape of the recurrence curve deserves one more sentence, because it is what makes the whole law useful. The effective-parameter multiplier over recurrence starts high and saturates: each early pass buys a large chunk of effective capacity, and each later pass buys progressively less, converging toward an asymptote that depends on the expert count. That is why the law is bounded where the earlier linear and power-law loop models were not, and why those older models systematically overstate the value of deep loops at fixed parameters. The sparsity-conditional piece says the asymptote itself is a function of expert count: more experts means a higher ceiling for recurrence, because the sparse routing gives each recurrent pass more distinct computation to exploit rather than re-deriving the same dense transformation. The practical consequence is that the optimal recurrence count is not a constant you tune once; it is a function of how sparse your model is, and the fitted law gives you that function directly.

Beyond prediction, the fitted law is used as a design tool, which is where the paper earns its practical keep. Given a compute budget and a memory budget, the law tells you the trade-off between expert count, recurrence, and active model size. The examples in the paper are concrete. Under a tight memory constraint of 3 gigabytes, the law selects a 1.0 billion active model with 8 experts and 2 recurrent passes. Under a looser 10 gigabyte constraint, it selects a 1.6 billion active model with 16 experts and a single pass. The pattern is that larger memory favors greater sparsity and less recurrence, because you can afford to store more experts and do not need to buy depth through looping. That is a directly usable result for anyone planning a looped-MoE deployment with a fixed serving budget.

The headline efficiency claims, measured downstream: sparsity delivers about 3 times active-parameter efficiency, recurrence yields about 2 times total-parameter efficiency on reasoning tasks, and jointly scaling both axes advances the performance frontier further than either alone. The mechanism behind the 3 times number: at a fixed compute of 5 times 10 to the 20 FLOPs, a 0.3 billion active MoE model with 8 or more experts outperforms a 3 times larger 1.0 billion dense model on both overall and reasoning. The 2 times number comes from recurrence at fixed expert count: at 10 to the 21 FLOPs, the 0.3 billion model with 4 or more recurrent passes matches or exceeds the 0.6 billion model with a single pass, and the 0.6 billion model with 3 or more passes matches the 1.0 billion single-pass model.

The scale-up experiment is the one that matters most, because scaling laws are only as good as their extrapolation. At matched training compute, a looped MoE with 0.3 billion active and 1.3 billion total parameters, with a law-derived recurrence of 4 or 5 passes, matches a non-looped MoE with 0.6 billion active and 2.9 billion total parameters on the reasoning benchmarks, while using about half the active and total parameters. The training is at trillion-token scale, roughly 3 trillion tokens for the looped model and roughly 6 trillion for the larger non-looped model. The trade is inference compute for parameter efficiency: raising the recurrence at serving time from a low to a high setting boosts overall performance by 10.3 points, from 36.6 to 46.9, which is the test-time scaling that looping gives you for free at the parameter level.
What I would trust here. The core scientific claim is the bounded, sparsity-conditional recurrence mapping, and the two supporting observations, diminishing returns in recurrence and the sparsity-raised bound, are the kind of clean empirical regularities that scaling laws need. The special-case recovery of both the dense and the MoE laws is a strong internal consistency check, and the per-axis held-out RMSEs give you a handle for judging whether the fit is actually better than the alternatives it claims to beat. The design-tool application, choosing expert count and recurrence under a memory budget, is the most immediately useful output in the paper, and the 3 gigabyte versus 10 gigabyte examples are the sort of decision a systems team would actually make.

What I would re-verify before adopting this. The law is fit on a sweep up to 1.0 billion active parameters and 500 billion tokens, and the trillion-token extrapolation, while reported, is a single comparison at a single matched-compute point, the 0.3B looped versus the 0.6B non-looped model, so the extrapolation claim rests on one data point at scale rather than a family of them. The reasoning benchmarks driving the recurrence efficiency number are the ones where looping has shown its gains in prior work, and I would want to see the overall-versus-reasoning split on a broader task suite before trusting the 2 times total-parameter efficiency outside reasoning. The memory-budget design examples use 3 and 10 gigabytes, which are edge-scale budgets, and the behavior of the law at much larger serving memories is not characterized. The FLOPs matching across the looped and non-looped models is done at training time, and the serving-time inference compute cost of 4 or 5 recurrent passes is real, which means the parameter efficiency is bought with a proportional increase in inference compute, a trade the paper acknowledges but that changes the answer if your constraint is latency rather than parameters. And as with all scaling-law papers, the fit quality depends on the range of the sweep, so the predicted losses outside the trained grid are extrapolations that should be checked against a fresh small run before you commit a training budget.

Bottom line. This is the first scaling law that treats recurrence and sparsity as joint axes, and the bounded recurrence mapping with its sparsity-raised asymptote is a genuinely useful architectural result, with the design-tool application making it directly actionable. The evidence at the small scale is thorough, and the trillion-token confirmation is a single matched-compute point that you should treat as encouraging rather than conclusive.
