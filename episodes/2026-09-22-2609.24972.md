RRSI: Regularized Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses. The first three authors are Peng Xia, Rujun Han, and Zifeng Wang, from Google Cloud AI Research, with co-authors at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Stanford University, and Washington University in St. Louis. The lead author did the work as a student researcher at Google Cloud AI Research.

The premise is that an LLM agent is a system, not a model. A frozen backbone sits inside a harness: the system prompt, the control flow that decides when to plan, act, reflect, or stop, the tool interfaces, the memory and skill files, and the context management that decides what the policy sees at each step. A lot of recent agent progress has come from editing that harness by hand, and a wave of recent methods automates the loop: run the current harness on a set of evolution tasks, summarize the failures into feedback, have a proposer model draft candidate harnesses, evaluate them, and keep the best one. That is, in practice, recursive self-improvement at the agent-system level. The paper's claim is that this loop has a real failure mode that nobody had cleanly isolated: the evolution overfits to the split it is scored on. The evolve-set score climbs while the gain on unseen benchmarks shrinks or disappears.

Their evidence for the problem comes first. They take four published harness-evolution methods and re-run them with the evolution split separated from the evaluation split. On an agentic workspace benchmark, the evolve-set gains do not transfer at all: prior methods retain little of their evolve-set gain, and several end up below the initial harness on the held-out set. In their Figure 1, the scatter of evolve-split gain versus out-of-distribution gain for the agentic workspace is essentially a flat line near zero, while RRSI sits above it.

The fix, RRSI, borrows the regularization intuition from machine learning and applies it to the search itself, on both sides of the loop. On the proposal side, the proposer operates under a temporally annealed edit budget: early rounds may bundle several edits into one candidate, and the budget cools down by cosine schedule toward a single edit in the final rounds. That has a direct effect: with bundled edits you cannot tell which change produced the measured gain, and as the budget anneals toward one, the evidence becomes attributable. The proposer also keeps an edit history: every atomic edit is tagged with its harness component, a hypothesis, and the measured gain and cost of the round. Components that have been exercised but produced no positive measured gain in a recent window form a pruning set, and the proposer is instructed to delete that machinery. They are explicit that the analogies to L-zero and Lasso are functional, not mathematical: it is a cardinality constraint on discrete harness structure, not a penalty on a parameter vector. There is also a stall signal: if progress over the last few rounds stays inside an empirically calibrated noise band, the proposer gets reserved slots to explore components it has never touched.

On the selection side, every candidate faces a stability floor: it must score at least the running best minus the noise tolerance, so a chain of small regressions can never accumulate. If the measured gain exceeds the noise band, a cost rule applies: extra policy tokens are allowed only in proportion to the measured improvement. If the gain is inside the noise band, a shaped admissibility rule takes over, crediting lower cost and novelty of never-before-accepted structural components, but not small score wobbles. The noise tolerance itself is calibrated by repeatedly evaluating the unchanged base harness, per domain: in the coding instance it corresponds to about three passes out of 178 trials. A final domain guard, in the engineering-design instance, rejects candidates that trade pass rate for a drop in valid-output rate or a rise in no-submission rate.

The evaluation spans eight benchmarks in three domains: coding, an agentic workspace bundle, and engineering design. In each domain the harness is evolved on one suite and then run unchanged on held-out suites. RRSI gains up to 14.1 points on the split it evolves against and up to 4.7 points on the five out-of-distribution benchmarks, while the final harness runs on 30 percent fewer policy tokens than the unregularized evolution spends. Concrete held-out numbers: on SWE-bench Verified the initial harness scores 81, the average of the four prior evolution methods 82.3, and RRSI 83.8. On the mean of JobBench, GDPval, and APEX-Agents, the initial harness gets 39.7, the prior-method average 39.4, and RRSI 43.6. On Frontier-Eng engineering design, 17.7 initial, 17.9 for the prior average, 22.0 for RRSI. In their scatter plot of evolve-split gain versus out-of-distribution gain, RRSI is the only method that clearly beats the one-to-one transfer line.

The honest reading. This is a methods paper about a search procedure, and the core result is modest in absolute terms: the headline out-of-distribution gains are a few points, and the in-distribution gain of 14.1 points is on the split the method was allowed to optimize against. What is actually valuable is the diagnostic: adaptive optimization over an expressive search space, scored on a finite noisy split, will overfit, and the standard harness-evolution recipes do not guard against it. The annealed edit budget is the piece I would steal first, because attributability of credit is the binding constraint in any iterative prompt-and-scaffold optimization. What I would re-verify before adopting: the held-out benchmarks are the authors' own selection; the hyperparameters, including the cost-allowance slopes, are tuned on the evolve environment only, which the paper states, but the noise band calibration is a per-domain fudge factor that determines how much of a gain gets treated as signal; and the comparisons against four prior methods are on their own re-implementations of those loops, not the published systems. The released code and round-by-round records are on their site, which helps.

Bottom line: RRSI is a credible, well-instrumented answer to a real failure mode in automated harness evolution, and the regularization framing is the right lens for anyone running iterative self-improvement loops. Expect single-digit out-of-distribution gains, not a transformation, and treat the 30 percent token reduction as the most portable result in the paper.

Worth understanding precisely, because it changes how you read the numbers: the search space is the harness, and the weights never move. The editable component vocabulary in their implementation has nine entries: prompt, control flow, config, output plumbing, context management, client tool, skill, memory, and subagent. Every candidate is a diff over these components. That is why the paper can talk about "sparsity": a candidate touching three components is a dense update, and the annealed budget is a direct cardinality cap on it. The history record per edit stores the round, the component, the hypothesis, the diff, the measured delta in score, the measured delta in cost, and whether the candidate that carried it actually won its round. Losing candidates still get recorded, which is what makes the pruning set meaningful: a component that was tried, measured, and repeatedly lost is a component the next proposal should stop touching. The case studies in the appendix make the selection behavior concrete. In coding round zero, two candidates add nearly identical pre-completion verification audits; the one with a 3.9-point measured gain is accepted, the one with 1.7 points and a 26 percent increase in inference cost is rejected by the cost rule. In round eight, a candidate that reduces cost is still rejected because its performance falls below the admissible floor. In engineering design, a small reusable control-flow correction is retained with little resource growth. The pattern is conservative: the loop prefers small, attributable, cheap changes, and it would rather keep the incumbent than accept noise.

The efficiency result deserves its own paragraph, because it follows from the selection rule rather than from any dedicated optimization. The gain-dependent cost condition means that a candidate can only spend extra tokens if the spend is paired with proportionally more measured gain, and the within-band rule credits cost reduction directly. The net effect across an evolution run is a harness that does less work per task: 30 percent fewer policy tokens than unregularized evolution, at higher held-out accuracy. For anyone running agent loops in production, that is the kind of result that pays for itself, and it suggests that most of the token bloat in evolved harnesses is accumulated noise that no selection pressure ever removed.
