Today's paper is FoMo: Forking Moment in Generative Trajectory as a Perceptual Distance, from Jaihyun Lew, Mingi Jung, and Minjun Park, at the Interdisciplinary Program in AI and the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at Seoul National University. The method generates training labels for image-quality metrics without any human annotation, by asking a diffusion model at which timestep two images stop looking like the same picture.

The setting is reference-based image quality assessment, or IQA. You have a reference image and a distorted or restored version, and you want a number that measures how far the distorted one is from the reference in the way a human would perceive the difference. This matters concretely for super-resolution, denoising, and compression, where you train a restoration model and need a reliable metric that ranks restored images the way a person would. The trouble is the ground truth. The classic approach is the mean opinion score, where many people rate each image on an absolute scale. It gives you a globally ordered label, which is great for rank-correlation training, but it is expensive and noisy. The authors cite KADID-10K, one of the largest human-annotated IQA datasets, which needed 30 crowdsourced ratings per image from over 2,200 subjects to produce 10,125 distorted images, all derived from just 81 reference images. The newer alternative is two-alternative forced choice labeling, where an annotator picks which of two images is closer to the reference. It is cheaper and more reliable, but it only gives you a relative comparison within a pair, so your loss is stuck doing triplet-wise comparisons, and you never get an absolute distance value you can compare across different image pairs.

The observation the paper builds on is how diffusion models generate an image. A diffusion model denoises over a fixed number of steps, and the structure of the image emerges in a particular order: the coarse, global layout is settled in the early timesteps, and the fine texture and detail are filled in during the later timesteps. Two images that share the same coarse structure but differ in fine detail will, if you trace them back through a generative process, diverge late. Two images that differ in their overall layout will diverge early. The authors call the point at which two images first stop being the same the forking moment. If you can find that moment automatically, you have a perceptual distance: the earlier the fork, the farther apart the images are. That is the whole idea, and it is genuinely clever because it turns a generative model's internal dynamics into a measurement instrument, and it needs no humans.

Before using the forking moment as a label, the paper validates it with two human studies, which is the right thing to do and most papers in this space skip it. In the first, for each reference image they synthesize five variants by injecting Gaussian noise at five different timesteps and denoising to completion, so later injection means a smaller perturbation and a closer variant. Thirty participants rank the five variants from most to least similar to the reference, and the resulting ordering agrees with the forking-timestep ordering with a Spearman rank correlation of 0.970 across 1,982 responses on 190 ImageNet validation images, with an inter-rater correlation of 0.960. In the second study they test global consistency, because a useful distance should order pairs regardless of which reference anchors the pair. In a strict forced-choice format, 28 participants judge which of two reference-variant pairs is more internally similar, and the label agrees with individual human choices on 90.2 percent of responses, with a Fleiss kappa of 0.82, and with the majority-vote consensus on 92.8 percent of items. The agreement climbs monotonically with the timestep gap: 64.3 percent for gaps of one to ten timesteps, up to 99.8 percent for gaps of 41 to 50. So the proxy holds when the differences are large and gets fuzzier right around the boundary, which is exactly the behavior you would predict.

The dataset construction is then straightforward. They use FLUX.1-dev as the diffusion model, with up to 50 sampling steps. For each reference image, they uniformly sample a forking step, build the corresponding noisy latent by interpolating the reference with Gaussian noise at the noise level for that step, and denoise the remaining steps to get a perturbed variant. The pair, plus the forking step as the distance label, is a training example. They generate 480,000 pairs total: 240,000 with real ImageNet references and 240,000 with synthetic FLUX-generated references, mixed to cover both domains. The training objective is where the pointwise label really pays off. Because each pair carries an independent distance value, the loss can be a RankNet-style binary cross-entropy over all B by B pairwise comparisons in a batch, not just triplets sharing a reference. That is a strictly more information-rich objective than the forced-choice losses the human-annotated baselines are forced to use, and it is the mechanical reason the same backbones get better numbers: the loss sees many more ordered pairs per batch. One practical detail worth noting: the forking step is sampled uniformly across the trajectory before generation, so the dataset's label distribution is flat by construction, which keeps the metric from learning to over-weight the easy, large-gap pairs.

The results are reported as Spearman rank correlation on four reference-based IQA benchmarks, PIPAL, TID2013, CSIQ, and LIVE, across both CNN and Transformer backbones. On PIPAL, the hardest of the four, FoMo-trained metrics average 0.683 across backbones, against 0.469 for a metric trained on the best human-annotated dataset with a forced-choice objective, and 0.319 for one trained on NIGHTS. The gap is even wider on the Transformer backbones, where human-annotated training gives correlations in the low 20s to 30s and FoMo pushes them into the mid 60s. On TID2013, FoMo averages 0.719 against 0.700 for the strongest human baseline. On CSIQ and LIVE, both benchmarks with milder distortions, the FoMo metrics lead as well, reaching 0.874 on CSIQ and holding at the top on LIVE. Everything was trained for about 240,000 iterations on a single RTX A40, with frozen feature extractors and a prediction head trained from scratch, so the comparison is on training data and objective, not on compute.

The skeptical pass. First, the entire pipeline inherits the perceptual priors of FLUX.1-dev. The forking moment is a property of how that particular diffusion model organizes generation, and the human studies validate it for FLUX-generated perturbations around ImageNet references. Whether the label stays calibrated for distortion types outside the diffusion model's notion of similarity, like sensor noise patterns or compression artifacts, is not tested. Second, the metric is trained and evaluated on standard IQA benchmarks, several of which share image sources with the training references in spirit if not in letter, so I would want to see the numbers on a fully held-out domain before trusting the generalization. Third, the pointwise RankNet objective is genuinely better than triplet losses, but that benefit is partly confounded with the data benefit: you are changing both the labels and the loss at once, and the ablations would need to separate them. This is the kind of result that makes the label-source angle look like the real win, not the backbone. My bottom line: this is a well-validated, genuinely novel label source that beats human-annotated baselines by a wide margin on the standard benchmarks, and the two human studies give it more grounding than most IQA work deserves, with the code and the generation pipeline released for reuse. But the whole instrument is built on one diffusion model's dynamics, and until the label is stress-tested on out-of-domain distortion types, treat the correlation numbers as benchmark wins, not as a solved measurement.
