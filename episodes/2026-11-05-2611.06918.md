From Pixels to Actions, Towards Native Vision-Language-Action Policies, is from Weichen Fan, Haiwen Diao, and Penghao Wu, working at the S-Lab at Nanyang Technological University, SenseTime Research, and Ropedia.

Every vision-language-action model you have probably looked at rests on the same quiet assumption: perception is compressed first, and reasoning about actions begins only afterwards. An image goes through a pretrained visual encoder, CLIP or SigLIP or DINOv2, the encoder emits a fixed feature vector, and only then does the language backbone and the action model get to work. The question this paper asks is whether that fixed visual endpoint is actually the right interface. The authors argue it is not, and they build a model to show why.

Their case is that manipulation hinges on exactly the visual signals that abstraction erases. Local geometry, object boundaries, depth, subtle state changes, the things a gripper actually needs, are the parts most vulnerable to being flattened away into a single encoder output. If the policy only ever sees the endpoint, it is reasoning from a lossy summary. Their proposal is called NEO-VLA, a structurally encoder-free vision-language-action framework. Instead of a separate pretrained encoder, images are tokenized by lightweight convolutional patch layers and then evolve jointly with the language tokens all the way through the backbone. A flow-matching action expert is allowed to draw from the instruction-conditioned visual representations at different depths, not just the last one. The key architectural claim is that action prediction should have direct access to the evolving visual computation inside the backbone, not a snapshot of it.

This is a real structural change, not a prompt trick. There is no separately pretrained vision encoder, no image codebook, no autoencoder. The conv patch embedder maps raw pixels into tokens, and those tokens live in the same sequence as the words and get transformed by the same layers the whole time. The flow-matching expert then reads from multiple depths. The authors ground the motivation in a few prior observations: that visual processing is critical to vision-language-action performance, that the visual components are disproportionately sensitive to pruning, and that action fine-tuning can degrade the pretrained visual representations. Their argument is that by not freezing a separate encoder, you avoid some of that degradation and keep the visual hierarchy available.

The paper also makes two claims that strengthen the controlled comparison beyond a single leaderboard row. First, it is an architecture claim, not a data claim: the native-versus-encoder comparison is drawn from models trained in the same framework, so the difference is the visual interface rather than a larger training set. Second, the abstract states the result directly, that full fine-tuning of the encoder-based counterpart reduces the gap but does not erase it, and that additional scale alone cannot recover it. Those two negative results are what convert a benchmark win into an architecture argument, because they rule out the two most obvious escape hatches, more fine-tuning and more parameters.

The central empirical claim, and the one I would focus on, is the controlled comparison. They show that native architectures outperform encoder-based counterparts when you hold everything else fixed. Two results make that convincing. First, full fine-tuning of the encoder-based version reduces the gap but does not erase it, which means the deficit is architectural, not just an under-trained encoder. Second, additional scale alone cannot recover it, which rules out the most obvious objection, that a bigger or better encoder would close the gap. If more parameters and more data on the encoder-based side still lose to the native side, the interface itself is the problem.

The second piece of evidence is the layer-wise probing, and this is the interpretability result that I think matters most for the audience. They probe the representations at each layer and find that the action-critical signals often peak well before the final visual representation. That is a direct, mechanistic statement: the information the action head needs is present in the middle of the network and then fades. If you only expose the last layer, you are reading from the layer where that signal is already degraded. That single observation is the real justification for letting the action expert sample across depths, and it is the part of the paper I would want to reproduce first, because it is the claim that predicts the architecture works.

Now the benchmarks, and here I want to be careful because the paper is honest about what the numbers mean. On LIBERO, the standard manipulation benchmark, NEO-VLA scores around 98.4, and the comparison models MMaDA-VLA is at 98.0, while Qwen-RobotManip reports a single aggregate of 99.1 without a per-task breakdown. So on the standard, in-distribution benchmark, the top of the table is a cluster, and the native model is competitive but not a clear outlier. That is a fair result and the paper does not oversell it.

Where the native model pulls ahead is distribution shift, which is the whole point of the architecture. On LIBERO-plus, evaluated by perturbation axis, NEO-VLA achieves an overall score of 89.7, the highest among the models in the table. On RoboTwin 2.0, which randomizes the environment to test robustness, the 2 billion parameter NEO-VLA gets 75.78 on the clean split and 69.40 on the randomized split, and that randomized number is the highest in the table, ahead of Qwen-RobotManip at 68.12 and pi-0.5 at 46.00. The pattern is consistent with the claim: on easy, in-distribution tasks everyone is close, but under perturbation and randomized environments the model that keeps the visual hierarchy accessible holds up better. Note also the size asymmetry: NEO-VLA is a 2 billion parameter model competing with 4 and 8 billion parameter baselines on the shift benchmarks, which makes the robustness result more interesting, because the native architecture is doing the work of a larger encoder-compressed model, not just a slightly better one.

There is also a real-world manipulation section with physical tasks, placing a tennis ball on a can, placing a tape roll, that kind of thing, and the model performs there, which matters because simulation-only results for manipulation are always discounted a little. The real-world numbers are context-setting rather than a controlled experiment, and the paper itself frames them that way, but they are useful as a sanity check that the encoder-free design does not only live in simulation, and they give you something to compare against when you are deciding whether to bring the architecture to your own hardware.

What would I trust and what would I re-verify? I trust the controlled comparison, the full-fine-tuning and scale ablations, and especially the layer-wise probing, because those three together make a coherent mechanistic argument rather than a single lucky benchmark. What I would re-verify is the magnitude of the in-distribution result, because LIBERO is a near-saturated cluster and a 98 versus 99 is not a differentiator, and the real claim lives in the shift benchmarks, which I would want to see with the exact perturbation axes and random seeds specified so I could reproduce the 89.7 and the 69.40. I would also want to check the compute cost: keeping patch tokens evolving through the whole backbone and reading from multiple depths is more compute than a single encoder call, and the paper should make the wall-clock and memory cost explicit before I would drop it into a real robot stack. The engineering gotcha is that encoder-free means the visual front-end is now trained from scratch as part of the policy, so your data requirements for the visual tokens may be higher than when you were borrowing a pretrained CLIP.

Two-sentence verdict. NEO-VLA makes a clean, mechanistically grounded case that encoder-free, multi-depth vision-language-action policies are more robust to distribution shift than the encoder-compress-first design, with the layer-wise probing as the key evidence. I would trust the shift and probing results, re-run the distribution-shift benchmarks with full seeds before believing the margins, and check the added compute before putting it on a real robot.
