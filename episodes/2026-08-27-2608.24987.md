D-cubed MOPD, full title Dynamic Data-wise Mixture Scheduling for Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation, comes from the AllSpark Team. The problem it attacks will be familiar to anyone who has trained a model against multiple objectives: when you distill from several specialist teachers at once — one for mathematics, one for code, one for instruction following, one for tool use — you must decide how to split your training batch among them. Standard practice fixes that mixture once at the start, in proportion to dataset sizes or gut feel, and holds it constant for the entire run. The paper's central observation is that domains converge at wildly different speeds, so any fixed mixture is wrong for almost the whole run: by mid-training some domains have already plateaued and are soaking up compute producing no progress, while others still need everything they can get.

First, the base recipe their scheduler plugs into, because it is elegant enough on its own. On-policy distillation has the student generate its own rollouts; each domain teacher then prefills those same rollouts, scoring what the student actually produced rather than what a reference would have said; the student minimizes per-token reverse KL divergence to the teacher along that trajectory. Run four domain-specialist teachers this way simultaneously and one unified student model inherits all four skills without ever serving the specialists at inference time. The failure mode the authors documented first is differential convergence: in single-domain control runs, the code teacher's reverse-KL plateaus by step forty-eight of training, mathematics follows around step ninety-six, while instruction following keeps descending past step one-forty-four with absolute KL sitting an order of magnitude higher than everyone else's throughout. A fixed mixture therefore spends most of its late-training budget re-confirming lessons already learned. The inverse failure is just as real: domains still descending get rationed to a trickle because the mixture was frozen back when they looked proportionally less important — which is exactly how instruction-following capability quietly under-develops in multi-domain runs that seemed healthy on their aggregate curves.

What makes the scheduling signal well-posed deserves scrutiny, because curriculum papers usually hand-wave the measurement question. Reverse-KL to a teacher is a per-token, per-domain quantity already computed inside the distillation loss; logging its running mean per domain costs essentially nothing beyond an accumulator. That gives every domain a progress reading in identical units even though its benchmarks differ wildly in scale and format. The subtlety is that raw KL level alone conflates difficulty with progress — instruction following simply lives at higher absolute KL, as the control runs showed, so comparing levels across domains misleads. Their composite avoids the trap by multiplying level-based remaining gap against trend-based velocity, then normalizing across domains before softmax, so allocation responds to both how far a domain has to go and whether it is actually going anywhere right now.

Their fix is refreshingly humble in engineering terms: zero new machinery inside the trainer loop. An off-process watcher reads a shared log of per-domain KL statistics every ten steps; a stratified data-source module assembles each batch to hit a target mixture computed from those statistics; the training loop itself never changes, coordinating through a status file so nothing ever blocks waiting on anything else. Anyone who has run long training jobs will recognize the deployment philosophy — make the scheduler a sidecar, not a surgical modification. The scheduling signal itself is composite. A remaining-gap term measures how far each domain's current smoothed KL sits above its seeded starting point. A descent-velocity term measures whether that gap is still shrinking at all — the maximum of zero and the mean relative change over three non-overlapping recent windows. Multiply the two, normalize across domains, map through a softmax with temperature, and add two stabilizers: a per-domain floor so no domain ever starves completely — protection against catastrophic forgetting — and small batch-level jitter, uniform noise within thirty percent, which ablations later reveal acts as exploration pressure, occasionally betting batches on struggling domains before the smooth signal catches up.

The experimental setup is credibly modest: student is Qwen 3.6, thirty-five billion total parameters as a mixture-of-experts with three billion active; four teachers share the exact same backbone, each reinforcement-tuned on its own domain — math from DAPO-style sets, code, instruction following, tool use — roughly four thousand prompts apiece. No exotic teacher zoo, so improvements cannot be attributed to bigger teachers.

Results close the efficiency argument cleanly. Measured as fraction of the achievable student-to-teacher gap closed, averaged across benchmarks, the dynamic schedule reaches ninety-seven percent against sixty-three percent for the fixed mixture — the difference between a distillation that mostly works and one that nearly saturates its own ceiling. Peak average score edges up from sixty-one point four to sixty-two point three, but arrival time is where the speed lives: the dynamic run reaches vanilla's best score by step forty-seven, where vanilla itself needed one hundred forty-three — triple-speed convergence. Even more striking, every single per-benchmark peak the fixed mixture ever achieved gets matched or exceeded within the first seventy-nine steps, meaning you could stop the dynamic run in a third of the wall-clock and lose nothing. Reverse-KL trajectory also validates itself as a monitor: the order in which benchmarks peak mirrors the order their KLs flattened, which justifies using exactly these signals for scheduling in the first place. And honesty where it counts: the student surpasses its own specialist teacher on three of seven evaluations — instruction following and both code-judge suites — but the authors flag themselves that those teachers started with gaps too small to beat robustly, normalize scores above one partway reflect small-denominator artifacts, and distillation closes gaps toward teachers rather than inventing capabilities beyond them, a constraint they credit to prior work explicitly.

Ablations decompose the contribution responsibly. Removing velocity costs little on final average but slows adaptation; removing the gap signal instead yields the single best instruction-following number — because pure velocity keeps feeding budget to still-descending domains — while flattening elsewhere. Removing jitter entirely costs seven tenths of a point on average and over two points on code benchmarks specifically, confirming jitter's role as exploration bursts rather than decorative noise. Shrink the velocity window count from three to one and the run starts faster but lands lower — short windows react quickly but mislead early. Every component pulls its weight somewhere; none is ornamental.

For deployment audiences, the operational details are the most transferable part, so they deserve enumeration. The watcher never touches trainer memory — it tails a log file, which means it can crash, restart, or run on another machine without disturbing training; the stratified sampler consumes whatever mixture snapshot is freshest and composes batches with exact integer counts per domain using a largest-remainder method, so there is no silent fractional-sample drift. Warmup sequencing protects against cold-start nonsense: uniform mixing until enough windows exist to estimate velocity at all, gap-only signal early while seeded KL baselines are still stabilizing, full composite signal only once measurements are trustworthy. The appendix even includes a theoretical comfort blanket: under an exponential-decay model of KL reduction, the composite score approximates normalized marginal gap reduction, which recovers greedy allocation as the principled default rather than a heuristic someone liked. None of this requires touching loss code — the entire scheduler is implementable in a day by anyone already logging per-domain metrics.

Skeptical assessment. Everything here trains against same-backbone specialists — the scheme's behavior when teachers differ architecturally, or when a domain has no clean self-measurable KL, remains untested. Four domains is enough to demonstrate scheduling but thin for the general claim; real curricula involve dozens of data sources where floors and softmax temperatures interact less predictably. The ninety-seven versus sixty-three percent gap-closure headline aggregates across only seven benchmarks, several clustered within math and code families. And reverse-KL as a universal progress meter presumes distillation targets — applied to RL-from-verifiers settings where no teacher density exists, the whole signal needs reinvention. Still, the transferable idea survives every caveat: your training loss is already computing a per-domain curriculum signal every step — log it, watch it, allocate against it — and the entire apparatus runs as a sidecar process rather than a rewrite. For practitioners running multi-objective post-training this month, that combination of demonstrated gains and near-zero integration cost is rare enough to act on.
