OmniVBench: A Benchmark and Large-Scale Dataset for Omni Reference-to-Video Generation. The first three authors are Wenxue Li, Peiyan Guan, and Haoyang Jiang, from Tencent's Online Video BU and The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology in Guangzhou, with the remaining co-authors at the same two institutions.

Reference-to-video generation, where a visual reference steers what a video model synthesizes, has moved from simple cases, one object reference for identity, toward compositional settings: the reference might supply content, motion, style, structure, or narrative, and multiple references might have to be combined. The paper calls this emerging paradigm omni reference-to-video, and argues that existing benchmarks do not measure it. They cover a limited range of reference types, they mostly score holistic consistency, whether the reference is "in" the video, and they do not check whether individual reference factors were preserved, disentangled from each other, and routed to the correct target. The paper contributes two artifacts. OmniVBench, a benchmark with 7 task families and 18 fine-grained tasks, 813 evaluation cases, and a factor-grounded evaluation protocol with 12,172 case-specific checklist items. And the Omni-R2V Dataset, 340 thousand processed training samples drawn primarily from a large corpus of professional video footage.

The benchmark's organization is the core contribution. The task space is split into five dimensions, content, motion, style, structure, and narrative, and then extended to multi-reference settings: multi-content, where several content references must be composed, and cross-aspect, where a reference contributes one aspect like motion while the instruction contributes another. The reference types span object, character, scene, action, camera motion, greybox layouts, line art, rough storyboards, and multi-panel storyboards. The evaluation decomposes each case into a case-specific checklist that scores three things: Reference Fidelity, whether the intended reference factors were faithfully preserved, Instruction Realization, whether those factors were correctly disentangled and bound to their targets and realized per the instruction, and Visual Quality. That decomposition is what separates factor-grounded evaluation from holistic scoring, because a model can copy reference content into the video and still apply it to the wrong target, which holistic consistency rewards. The checklist items are authored per case rather than shared across a task family, which is part of why the 12,172-item total is large relative to the 813 case count: each case carries its own factor set, its own binding targets, and its own quality criteria.

The dataset side has 340 thousand samples with task-specific pipelines for constructing reference-target pairs: 18,615 content-reference pairs, 57,400 motion, 8,842 style, 38,837 structure, 167,881 narrative, 19,497 multi-content, and 28,498 cross-aspect. The processed pairs are provided directly, rather than raw footage that users have to process themselves, which the authors note is a practical difference from some existing R2V resources that ship only the underlying clips.

The authors evaluate eleven models, five open-source, UniVideo, OmniWeaving, LoomVideo, Bernini, and MiniMax H3, and six closed-source, including Kling 3.0 Omni, Vidu-Q2-Pro, Gemini Omni, Happy Horse 1.0, Seedance 2.0, and Seedance 2.5. On overall score, Seedance 2.5 leads at 72.68, followed within 0.3 points by MiniMax H3 at 72.41, with the strongest closed-source models in the same band, while the weaker open-source entries sit between 48.88 and 56.95. A 0.3-point gap at the top of a benchmark with 813 cases and case-specific checklists should be read as statistical noise, not as a ranking. The central finding is that no model performs strongly across all task families: content-reference scores are high across the board, while motion, style, structure, narrative, and especially the multi-reference settings show large variation. MiniMax H3, which uses an official reference-preprocessing module, is the only open-source model in the top tier and scores 98.21 on instruction-realization disentanglement and routing for content tasks, near the closed-source leaders.

The fine-grained results carry the paper's argument. Instruction Realization is decomposed into disentanglement and routing, and target compliance. Several models achieve relatively high target compliance while their disentanglement and routing scores are substantially lower, particularly on multi-content and cross-aspect tasks. The authors read this as a distinct capability gap: a model can follow the target instruction while still copying irrelevant reference content or applying the referenced factor to the wrong target. Reference Fidelity itself decomposes into content, structure, motion, style, and narrative sub-scores, and strong content fidelity does not transfer to comparable motion or narrative fidelity, which is the empirical justification for factor-level rather than holistic scoring.

The evaluation protocol is validated against humans: 100 cases covering all task families and sub-tasks, 965 model outputs, three annotators rating each output on a five-point scale. The automatic evaluation correlates strongly with human judgments, with Pearson correlations of 0.81 for Reference Fidelity, 0.78 for Instruction Realization, and 0.86 for Visual Quality. An ablation shows the factor-grounded checklist correlates better with human judgments than holistic evaluation, 0.77 versus 0.67 Spearman on Reference Fidelity and 0.74 versus 0.69 on Instruction Realization. One practical detail: because the checklists are case-specific, re-running the benchmark as models improve does not require re-authoring the evaluation, but it does mean the benchmark's judgment about which factors matter in each case is fixed at construction time, and models that learn to game the specific checklist items rather than the underlying capability would not be caught by this protocol.

The skeptical read. This is a benchmark paper, so the question is not whether the authors' model wins but whether the measurement is worth adopting. On that question, the paper does its homework: the factor-grounded checklist is validated against human ratings with correlations in the high 70s to low 80s, and the ablation directly shows the checklist beats holistic scoring on human agreement, which is the specific claim the paper needs. The disentanglement-and-routing versus target-compliance split is the most useful diagnostic in the R2V evaluation literature I have seen, because it names a failure mode, applying a referenced factor to the wrong target, that holistic consistency scores cannot distinguish from success. And the fact that the top two scores are within noise of each other, with an open-source model in the top tier at all, is itself an interesting data point: the omni R2V field is close enough that measurement granularity, not just model quality, is now the differentiator.

The caveats are about scale and independence. First, 813 evaluation cases is modest for a benchmark that claims to define a paradigm, and the human validation covers 100 cases and 965 outputs, which is enough to establish correlation but not to pin down per-task-family reliability. Second, the factor-grounded checklists are case-specific and were constructed by the authors, so the benchmark encodes their judgment about which factors matter per case, and a model optimized against this benchmark is partly optimized against that judgment. Third, the model slate is the authors' selection of current systems, and the closed-source set includes models whose exact configurations are not fully disclosed, so cross-vendor comparisons should be read as indicative rather than definitive. Fourth, the dataset side, 340 thousand processed samples, is a training resource rather than a scientific contribution, and its value is practical: if you are training an omni R2V model and cannot afford to build your own reference-target pipelines, this is a real shortcut, but the task-specific pipelines are described at a level that suggests rebuilding them exactly would still take effort.

Bottom line: OmniVBench is the most granular measurement of reference-to-video capability available right now, and its disentanglement-and-routing metric is worth copying into any R2V evaluation you run, even if you never touch the dataset. The honest summary of the field it measures is that current models are strong on content references and meaningfully weaker on motion, narrative, and multi-reference routing, with the open-source and closed-source gap narrowed to a few points at the top, which should make any future R2V leader claim on this benchmark worth taking seriously.
