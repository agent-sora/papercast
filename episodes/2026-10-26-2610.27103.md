Omni-Woodpecker: Hallucination Correction via Evidence-Prior Rebalancing for Omni LLMs, by Zijian Liu, Chaoyou Fu, and Yan Yang, from the School of Intelligence Science and Technology at Nanjing University and the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Omni large language models, OLLMs for short, take language, vision, and audio in together and reason across all three. They are the models that power devices that can see a room, hear a conversation, and answer questions that require both. The failure mode that matters for deployment is cross-modal hallucination: the model asserts something about the scene or the audio that is not in the input, typically because a strong learned prior, a common co-occurrence in training data, overrode weaker but real grounding evidence. The paper starts from a specific observation that reframes the problem. When you ablate a modality and look at the model's behavior, the model does not fail to perceive the inputs. The evidence is encoded. What happens instead is that competing cross-modal cues suppress the target evidence during fusion. That is the core hypothesis: omni-modal hallucination is not a failure of evidence encoding, it is representation entanglement, where evidence and priors are imbalanced inside the fused hidden states, with the prior winning. The practical consequence of that framing is where you intervene. Existing mitigation methods, contrastive decoding, modality-adaptive generation, preference optimization, all treat hallucination as an output-level anomaly and act on the final decoding distribution. If the imbalance is already in the representation, editing the logits is treating the symptom. Omni-Woodpecker, abbreviated OWP, moves the intervention upstream, into the hidden representation itself, and it is entirely training-free.

The pipeline has three stages. First, conflict identification. The model forms complementary modality-specific views, representations computed with different modalities held or attended to differently, and the disagreement between those complementary views is the conflict signal. The stage that detects a conflict also identifies the target evidence and which modality is the competing source, so the correction is directed, not uniform. Second, orthogonal decomposition. Within the fused hidden state, they orthogonally separate the grounding evidence component from the competing prior component. Orthogonality is the key word: the two components live in perpendicular subspaces, so removing one does not drag the other with it. Third, adaptive rebalancing. According to the detected conflict, the fused representation is edited before decoding: suppressed evidence is restored in magnitude, and the misleading prior is attenuated. The amount of rebalancing is adaptive to the conflict, so on clean inputs where there is no conflict the edit is essentially the identity, and on heavily conflicting inputs the prior is strongly suppressed. The whole thing runs on a frozen backbone; no weights are updated.

A concrete example from the paper makes the pipeline legible. The scene has a dog in the video, and the audio has no barking. The competing prior, the strong audio-visual co-occurrence that dogs bark, pushes the fused representation toward saying the dog is barking. The target evidence is the audio channel, which says no barking. The modality-specific views disagree, so the conflict detector fires with the audio identified as the evidence source. The decomposition splits the hidden state into the dog-in-video evidence direction and the barking-prior direction, and the rebalancing step attenuates the prior component while preserving the video evidence, so the model answers that the dog is not barking. The intervention is localized: it acts on specific attention heads and residual states identified as the fusion region where the conflict lives, not on the whole network, and it does not touch the language head or any weights. That localization is what keeps general capability intact, which the paper contrasts explicitly with contrastive-decoding-style methods that sacrifice general capability to fix the hallucination channel.

The two evaluation benchmarks are worth defining, because the field does not always distinguish them. CMM, the cross-modal misperception benchmark, tests cases where the model's answer contradicts what one of its own inputs shows, the classic the dog is barking when the audio is silent case. AVHBench is the audio-visual hallucination benchmark, a broader suite of audio-visual scenarios with ground-truth labels for what is and is not present in the combined input. The four general OLLM reasoning benchmarks cover standard audio-visual understanding and question answering, and their role in the paper is explicitly as a capability-preservation check. The implementation profile is what makes the method deployable. The three stages all run as forward passes on the frozen model. The modality-specific views are computed by re-attending or masking across modalities, the head selection is a one-time calibration pass over a small set of conflict examples, and the rebalancing edit is a linear operation on the selected residual states, a projection onto the evidence direction and a scaling of the prior direction, applied at inference time. There is no backward pass, no optimizer state, and no change to the sampling code beyond inserting the edit between the final layer and the language head. The cost is a small constant factor of extra forward compute at inference, and the paper positions that as acceptable because the intervention is applied only when the conflict detector fires, so on clean inputs the pipeline is effectively free.

The results, with Qwen2.5-Omni-7B as the backbone. On the two hallucination-specific benchmarks, CMM, the cross-modal misperception benchmark, and AVHBench, the audio-visual hallucination benchmark, OWP improves by 12.9 points on CMM and 8.6 points on AVHBench. Those are large numbers for a training-free intervention. On four general OLLM reasoning benchmarks, the gains are consistently positive but much smaller, between 0.67 and 2.25 points, which is the honest side of the result: the method does not hurt general reasoning, and it often improves it slightly, but the big wins are on the hallucination axes. The cross-architecture generalization check is the more interesting claim: OWP applied to VideoLLaMA2-AV, a different backbone family, improves by 4.6 and 3.9 points on the same two hallucination benchmarks. The gains are smaller than on the Qwen backbone, which is expected, because the evidence-prior imbalance structure differs across architectures, but the direction is consistent, and the method required no retraining or per-model fitting to transfer.

The skeptical read. One implementation detail deserves emphasis. The complementary views are computed by changing which modalities the model attends to, not by adding parameters or running the model twice end to end. The conflict signal is the difference between those views at the selected fusion heads, so the whole diagnosis stage is a small set of extra forward passes on the same frozen weights. The rebalancing edit itself is linear algebra on residual states: project onto the evidence direction, scale the prior direction down by the conflict magnitude, and recombine. There is no learned correction model, no calibration dataset beyond the head-selection pass, and nothing that needs to be re-fit when you swap in a new input domain, which is the property that makes the cross-architecture transfer claim plausible rather than hand-wavy. The central claim, that hallucination is a representation-imbalance problem rather than an encoding problem, is supported by the modality-ablation evidence and it is a genuinely useful reframe, because it tells you where to intervene. But the method is a three-stage intervention pipeline with real degrees of freedom: how the complementary views are constructed, which heads are selected as the intervention region, and how the orthogonal split is computed. The paper reports strong aggregate gains, but the sensitivity to those choices, particularly the head selection, is where a practitioner would spend their time, and the paper does not fully stress-test it. The 12.9 and 8.6 point gains are on two benchmarks, and the smaller general-benchmark gains are reassuring but narrow. The training-free property is the selling point for adoption: you can drop this in front of an existing omni model, measure the CMM and AVHBench deltas, and decide in a day, which is a low-risk experiment. The cross-architecture transfer to VideoLLaMA2 is the strongest evidence that the evidence-prior imbalance is a real, shared structure rather than an artifact of one model's training. If you run omni models in production and cross-modal hallucination is a support-ticket driver, this is worth a serious pilot, and the head-localized intervention design is worth studying even if you build your own variant. Trust the reframe and the training-free deltas on the two hallucination benchmarks. Treat the general-benchmark numbers as non-degradation evidence, not as capability gains, and re-verify the head-selection sensitivity on your own backbone before committing.
