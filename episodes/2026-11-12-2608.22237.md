Read Less, Solve More: Token-Efficient Sparse Reading of External Content for AI Agents, by Zedong Liu, Jiaan Wu, and Xinyang Ma, from the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences and the Institute of Computing Technology, with a co-author at Songshan Lake Materials Laboratory, is an agent-infrastructure paper about controlling what an agent is allowed to read from external artifacts before the content enters the model's context.

The problem they quantify first is over-reading. External observations account for 60 to 80 percent of the tokens an agent processes across a trajectory, yet the reading interfaces used by most agents expose entire objects: a whole page, a whole file, a whole tool response, when the next decision often needs a handful of passages. In their annotations, supporting evidence for a long-document question covers a median of about 3 percent of the source tokens, and across representative workloads the share of exposed content that actually contributes to the answer ranges from 6 percent on low-sparsity tasks up to 69 percent of the reading budget being unnecessary on long-document question answering. And the cost compounds: content admitted at step i can be replayed in every later model call, so they define a replay-weighted exposure metric, and in paired runs the unmodified agent spends up to 17.3 times more tokens than the controlled reader without any task-quality improvement. Excess content is not just expensive, they argue, it dilutes the relevant evidence and can induce wrong tool use, source confusion, and failures to stop.

The proposed system is called SparseRead, and it is explicitly a control layer, not a model change. It sits between the agent and its artifacts and implements a contract: the agent submits a structured Read Intent describing what it needs for its next decision, and the layer returns an EvidencePack containing bounded, source-anchored evidence, the scope that was examined, the amount exposed, and a per-slot coverage record saying which evidence needs are covered and which are still unresolved. Neither the protocol nor the reader ever declares the evidence sufficient; the agent decides whether to answer, refine, or verify. That separation is the design principle, and it is what distinguishes this from retrieval, which locates passages, or compression, which shrinks content, because neither of those tracks evolving intent or coverage across heterogeneous artifacts.

There are three components. The Read Intent Protocol maintains the evidence state: a SHA-256 fingerprint identifies the artifact version, and when the artifact changes, the recorded evidence and coverage for it are invalidated. An underspecified intent gets a bounded object preview to refine against, and the access-level intent always takes precedence over the session intent from the user's original request. The Adaptive Reader Backends implement a unified reader interface over different artifact types, and the key empirical finding that motivates configurability is that no single location method wins: dense hybrid retrieval works best on long text, column queries on tables, and key-path lookup on JSON and logs. The backends can also do full-object computation with small model exposure, so a Reader can scan an entire table to compute a column sum and expose only the result, the row count, and the source, which decouples processing scope from model exposure. The third component is the Budgeted Read Gate, an inference-time controller that bounds cumulative raw exposure per episode and decides when sparse reading is no longer economical, with an explicit faithful full-view fallback. That fallback is important because their own ablations show the regime boundary is real: sparse reading cuts tokens by 80 to 88 percent on high-sparsity question answering and audit tasks, but forced sparse reading increases tokens by 309 percent on global statistics and 210 percent on a rule-coding task, where repeated selective access outweighs the savings.

The evaluation covers six models, including Claude Opus 5 and several DeepSeek and Qwen variants, across five scenarios: long-document question answering, PDF and report question answering, multi-file audits, security auditing, and web deep search. The choice of scenarios is deliberate, because it spans the two ends of the sparsity spectrum the paper cares about: the audit and question-answering tasks are the high-sparsity end, where a small slice of the artifact carries the answer, and the web deep search is the mixed end, where some pages are dense with signal and others are noise, and where the agent controls how many pages it ever looks at in the first place. The aggregate claim is a reduction of total tokens by up to 92.9 percent and wall time by up to 89.0 percent, while preserving or improving task quality in 97 percent of the model-by-scenario settings. On the web deep-search tasks specifically, tokens drop by 31 to 43 percent and task completion time by 23 to 37 percent, which is the more realistic number because deep search is where agents actually spend their budgets. A protocol-repair ablation shows that just having the intent and coverage record, with a fixed reader, reduces total tokens by 2.1 to 6.3 times across long-book question answering, multi-file audit, PDF report question answering, and web search, because the agent stops reopening artifacts it has already inspected. They also integrate the layer into three existing agent frameworks to demonstrate portability without model changes.

A few implementation notes for the skeptical reader. The method is model-agnostic in the sense that it wraps any LLM and adds no generative compression model of its own, which keeps the cost story honest: the savings are not paid for by a hidden summarizer call, though the intent validation and preview steps do add model calls. Prefix caching is handled explicitly: ordinary sparse reads leave preceding turns intact so exact-match prefix reuse survives, but a fallback that retires earlier sparse observations can break the cache from the edit point onward, which is a real serving cost the paper acknowledges. The evidence-sparsity annotations are manual and dataset-specific, so the 3 percent figure is a motivation, not a guarantee that your workload is that sparse. One more pattern from the teaser plots is worth internalizing: stronger models save more tokens under SparseRead, while the smaller model in the panel gains more accuracy, which suggests the control layer helps a weak model mostly by not drowning it in irrelevant evidence and helps a strong model mostly by not wasting its budget. That split is exactly what you would want from a reading gate, but it also means the magnitude of the gain is model-dependent, so the aggregate percentages should not be read as a per-model promise.

Skeptically: the headline percentages are dominated by the highest-sparsity scenarios, and the paper is honest that the boundary between sparse-friendly and full-view tasks cannot be predicted from object size or type alone, which means the gate is doing genuine online control and its behavior on workloads where the boundary is fuzzy is the part to test. The 97 percent quality-preservation figure is an aggregate over settings, and the 3 percent that are not preserved are exactly the settings you would care about in production, so I would want the per-setting table. The replay-weighted exposure metric is a logical measure, not a billing measure, and they note prefix caching can lower marginal cost, so the token-count story and the dollar story can diverge. And the web-search numbers, while directionally consistent, are on a fixed set of deep-search tasks, so transfer to open-ended agentic coding or browsing is not shown.

Bottom line: SparseRead is a well-scoped infrastructure contribution that treats reading as a controlled, stateful process rather than a retrieval call, and its central claim, that bounded admission with a full-view fallback saves tokens without costing quality on sparse workloads, is supported by the ablations and by the honest reporting of the regimes where forced sparsity hurts. The practical value depends on the workload being genuinely sparse, and the gate's boundary behavior on ambiguous workloads is the thing I would benchmark before putting this in front of a real agent.
