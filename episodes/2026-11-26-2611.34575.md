Sage-Med: Scaling Medical Multimodal Intelligence, from Ming Hu, Cheng Tang, and Wei Li, at the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. The claim: a 35-billion-parameter medical multimodal model with 3 billion activated parameters, trained on a staged curriculum over 322.7 million curated records, averages 58.26 percent across 31 public medical benchmarks and beats the strongest open-source medical model by over seven points.

This is a foundation-model report in the strongest sense, a new base model for medical multimodality, and it is built on a systematic negative result that motivates the whole effort. The authors evaluate the existing landscape and find that medical multimodal models tend to underperform frontier general-purpose models across medical tasks. When you scale a medical model's size, the gains go mostly to text capability, not to multimodal perception, and turning on reasoning modes does not close the visual gap. The comparison is stark: medical models average 42.71 percent on knowledge-intensive understanding against 57.12 for general models, and 65.00 against 78.14 on comprehensive text understanding. The gap is not a data-coverage problem you can fix by bolting on more medical text; it is a curriculum and curation problem.

The model is Sage-Med-Preview, a 35-billion-parameter mixture of experts with 3 billion activated per token, in the same sparse family as Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and Intern-S2-Preview. The training recipe is the contribution. Stage-specific curation first screens 322.7 million candidate records, then a progressive curriculum runs medical pretraining, staged supervised fine-tuning across three rounds, and finally adaptive reinforcement learning. The ablations isolate what each stage buys, and the most interesting result is that a refined continual-pretraining step on a selected 30-million-record subset with a balanced modality distribution outperforms pretraining on the full data pool, which is a concrete argument that curation beats volume for this task family. The curation is motivated by two documented gaps in public medical data. High-quality, modality-balanced paired multimodal records are scarce, which is exactly where the models are weakest, and the training distribution is dominated by single-turn, complete-information questions with short answers, giving little supervision for seeking information or handling uncertainty across turns. The curriculum is designed to attack both: balanced modalities at the pretraining stage, and multi-turn, information-seeking supervision in the later supervised rounds, before the adaptive reinforcement stage adds reward-based optimization on top.

The numbers break down by stage in a way that is worth quoting, because it is the actual recipe. After the first instruction-alignment stage, the macro-average score sits at 61.26. A second targeted supervised round lifts it to 61.45, a gain of zero point one nine percentage points, and a third targeted round lifts it to 62.02, a gain of zero point five seven. The reinforcement-learning step that follows the three supervised rounds then adds further improvement, and the paper's reading of this is that task-specific reward feedback still has useful work to do even after three supervised stages. That is the empirical case for the adaptive reinforcement-learning tail, and it is the part of the curriculum that is least common in a medical model report. On the aggregate, the model averages 58.26 percent across 31 public multimodal benchmarks, against 50.99 for the state-of-the-art open-source medical model Hulu-Med-235B-A22B and 52.24 for the general scientific model Intern-S2-Preview, and it approaches the proprietary GPT-6.2-Sol across comprehensive multimodal understanding, report generation, and visual grounding.

The fine-grained results are where the model pulls ahead of general-purpose frontier models. On the five comprehensive multimodal understanding benchmarks it averages 80.05 percent and exceeds all twelve comparators it is tested against. On surgical attention region localization, a task that asks the model to find where the surgeon is focusing in each laparoscopic frame, it reaches 78.85 percent at a recall threshold of zero point three and 48.50 mean intersection over union, beating all twelve comparators on both metrics. On a text-only orthopedic admission diagnosis task from electronic health records, it scores 61.25 percent and beats nine of the twelve comparators, including GPT-5.4 and Gemini-2.5-Flash. On a longitudinal clinical benchmark that follows a case from assessment through follow-up and credits a case only when every question is correct, it reaches a case accuracy of 47.48 percent and a question accuracy of 84.83 percent, ahead of the strongest open-source medical model at 40.23 and 81.88.

The per-task numbers are the ones to read if you care about deployment. On report quality, measured by a score that checks the correctness of clinical entities, the model reaches 66.52 percent and exceeds nine of the twelve comparators, including Hulu-Med-235B at 66.13, GPT-5.4 at 64.32, and GPT-6.2-Sol at 58.80, which is a rare case of beating the proprietary frontier on a generation task. On melanoma metastasis outcome prediction from dermoscopy images it scores 76.80 percent and exceeds eleven of the twelve, ahead of GPT-6.2-Sol at 75.26. On the clinical examination benchmark covering recommendation, diagnosis, and treatment planning, it achieves a one-turn accuracy of 52.14 percent, ahead of the open-source medical models. The pattern across these fine-grained tasks is consistent: the model is strongest on perception-heavy tasks that reward the curated, modality-balanced data, and it is the perception, not the reasoning, that the curriculum is actually buying.

The skeptical pass starts with the headline comparison. The 58.26 average is against a medical-model cohort and a small set of general models, and the paper itself is careful to note that open-source general models with far fewer activated parameters already match the strongest medical models in aggregate: Intern-S2-Preview-35B-A3B activates 3 billion parameters against 22 billion for Hulu-Med and still averages 59.90 percent. So the correct reading of the headline number is not that medical-specific pretraining dominates general scaling, but that a curated medical curriculum, on top of a strong general base, produces a model that is competitive with or better than purpose-built medical models while staying in the same parameter class. That is a useful and defensible claim, but it is narrower than the title implies.

There are also the usual report caveats. The evaluation mixes 61 public and 13 private multi-center benchmarks across more than 40 modalities, which is a strength for coverage, but the private benchmarks cannot be independently reproduced, and the aggregate average flattens large per-task variance. The model is a preview release, and the paper is explicit that substantial challenges remain in complex medical reasoning and diagnosis. One of the negative results deserves emphasis, because it is a direct warning for anyone planning to add a reasoning mode to a medical model: within the same backbone, enabling the thinking variant does not improve perception and in some cases weakens it, with the perception benchmarks of the two groups separated by a difference of only zero point five four percentage points. The paper's own stated direction for closing the remaining gap is agentic, wrapping the model in frameworks with specialized clinical tools, iterative verification, and structured harnesses, which is a candid admission that the base model, however well curated, is not yet a complete clinical system. If I were deploying this, I would re-verify the surgical and radiology localization tasks on my own imaging distribution, because domain shift in medical imaging is where aggregate benchmark numbers most often mislead. The curation result, that a balanced 30-million-record subset beats the full pool, is the most transferable finding here, and it is the one I would trust enough to change my own pretraining recipe.

Bottom line: a credible medical foundation-model report whose main contribution is the staged curation-plus-curriculum recipe, and whose aggregate numbers are strong against medical peers while honestly competitive, rather than dominant, against general frontier models. The 30-million-record curation ablation and the negative result on reasoning modes are the two findings I would carry into my own work.
