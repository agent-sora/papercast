OSWorld-Pro is from Zhilin Wang, Shaokun Zhang, and Yifan Zhang, with a full team of twelve authors at NVIDIA. It is a process-based benchmark for computer use agents, the class of models that operate a real desktop environment, clicking, typing, and navigating applications to complete long-horizon tasks. The paper's argument is that the standard end-state evaluation, where you check whether the final deliverable is correct after hundreds of steps, hides how and why agents fail, and OSWorld-Pro is built to expose the process.

The motivation starts from a concrete limitation. Existing computer use agent benchmarks, including OSWorld itself, score the terminal state: did the agent produce the right document, the right file, the right final screen. That has two problems. First, it gives no diagnostic signal, an agent that fails a task might have done everything right for ninety steps and fumbled the last one, or it might have been lost from step ten, and the final-state score looks identical in both cases. Second, the failures it does reveal are opaque, so the next round of model improvement has no clean target. The paper's example is specific: an agent that errs during keyboard inputs needs a different fix than one that errs in its click targeting, and end-state evaluation does not tell you which it is.

The design of OSWorld-Pro addresses this directly. It contains over 300 tasks with over 2,800 subgoals, grounded in over 67,000 human annotations. Each task is decomposed into constituent subgoals, and the benchmark scores the fulfillment of each subgoal along the way, not just the final outcome. This gives partial reward for early success without requiring the final deliverable, which is the core methodological change. The subgoals are not independent rubric items; they are sequentially dependent, which mirrors how real desktop tasks actually work, where completing step three presupposes that steps one and two landed. The annotation effort behind that structure is what makes the benchmark expensive and also what makes it defensible.

The task design itself is worth a paragraph, because it is where a lot of the signal lives. A representative task chains four or more different applications: write a Python function in a code editor, run it in the terminal, copy its output, paste it into a word processor, format it, and save it with a specific name. The subgoal decomposition follows that chain, so the benchmark can tell you whether the agent failed at the coding step, the run step, the copy-paste step, or the formatting step. The task categories include coordination tasks that span four or more distinct applications, and the benchmark explicitly measures generalization across Linux distributions and application configurations, which is a real deployment concern that single-VM toy environments do not capture. The paper also reports that the task construction was validated with feasibility annotations, where annotators indicate whether a subgoal is actually achievable in the environment, which filters out the ambiguous subgoals that would otherwise poison the scoring.

The scoring layer is an LLM-judge, and the paper is unusually transparent about how hard it was to get right. The early experiments used GPT-5.6-Luna with default medium reasoning, but judging trajectories that run to hundreds of steps across tens of subgoals, with screenshots up to 500 megabytes, required extensive iteration on the judge design. They report that they only found success with the OpenAI GPT-5.6 model family, while Claude, Gemini, and open models raised various errors related to payload size, image quantity, or context limits. That is a significant practical caveat, because it means the benchmark's scoring is currently tied to one vendor's models, which is a dependency the authors are honest about.

The reliability of the judge is then validated against the human annotations. They report excellent agreement, with Cohen's kappa above 0.8 across all scoring aspects, and an agreement rate of 0.987 on the aspects where annotators themselves agreed. They also flag, with appropriate care, that some subgoals have lower inter-annotator agreement, and the benchmark is careful to separate the cases where the judge is measuring a well-defined subgoal from the cases where even humans disagree.

Now the findings, which are the interesting part. The benchmark is hard for state-of-the-art models. The top performer, Claude Opus 5, achieves only 75.7 percent on OSWorld-Pro versus 83.4 percent on the original OSWorld. That is a meaningful drop, and it tells you that the process-based evaluation is measuring something the end-state evaluation was not. The drop decomposes in a way the end-state score never would: the gap is concentrated in the middle of the trajectory, in the subgoals that require holding a plan across application boundaries, rather than in the first or last subgoals, which the models tend to handle. That pattern is what the partial-credit scoring makes visible, and it is the finding an agent team would actually act on. The paper then identifies process-focused failure modes across the models it evaluates, including subgoal-irrelevant actions, where the agent takes an action that does not advance any of the current subgoals, and click-based failure patterns, where the agent's click targeting diverges from its intended action. The paper also notes, as a behavioral observation, that Claude Opus 5 was shown to occasionally engage in exploratory behavior that does not map to the task subgoals, which is exactly the kind of process signal that end-state evaluation would have swallowed.

There is also a model-scale analysis. Within the GPT-5.6 family, the larger models generally do better on subgoal identification, and GPT-5.6-Sol with maximum reasoning effort approaches human performance on identifying targeted subgoals. The task categories include coordination tasks that span four or more different applications, and the benchmark measures how well models generalize across Linux distributions and application configurations, which is a real deployment concern that toy environments do not capture. One more design detail deserves mention: the benchmark distinguishes the subgoals an agent must complete from the rubric items a human would check at the end, and it scores them separately, so a task can be half complete in subgoals while the final state looks plausible. That separation is what makes the 75.7 versus 83.4 gap informative rather than just a lower number. The annotation pipeline is also described as a three-stage process: annotators first decompose a task into candidate subgoals, then a second pass verifies each subgoal against the task instructions, and a third pass scores the whole set, which is the source of the 67,000 figure and the reason the benchmark claims the subgoal layer is auditable rather than a black-box rubric.

Where I would be skeptical. First, the LLM-judge dependency is the biggest one. If the scoring layer only works reliably on one vendor's models, then the benchmark is partly a property of that vendor's infrastructure, and a future change to the judge models could shift the numbers without any change to the agents being tested. The paper mitigates this with the human-agreement validation, but the validation is a snapshot, not a standing guarantee. Second, 300 tasks with 2,800 subgoals is a solid benchmark but still small by the standards of the math or code evaluation suites, and the variance on any single model's score is going to be meaningful. Third, the subgoal decomposition is an editorial choice made by the annotators, and while the high inter-annotator agreement is reassuring, the choice of where to draw the subgoal boundaries can itself favor or penalize particular agent strategies, for example an agent that reaches a correct subgoal state through an unexpected path.

The verdict: this is the most serious attempt yet to bring process-level evaluation to computer use agents, with the annotation scale and the human-agreement validation that make it credible, and the finding that the top model drops from 83.4 to 75.7 percent is itself the paper's main result. If your team is building computer use agents and you only track end-state success, OSWorld-Pro tells you where the process is actually breaking down, and that diagnostic value is worth more than the raw scores.
