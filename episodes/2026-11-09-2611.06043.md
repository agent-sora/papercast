The paper is called "Confucius4-R2T2," which is short for Real Real-Time Transcription, and it describes a stable-prefix streaming speech recognition model. The first three authors are Daniel Huang, Qi He, and Shaoxiong Duan. The work comes out of NetEase Youdao in Beijing, with Shaoxiong Duan affiliated with UC San Diego.

The problem is specific and practical. Streaming speech recognition increasingly feeds machines rather than human readers. A live translator, a captioning system, or a voice agent consumes each token the moment it appears and starts acting on it. If a token is emitted early and then revised later as more audio arrives, that is not a cosmetic annoyance. It is wasted computation, and in an agent it is a possibly wrong side effect that has already fired. This failure mode has several names in the literature, flicker, revision, hypothesis instability, but the paper gives it a clean formulation. They define a stable-prefix streaming ASR model as one where, for every audio signal and every pair of times, the text released at the earlier time is a prefix of the text released at the later time. In plain terms, once a token is released, it never changes. They are careful to point out this property is purely behavioral, it constrains only the ordering of the outputs, not the architecture, not the encoder, not the training recipe. And it is orthogonal to accuracy and to latency. A system that emits nothing until the end of the utterance is perfectly stable but useless in real time; a system that emits a fixed token sequence is stable but wrong. So stability is a constraint to satisfy while you optimize the other two.

The core new idea is something they call the Longest Stable Prefix, LSP. Take the full-context output, that is, what the model produces when it can see the entire utterance. That is your reference. Now feed the audio in progressively, and at each time step look at everything the model has output so far. The LSP at that time is the longest earlier output that is entirely a prefix of the full-context output. The crucial constraint is that the label must be model-admissible, meaning it has to equal a whole output the model actually produced from the audio available at that time. It cannot be a truncation of one output or a string stitched together from several. They prove a set of properties for this definition: it is monotone under the prefix order, it is consistent with the full-audio output, and it is the maximal label among all labels that equal a complete earlier output. They spend real space distinguishing their definition from two other stable-prefix notions in the literature, one from an older measurement paper and one from a recent simultaneous translation paper, and showing that the three are not the same object and that theirs is the one that is both final-anchored and model-admissible. The trade-off of their choice is that it is conservative: if even one token in an output diverges from the eventual output, the credit for that step drops to zero, which biases the model toward emitting later rather than earlier.

The adaptation method is what makes this buildable. They start from Qwen3-ASR-1.7B and post-train it on roughly 1,500 hours of Chinese and English audio, while freezing the pre-trained audio encoder and the projector. The architecture is left completely unchanged, no causalization, no attention masks, no new layers. The base model has a Qwen3-1.7B language model, a projector, and an audio encoder called AuT with 300 million parameters and a hidden size of 1,024, which downsamples feature-bank inputs by a factor of eight to a token rate of 12.5 per second. Because the encoder stays bidirectional and frozen, the train-test mismatch that streaming usually introduces is handled in the decoder instead. The decoder learns to emit a special Wait token when the audio received so far is not enough to determine the next token. The Wait token lives in the same vocabulary as the text tokens, so it is a supervision choice, not an architectural change. This separates the timing decision, when to emit, from the content decision, what to emit.

The training data construction matters for the failure modes. They augment the audio to target hallucination on non-speech, degradation under noise and reverberation, and the tendency to translate instead of transcribe when the language cue is ambiguous. Noise augmentation uses additive noise with signal-to-noise ratios sampled from 10 to 35 decibels, plus real or simulated room impulse responses for reverberation, and they flip the language tag on a subset of samples while keeping the transcript target. For labeling, they feed the teacher model cumulative audio prefixes every 80 milliseconds, record the intervals where the output agrees with the LSP, merge those into stable regions, and label each region by its start and end timestamps. During training they cut each sample at a random point, keep only the prefix, and the target is the LSP if the cut falls inside a stable interval, or the LSP followed by a Wait token otherwise. They also introduce a single scalar they call streamability, the area under the curve of how much of the correct prefix the model has committed as a fraction of the full transcript, as a function of the fraction of audio received. They use it to argue Qwen3-ASR-1.7B is the best base for streamization, with a normalized streamability of 0.493, and to note that a causally-masked streaming variant of Whisper performs worse than its own full-context base because its instability shortens the stable prefix.

The results are measured at 160 millisecond chunks in the main comparisons. On English word-error-rate across a set of test sets, the base model's offline decode is the accuracy reference, and R2T2 at 160 milliseconds lands close to it on the clean sets. For example, on the LS-clean set the offline base is 1.62 and R2T2 is 1.91; on LS-other, offline 3.39 versus R2T2 4.12; on Giga-clean, offline 8.60 versus 8.81; on AMI, offline 9.18 versus 10.07. They report that the streaming model stays within a point or two of full-context on the easier sets and further behind on the noisy and conversational ones, which is the expected pattern. They also report a provisional-token budget, a setting they call U, that trades accuracy against emission latency: U equals 1 prioritizes recognition quality, U equals 0 prioritizes lower latency, and the committed text is identical under both because of the stable-prefix guarantee. The latency is reported as a retrospective emission lag, the audio time at which a prefix becomes permanently stable minus the force-aligned reference end, measured in a way that excludes execution, queuing, and rendering time.

Now the skeptical read. The stable-prefix guarantee is a genuinely clean contribution, and the model-admissibility requirement is a real refinement over the prior definitions, so the theoretical framing is sound and worth citing. But the practical case rests on a few things to check. The accuracy comparison is against their own base model and a handful of open and proprietary streaming systems, and the proprietary numbers come partly from API calls whose server-side buffering is undisclosed, so the latency figures for those are estimates, not measurements. The 1,500 hours of post-training audio is proprietary, so you can reproduce the method on your own base model but not their exact data. The streamability metric is a nice diagnostic but the paper itself flags that whether it generalizes beyond ASR, for instance to simultaneous translation, is open. What I would trust is the design principle, that you can convert a strong full-context recognizer into a stable streaming one by learning a Wait-gated commit rule against self-generated LSP labels, with a frozen encoder and no architectural change. What I would re-verify before deploying is the accuracy gap on your own noisy and multilingual data, and the real end-to-end latency once you include the inference overhead that their retrospective metric deliberately leaves out.

The bottom line: Confucius4-R2T2 turns a full-context speech model into a never-revises streaming one through a well-defined stable-prefix training target and a frozen-encoder adaptation, and it gets close to full-context accuracy with low emission lag. The guarantee and the labeling method are the durable ideas; the specific numbers are strong but tied to proprietary data and an in-house evaluation.
