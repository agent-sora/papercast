Are Dense Rewards Worth Their Tokens in GRPO, by Vikash Singh, Weicong Chen, and Debargha Ganguly, from Case Western Reserve University, is a matched-compute study of whether dense or process rewards actually help group-relative policy optimization once you count what they cost in generated tokens and training time. The headline finding is that matching compute reverses the ranking: a Monte-Carlo process reward that beats standard GRPO per training update ends fourteen point three percentage points lower at equal tokens and seven point eight points lower at equal training time, and none of four dense-reward variants outperforms GRPO even when given as much training time as the baseline.

The problem is a real and common one in how people validate a change to their RL training. RL with verifiable rewards is the standard way to improve a language model's reasoning, and GRPO is the widely used algorithm. In GRPO, the model samples a group of completions for a prompt, a verifier gives each completion a binary reward, and each completion's advantage is its reward relative to the rest of the group. Critically, GRPO gives every token of a completion the same credit, which means a solution that sets up a problem correctly but slips in the last step is penalized for its good steps as well as its bad one. That is the motivation for dense rewards, also called process rewards, which give different steps different credit, derived from human step labels, from a prover's progress, from rewards learned on outcome labels, or from a Monte-Carlo estimate of the probability of reaching the correct answer from each step. The question the paper tackles is how you decide whether such a reward helps, and the answer the authors give is that the two cheap checks people actually run, a per-update comparison and a per-token gradient test, both leave out the cost.

The hidden cost has two parts. First, a dense reward consumes generated tokens. In their setting, the Monte-Carlo reward's continuations account for ninety-one percent of all generated tokens. The way the Monte-Carlo reward works is that you cut each completion at five fixed token positions, splitting it into six segments, and you sample four continuations from the prefix ending at each cut, using the fraction that end with a correct answer as an estimate of the prefix's value. Those continuations are only generated and scored, never trained on. So with the same token budget, outcome-only GRPO makes five hundred eighty-four updates and the Monte-Carlo reward makes fifty. Second, the reward consumes GPU time, but not in proportion to its tokens, because an inference engine generates those continuation tokens in large batches much more cheaply than a training step processes them. So a reward can win per update and lose per token, and a comparison that counts only tokens can mislead about GPU-hours.

The authors' approach is a matched-compute protocol. Every training intervention gets the same budget, measured in both generated tokens, counting continuations and discarded samples, and in training GPU-hours broken down by stage. They apply it to two interventions that spend compute in opposite ways. Dense rewards spend tokens to improve each update. The other, prompt selection, spends one forward pass over candidate prompts to choose those whose predicted success is nearest one-half, because a group whose completions are all correct or all wrong produces no update. They reimplemented the GRPO trainer and ran one thousand six hundred sixty-eight runs, about one thousand four hundred sixty-eight GPU-hours, on six small Qwen and Llama models. Because the expected gains are small, comparable to the variation between random seeds, they preregistered each study, fixing the hypotheses, primary comparisons, and equivalence margins before running, with controls for side effects including a random-choice control, a retuned-learning-rate baseline, and a strict verifier to rule out drift in what the reward accepts.

The results are the point, and they are stark. Matching compute reverses the ranking: on Qwen two point five one and a half billion, the Monte-Carlo reward leads standard GRPO per update, yet ends fourteen point three points lower at equal tokens and seven point eight points lower at equal training time, in every seed, and the deficit recurs across Qwen three at zero point six, one point seven, and four billion, on GSM8K, and with longer completions. Even when given as much training time as the baseline, none of the four dense-reward variants shows a significant gain. The gradient test, which is the cheap pre-training check, orders the rewards the way training does, but it overestimates each one by one to two points, which is as large as the gains the test is meant to detect. And here is the constructive half: training on the prompts whose predicted success is nearest one-half does improve accuracy at matched compute, by one point seven points on Qwen three zero point six billion and zero point eight on Qwen two point five one and a half billion, and the gain replicates on fresh seeds and on one thousand five hundred forty-three unseen test problems, while a random-choice control does not reproduce it. The protocol also measures what those gains are worth: they come early in training, and standard GRPO with twice the initial learning rate comes within half a point of them.

Now the skeptical part, which is where I would start before trusting the headline. First, the study is entirely on small models, from zero point six to four billion parameters, and on the MATH benchmark with numeric answers. The cost structure of the Monte-Carlo reward, the ninety-one percent of tokens on continuations, is a property of the method, but the magnitude of the quality gap is a property of the model and the task. I would want the matched-compute comparison at a scale where the base policy is stronger and the problems harder, because it is entirely plausible that the dense-reward gap narrows or even flips when the credit assignment problem is genuinely harder, which is exactly where process rewards are supposed to help. The authors do note that some single-seed pilots at seven billion are positive, which is a caveat to the generalization, but it is a small and uncontrolled caveat.

Second, the constructive result, that prompt selection near one-half helps, is the more transferable finding, and it has a subtle dependency. It relies on a ridge critic on the policy's hidden state at the end of the prompt to predict each candidate's success, and the whole gain comes from a single extra prefill pass that scores the candidates. The rule that the gain appears where the candidates are predicted to fail more often than not is a nice diagnostic, but it is post-hoc, and the fact that standard GRPO with twice the learning rate gets within half a point is a real warning that the one point seven point gain may be partly an under-tuned baseline. I would want the retuned-baseline comparison run on all the models, not flagged as a control, before I accepted that the prompt-selection gain is robust rather than a schedule artifact.

Third, the protocol itself is the strongest contribution and the most worth adopting, but it is expensive and the preregistration is what makes it credible, which is also its limitation. One thousand six hundred sixty-eight runs to resolve effects at the one-to-two point scale is a serious compute commitment, and the equivalence margins of plus or minus two points, or one point on some models, are set in advance in a way that is methodologically correct but means the study is specifically designed to call things equivalent within those bands. I would want to see whether the protocol's verdicts are stable to a modest change in the margin, because a finding that a reward is equivalent to the baseline within two points is only as strong as the chosen margin.

The engineering gotchas are worth naming if you are going to use this. The matched-compute protocol requires you to instrument your trainer to count every generated token, including continuations and discarded dynamic-sampling groups, and to break GPU time down by stage, which most RL frameworks do not expose out of the box. The Monte-Carlo reward's cost is dominated by an inference engine, so the token-versus-GPU-hour distinction is real and you need both budgets to make a fair call, not just one. And the strict-verifier control matters: with a lenient verifier that also accepts answers outside the boxed marker, runs gradually stopped boxing their answers, which is a silent reward drift that a strict verifier removes without lowering accuracy, so if you are reusing a lenient verifier you should be aware your training objective has quietly changed.

The bottom line is this: counting the compute cost of a training intervention, and validating it at matched tokens and matched GPU-hours rather than per update, is a genuinely important methodological correction that overturns a popular process-reward result and should become the default way people vet changes to their RL recipes. But the negative result on dense rewards is established on small models and a single benchmark, so I would re-run the matched-compute comparison at my own scale and on my own task before I concluded that dense rewards are worthless rather than merely over-costly in this particular regime.
