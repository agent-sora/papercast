DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash: Pushing the Limits of KV Cache Compression. The first three authors listed are Anyi Xu, Bangcai Lin, and Bing Xue, from DeepSeek, a DeepSeek-AI paper with an author list of well over five hundred names.

This is a foundation-model technical report, and it is aimed at one specific bottleneck: the cost of long-context, input-heavy agentic workloads. The authors' framing is that prior work has cut the cost of long-context computation, but prefill is still expensive, and large KV caches still strain HBM, SSD capacity, and data-transfer bandwidth. Compute, storage, and bandwidth together are what keep deployment costs from falling further, and this model is designed to attack exactly that triple. Checkpoints are released on Hugging Face under the DeepSeek organization, so everything described here is reproducible in principle, which matters for a report like this.

The model is a multimodal Mixture-of-Experts with a 552 billion parameter backbone and support for contexts up to one million tokens. The headline architectural piece is something called the Causal Encoder-Decoder, or CED. In a CED, the upper half of the layers can directly reuse the key and value cache generated by the lower half, which roughly halves the depth of KV generation during prefill. The effect is asymmetric between the two phases of inference: during decode, 16 billion parameters are activated per token, but during prefill only 8 billion. Since agentic workloads are overwhelmingly prefill-heavy, frequent tool calls each triggering a large prefill, that asymmetry is the economic core of the whole design. The paper states that for a sequence length much larger than the sliding window, CED reduces prefill complexity from the product of sequence length and layer count to roughly half of that.

On top of CED sits the KV compression stack, and this is the part the title refers to. Compressed Sparse Attention 2, CSA2, introduces cross-layer KV and index reuse: layers share the sparse index structure and the selected KV entries, with a hierarchical sparse indexer deciding which entries to select. CSA2 is combined with FP4 KV caching, four-bit floating point, for the main key and value cache. The result is a global KV cache footprint, the part that must always live in HBM, of 890 bytes per token, which the paper reports as roughly a quarter of the corresponding footprint of DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Against the original DeepSeek-V1, the per-token global KV cache size is down by a factor of 437. There is a second tier for the persistent cache that lives on SSD or host memory, managed by a deployment optimization called SWA Bounded Replay, which only prefills the last window of tokens for sliding-window attention instead of the full prompt, because the effective receptive field of the sliding window is much smaller than the theoretical window times half the depth. That brings the persistent KV footprint down to roughly one eighth of DeepSeek-V4-Flash.

The paper also includes several supporting components. Engram is an external embedding store: a table of approximately 16 million entries, with table sizes chosen to be distinct primes, used at FP8 precision alongside the key and value projections, and optimized with momentum-based rules that treat the embedding tables, token embeddings, and prediction head as a coupled group. There is a single-pass variant of a hyper-connection mechanism, a DSpark component, and FP4 for the main KV cache. For training, the model is pretrained on a multimodal corpus of 45 trillion tokens, followed by comprehensive post-training that includes large-scale synthetic agent tasks, reinforcement learning in synthesized environments, a dedicated sandbox platform called DSec for running agents at scale, and a controllable-reasoning-effort mechanism in the RL reward.

The training and serving infrastructure is a substantial part of the report, and it is where a deployment team will actually live. On the training side, the multimodal pipeline is built to handle the 45 trillion token corpus, and the CSA2 cross-layer attention sharing requires a dedicated attention-sharing training mode so the layers can learn to reuse each other's KV structure rather than treating it as an afterthought. The Engram external embedding store is trained jointly with the token embeddings and the prediction head under momentum-based updates, which is a non-trivial optimization detail because three coupled parameter groups with different update dynamics are easy to destabilize at this scale. On the serving side, the persistent KV cache management and the SWA Bounded Replay are explicitly framed as deployment optimizations, not research curiosities, and the paper is honest about the failure modes it had to engineer around in the sandbox platform, DSec, including filesystem driver issues, illegal memory access under AppArmor, and answer leakage through package mirrors, all of which are the kind of production detail that does not show up in a model card but determines whether the agentic RL pipeline actually runs.

The evaluation results, as reported. The base-model comparison in Table 1 puts three models on the same internal benchmark framework: DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base with 13 billion activated parameters and a 284 billion backbone, DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base with 49 billion activated and a 1.6 trillion backbone, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-Base with 8 billion activated during prefill and 16 billion during decode over the 552 billion backbone. On world knowledge, AGIEval exact match scores are 83.9, 84.4, and 83.4 respectively. The authors state that scores within a gap of 0.3 are considered at the same level, and their claim is that the V4.1-Flash base, with a substantially smaller activated parameter count and a heavily reduced KV cache, is fully on par with both baselines. That is a strong claim resting on their own internal framework, and I would want to see it confirmed on the standard public suites before treating the parity statement as settled. The post-training section reports strong results across text-based and multimodal agentic scenarios, with the DSec sandbox and the agentic RL pipeline being where the bulk of the post-training engineering sits, including handling reward hacking attempts and environment crashes at scale.

What would I trust? The KV-cache math is the most defensible part. The per-token footprint numbers are concrete: 890 bytes per token globally, a quarter of V4-Flash, a 437-fold reduction over V1, and one eighth persistent. Those are the kind of numbers that follow directly from the architectural choices, cross-layer reuse plus FP4, and they are exactly the levers a serving team needs when they are sizing HBM. The CED prefill asymmetry, 8 billion active for prefill versus 16 billion for decode, is also a clean, verifiable property. For anyone serving long-context agentic traffic, the prefill-heavy cost profile is the right target, and the paper is honest about which phase each design decision helps.

What would I re-verify before adopting? First, the base-model parity claim is entirely on their internal framework, and the 0.3 tolerance for calling scores equivalent is their choice. A 552 billion backbone with 8 billion active parameters in prefill is an unusual configuration, and the question of what capability is being traded for the prefill speed is only answered by their own harness. Second, the post-training agentic results are where a model like this actually earns its keep, and the report points to its own benchmarks, DeepSWE and SWE-Bench Pro among them, plus internal agentic evaluations. I would want independent runs on public agentic suites, and on the long-context end I would want the one million token context tested past the 64k and 128k marks where most of the published evidence lives. Third, the SWA Bounded Replay trick is a deployment optimization with a bounded receptive field, so it is a place where behavior can silently degrade on workloads that depend on distant context within the sliding window, and the paper's own observation that the effective receptive field is much smaller than the theoretical one is a warning flag, not a reassurance. The FP4 KV cache is the most aggressive of the compression choices, and any quality delta it introduces would show up as subtle recall degradation in retrieval-heavy tasks rather than as a benchmark collapse.

Bottom line: this is a serious foundation-model release whose central contribution is a concrete, measurable reduction in the cost of long-context serving, with 890 bytes per token of global KV cache and a prefill phase that activates half the decode-time parameters, delivered at a base-model capability the authors report as on par with both their flash and pro lines. Before you build a serving stack on it, independently verify the long-context and agentic parity claims on public benchmarks, and treat the FP4 KV cache and the bounded SWA replay as the two places where you should expect the first quality regressions to surface.
