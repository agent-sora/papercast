WorldLeague, by Xinyang Zhou, Qi Li, and Xinchao Wang, at the xML Lab of the National University of Singapore, is a benchmark for one question: what range of physical goals can today's AI systems actually achieve autonomously? The benchmark is 60 simulated robot tasks, and the design choice that distinguishes it from household-activity benchmarks is that tasks are organized by the physical demands that determine success, not by the activity they resemble. Delivering a cup when the cup is named and in plain sight, and delivering the cup that has moved since the robot last saw the room, both look like cup delivery, but only the second tests memory, and WorldLeague exists to make that distinction measurable. The headline result is that among 13 evaluated systems, the best, Claude Fable 5.2, achieves 13 of the 60 physical goals and a mean Interactive Score of 28.58 out of 100. The benchmark is currently a measurement of how far everyone is from the goal, and the numbers say the field is far.

The structure is three tracks spanning six capability dimensions. WorldSeek tests navigation and goal grounding: the robot explores scenes to find hidden objects and gather evidence. WorldShape tests geometric reconfiguration and physical reasoning: rearranging objects, fitting things through openings, changing the scene. WorldShift tests temporal memory and constraint management: tracking a world that changes, recovering from disturbances. The six dimensions are navigation, goal grounding, geometry, physics, memory, and constraints. Each task is a specific scene with specified objects, initial conditions, and constraints, and physical predicates check conditions like stable placement or recovery after a disturbance. The scenes are built on the BEHAVIOR-1K suite, which is what gives the predicates their grounding in a maintained simulation environment rather than hand-built scenes. The authors state that they hand-inspected all 60 task-and-scene pairs, verified reference runs meet the goals, and checked the scoring conditions against the task specifications, which is the right level of care for a benchmark whose value depends entirely on the predicates being correct. Their case studies make the scoring concrete: in one episode, a system completes the nominal motion but the object's relation and stability checks fail at termination, so it earns partial credit of 33.33 for physical progress while still scoring 100 on the terminal questions, a clean illustration of the two scores measuring different things.

The comparison protocol is what makes it possible to put VLMs, VLAs, and world-action models on one scoreboard. VLMs use a decision-preserving action abstraction: the model chooses targets, action parameters, sequences, and responses to feedback, while an interface executes the motions, an adaptation of the Show-Harness style interpreter. VLA and WAM policies use their native control interfaces. What is shared is the goal, the constraints, and the physical scoring. Each system is scored two ways per task. The Interactive Score measures progress toward each requirement of the physical goal, a partial-credit measure from 0 to 100, and the physical success rate measures how often the full goal and all constraints are satisfied. Together, per dimension, they form what the paper calls a Physical Agency Profile. For VLMs there is also terminal question answering as an auxiliary measure of whether the model understood the scene, recognized the outcome, and remembered earlier events, in the same episodes.

The results are stark, and the two scores diverge in a way that matters. Claude Fable 5.2 leads overall with a mean Interactive Score of 28.58 and physical success on 21.7 percent of its 60 tasks. Its 47 non-success episodes split into 12 with partial progress and 35 with zero credit. GPT-6.1-Sol and GPT-6-Astra sit behind at 22.28 and 20.06, with physical success on 15.0 and 13.3 percent, and the field drops off a cliff below them: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash averages 11.86, Kimi-K3.1-Pro 9.08, and Qwen3.8-27B 6.31, with the open-weight systems at the bottom of the table. The profile shapes differ: Fable leads in navigation, goal grounding, geometric reconfiguration, and temporal memory, while GPT-6.1-Sol and GPT-6-Astra tie for first on constraint management, where both hit a physical success rate of 50 percent on the small number of constraint tasks, while Fable scores 16.7 percent there. The geometry dimension is where the whole field is weakest, with Fable at 17.7 percent success and a mean Interactive Score of 23.53, and most systems near zero on physics. Across the 59 tasks shared by all 13 systems, the systems collectively achieve only 16 distinct goals, which is the number to remember: 13 frontier and capable systems, and 43 of the 59 shared tasks that none of them fully completes.

The finding I find most interesting is the one that separates understanding from doing. In the VLM episodes with terminal question answering, the model answers the final questions correctly, meaning it identified the right objects, recognized the outcome, and remembered the relevant events, in 347 episodes. In 249 of those, 71.8 percent, the Interactive Score is zero. The model knew what the goal was and knew the state of the world, and still made no physical progress toward the goal. Goal-relevant understanding and physical goal achievement are being measured as separate variables here, and the data says they are. This is a clean experimental version of a suspicion the field has had informally: the bottleneck in embodied performance is not perception or reasoning in the abstract, it is the translation of correct beliefs into motion that satisfies a physical predicate. The trajectory analysis goes further and identifies which requirements remain unmet after partial progress, which turns the benchmark from a scoreboard into a failure taxonomy, and the paper argues that is the point of scoring per-requirement rather than per-episode.

A useful structural detail is how the three classes of system are placed on the same board. The vision-language models run through a decision-preserving action abstraction: the model emits targets, action parameters, sequences, and responses to feedback, and a harness executes the motions. The vision-language-action and world-action models, by contrast, use their native control interfaces, the same low-level control stack they would run in deployment. What the three classes share is only the goal, the constraints, and the physical scoring, which is exactly what keeps the comparison meaningful even though the interfaces differ. And the shared-task analysis is where the comparison tightens: across the 59 tasks that all 13 systems ran, the leading system completes 12, the next two complete eight and seven, and all thirteen together complete only 16 distinct goals, which is a sobering picture of how little the current systems overlap on the part of the task space they all attempt.

What to check before using this number. The scores are at seed 0, a single seed, and the physical success rates are computed over 60 tasks or 59, so a single task flip is 1.7 percent of the overall rate. The VLA and WAM comparisons run on 59 shared tasks rather than 60, and the paper should be read for which tasks each system class was scored on before comparing success rates across classes. The 60 tasks are simulated, and while the predicates are physical, the sim-to-real gap is exactly the phenomenon the companion benchmark work this month shows flips the conclusions. And the Interactive Score is a partial-credit measure whose weighting across requirements is a design decision the paper should have you audit before you treat 28.58 as a comparable quantity across system classes.

Two-sentence verdict: WorldLeague is the most principled scoreboard yet for physical agency, and its central finding, that even the best system completes barely a fifth of the tasks and that correct scene understanding coexists with zero physical progress in nearly three quarters of such episodes, is a useful and humbling baseline. If your embodied stack claims goal achievement, this is the benchmark to run it against, with the caveat that everything reported is single-seed and simulated.
