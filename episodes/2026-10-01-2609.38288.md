AREX-2. This is from the AREX Team at the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence, with Hongjin Qian, Chaofan Li, and Kun Luo as the first three named authors and Zheng Liu corresponding.

The paper defines self-improvement in a specific, testable way: the ability of an agent to iteratively refine a solution at test time. It decomposes that ability into two components. Reflection: given the current solution, produce a better one. Long-horizon execution: keep doing that effectively over many rounds, without the quality of each round degrading. The working hypothesis is that both capabilities are domain-agnostic, so they can be learned in domains that offer clean, verifiable feedback, and then expected to transfer. The chosen domains are machine learning engineering and algorithmic programming, because both give you an automatic score for every candidate solution, which is exactly what you need to build a trajectory of sustained improvement.

The data construction is the core contribution. The team synthesizes long-horizon improvement trajectories: they build environments where an agent can repeatedly attempt a task, and they run an agent inside those environments until it produces a sequence of attempts where each attempt is better than the last, or at least where the improvement steps are well labeled. Section 2.2 covers how the environments are built, 2.3 how the agent runs to produce trajectories of sustained improvement, and 2.4 how trajectories are selected and converted into supervision. The selection step matters, because most rollouts will not show monotone improvement, and training on flat or regressing trajectories would teach the opposite of what you want.

They train on this mixture, together with the deep-research data from the original AREX recipe, and the model is built on Qwen3.8-27B. The headline results: 81.8 on MLE-bench Lite, which is the highest score in their comparison table, 8.1 points above the strongest baseline, Naive-N0.5-Flash, and 9.1 points above the strongest closed-weight baseline, GPT-5.6 Sol. On Frontier-CS, 70.7, the highest among open-weight models, exceeding the strongest reported open-weight baseline by 16.0 points and sitting within 5.7 points of the strongest closed-weight system, GPT-5.6 Sol at 76.4. On transfer: 84.0 on BrowseComp, 52.6 on Humanity's Last Exam text-only, 92.2 on GAIA, and 93.8 on DeepSearchQA, leading the reported models with at most 40B parameters on BrowseComp.

The scaling result is the one I would put on a slide. The question is whether a bigger budget in rounds actually buys more improvement, or whether the agent burns through its useful reflections early and then spins. On Frontier-CS, AREX-2 takes the lead as the budget grows, reaching 54.4 after one hour, 65.9 after two hours, and 70.7 after five hours. The comparison models plateau much earlier: DeepSeek-V4-Pro stops improving at 44.7 after two hours, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash at 39.1 after three hours. AREX-2 keeps gaining, adding 4.8 points between the second and the fifth hour, of which 2.2 come in the final hour. On BrowseComp, accuracy rises from 64.8 at 47 turns to 84.0 at 143 turns, with diminishing returns. The prior AREX model at 122B also improves with more turns, but far less efficiently: at a similar budget of about 140 turns, AREX-2 at 27B is more than 12 points ahead. That is a 27B model, less than a quarter of the size of AREX, outperforming it at matched compute.

The ablation decomposes where the gain comes from, and it is clean. They run four stages, M0 through M3, all starting from the Qwen3.8-27B base. M0 has no skills, M1 adds general skills, and the later stages add task-specific skills with more rounds. The base model alone scores 28.8. Adding general skills, no training at all, raises it to 41.2. Adding task-specific skills with more rounds, still no training, raises it to 68.2. So skills plus budget, without any weight updates, get most of the way. Training then adds on top: AREX-2 reaches 75.8 with the base number of rounds and 81.8 with more rounds. The paper's reading is that the long-horizon reflective data teaches the model two things at once: how to use skills, and how to keep improving as the number of rounds grows. The trained model turns more rounds into further improvement, gaining 6.0 points from M3 to M4, which is the scaling behavior you would want to see transferred.

A practical detail on the data side. The paper chooses machine learning engineering and algorithmic programming for a stated reason: both domains offer verifiable feedback, so an improvement trajectory can be selected by actual score deltas rather than by a judge's opinion, and both reward sustained iteration over a single attempt. That verifiability is what makes the trajectory selection step, the filtering of rollouts down to those showing real sustained improvement, well defined. The selection criterion is the load-bearing part of the pipeline, since training on flat or regressing rollouts would teach the opposite behavior, and the released trajectories on Hugging Face are the artifact to inspect first when deciding whether the mixture generalizes. The deep-research transfer, by contrast, has no verifiable feedback at training time, which is exactly why the transfer result is the interesting one.

The evaluation detail worth noting for reproducibility: AREX-2 is evaluated on MLE-Lite with skills in its context, and the paper reports how much of the score the skills contribute separately from the training. The compute budget for the long-horizon runs is up to 12 hours per task, which is not something you can casually reproduce, and it means the headline scores are conditioned on a generous wall-clock budget.

What I would trust here. The decomposition of self-improvement into reflection and long-horizon execution is a useful formalization, and the scaling-with-budget curves are the strongest evidence in the paper: an agent that keeps improving at hour four and five is doing something structurally different from one that plateaus at hour two. The 12-point advantage of the 27B model over the 122B predecessor at matched turn budgets, with the same deep-research data, isolates the contribution of the new long-horizon reflective trajectories fairly cleanly. The transfer numbers, while impressive, are in the same spirit as the original AREX paper, and the model weights are released on Hugging Face, which lets you check the claims yourself.

What I would re-verify before adopting this. The training data is synthesized from two domains with verifiable feedback, and the transfer claim depends on the hypothesis that reflection is domain-agnostic. The BrowseComp and HLE numbers are close to other systems in the table, within 1 to 4 points of DeepSeek-Pro, GPT-5.6 Luna, and Iris-mini on BrowseComp, so the transfer gains are real but thin at the top end. The MLE-bench Lite comparison table mixes closed and open models with different harnesses, and the 9.1-point margin over GPT-5.6 Sol should be read against harness differences rather than taken as a pure model delta. The 12-hour-per-task budget means the long-horizon result is a compute result as much as a capability result, and I would want the per-hour cost of the 27B model compared against the per-hour cost of the larger baselines before drawing efficiency conclusions. The environments used to synthesize the trajectories are described but the full environment suite and the trajectory selection criteria are the parts I would want to see in detail before reproducing the data pipeline, since selection bias in the training trajectories could explain a meaningful fraction of the long-horizon improvement.

Bottom line. AREX-2 is the most direct evidence yet that long-horizon reflective data, synthesized in domains with verifiable feedback, teaches an agent to keep improving over many rounds rather than just to improve once. The 27B model beating a 122B predecessor at matched budgets is the result to remember, and the caveat is that the transfer margins at the top of the leaderboards are thin enough that the scaling-with-budget evidence, not the leaderboard positions, is what you should lean on.
