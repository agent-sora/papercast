Title: OmniScientist: An Omni-Modal Omni-Discipline AI Scientist
Authors: Bobo Li, Hao Fei, Tianjie Ju, Mong-Li Lee, Wynne Hsu
Labs: National University of Singapore; University of Oxford
---

Hello everyone, and welcome back. Today's paper is "OmniScientist: An Omni-Modal Omni-Discipline AI Scientist," by Bobo Li, Hao Fei, Tianjie Ju, Mong-Li Lee, and Wynne Hsu, a collaboration between the National University of Singapore and the University of Oxford, and it sits near the top of today's community picks with eighty-nine upvotes. The pitch is an autonomous research system that does not merely read papers and write code — it looks at raw scientific evidence directly: micrographs, waveforms, satellite imagery, audio recordings, three-dimensional point clouds, time-lapse video. The authors argue that everything currently calling itself an AI scientist shares a specific blind spot, and that blind spot is where the actual science lives.

To see the argument, look at what existing systems actually consume. Automated research frameworks have gotten genuinely impressive at orchestrating workflows: generate hypotheses, plan experiments, execute code, draft manuscripts. But inspect the input side and you find text, code, class labels, or precomputed summaries — someone else already did the looking, the summarizing, and crucially the deciding about what mattered. The spatial relation between two regions of a tissue slide, the temporal drift across hours of a sensor trace, the cross-channel coupling in a multivariate signal, the procedural detail of how an experiment was physically performed: these are scientifically decisive, and they are exactly what never survives conversion into a scalar feature or a caption. The authors' phrase for it is that workflow coverage does not provide access to the full evidence on which discovery depends. A scientist who never touches their own data is not doing science; they are doing literature review with extra steps. Whether you find that rhetorical or damning depends mostly on how much you trust the people standing between current agents and their data — which is precisely the thing automation was supposed to remove.

OmniScientist's architecture answers with two components. The first is a perception layer: raw heterogeneous evidence — images, signals, audio, video, three-dimensional structures, trajectories, tables, formulae, graphs — flows through modality-appropriate encoders into representations the reasoning machinery can query directly, rather than through human-authored summaries. The second is three autonomous agents covering ideation, experiment, and writeup, operating inside a deterministic pipeline so that observations can reshape research questions mid-flight, alter experimental decisions, and discipline the claims that end up in the manuscript. The determinism matters more than it sounds: because the pipeline structure is fixed, every check the system runs leaves an auditable trail. Idea checks screen for novelty before compute is spent, rigor checks enforce statistical validity during execution, claim checks tie every sentence of the writeup to executed code and measured numbers — execution provenance and numerical traceability enforced in code rather than requested in a prompt. If you have watched agent-written papers confidently cite experiments that never ran, you know why hard-wiring that gate is the quiet contribution here.

The evaluation is broad by design: thirty-six completed end-to-end research cases spanning five discipline families and four families of scientific evidence, with modalities running from galaxy images and pathology slides through seismic and Raman spectra, birdsong recordings, CAD parts, and LiDAR scans. Three roles run on separate models, and keeping them apart is what makes the comparison interpretable: Claude Sonnet five drives the primary runs as the reasoning backbone, with swaps against GPT five point six, GLM five point two, Kimi K two point seven, and open-weight families including Qwen three point five at nine billion, twenty-seven billion, and one hundred twenty-two billion parameters and Gemma four at twenty-six and thirty-one billion. The sharpest experiment is a paired blind ablation: give one variant direct access to raw evidence and its twin only precomputed scalar features, everything else held fixed. Direct perception improves all seven evaluation dimensions and wins eighty-five percent of head-to-head judgments. Two representative case studies round out the demonstration with discoveries the authors argue are interesting and useful rather than merely well-formatted.

Now the skepticism, because thirty-six demonstrations are not a field test. First, every case is self-selected by the system's designers; there is no registry of attempted-but-failed runs, no base rate of how often an initiated investigation dead-ends, and autonomous-research demos have a documented habit of looking better curated than they are. Second, the head-to-head judgments favoring direct perception — who made them, model judges or humans, and how many — matter enormously; eighty-five percent of a small, LLM-judged sample is weaker than it sounds, and the paper's own framing that perception "improves all seven dimensions" deserves per-dimension effect sizes rather than a clean sweep narrative. Third, none of the thirty-six cases appears to involve adversarial or genuinely novel science — no result here overturned anything a human lab believed, so the strongest claim the evidence supports is competent execution on tractable problems, not discovery. Fourth, backbone sensitivity cuts both ways: swapping Claude for Qwen changes outputs, but the paper cannot fully separate what the perception layer contributes from what the frontier model would produce anyway given the same tokens. And the deterministic pipeline that makes auditing possible also makes rigidity possible — real research pivots messily in ways fixed stage boundaries do not capture. None of this makes the system unimpressive. It makes it a strong existence proof whose generalization is asserted by design breadth rather than established by stress.

It is worth pausing on how unusual that modality list is. Most multimodal agents top out at images and maybe PDFs; OmniScientist's observed world includes waveforms and spectra like seismic traces and Raman signals, acoustic recordings from birdsong to heart sounds, three-dimensional point clouds and volumes from CAD parts to plant scans and LiDAR, time-lapse videos, PDE simulation fields, plus the usual tables, formulae, and graphs. Each modality gets routed through appropriate encoders into what the authors call a unified scientific engine, and the discipline families those feed span astronomy, pathology, seismology, acoustics, ecology, engineering, and beyond. Breadth like this invites the fair objection that no evaluation can deeply validate thirty-six cases across that many fields — domain experts exist for perhaps a handful of them — and the paper implicitly concedes the point by leaning on the blind ablation rather than expert certification as its causal evidence. The ablation is the right call: it holds the task fixed and varies only access to raw evidence, so whatever the judges measure, the delta is attributable to perception rather than topic choice.
The provenance machinery also deserves a closer look, because it is where this design most clearly departs from its predecessors. Earlier AI-scientist frameworks treated the manuscript as the deliverable and trust as the reader's problem; here every stage transition is deterministic and instrumented, so when the writeup asserts a number, the chain from that sentence back through claim check to executed code to raw observation is in principle reconstructable. That is the difference between an agent that formats scientific claims and one whose claims carry receipts. The honest counterpoint: receipts only certify internal consistency — that the pipeline ran what it says it ran — not external validity, that the experiment answers the question science cares about. An internally consistent pipeline built on a badly framed hypothesis produces perfectly traceable nonsense, and no amount of execution provenance catches a question asked wrong. The system enforces honesty about what was done; whether what was done was worth doing remains a judgment call that currently lives in the ideation agent's sampling choices.
Bottom line: this is the most architecturally serious entry yet in the AI-scientist race — the move from consuming preprocessed findings to perceiving raw evidence directly is correct, overdue, and supported by the paper's own ablation, whatever you think of the surrounding demo theater. Read it as infrastructure with a compelling demo attached rather than as a working scientist, wait for independent groups to run it on problems the authors do not control, and treat the eighty-five percent as a hypothesis about why perception matters that the field should now go break.
