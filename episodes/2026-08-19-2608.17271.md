ASI-Bench: At the Dawn of Artificial Superintelligence. Core authors Junwei Zhou, Zhen Sun, and Binyu Li, from a large collaboration spanning Tsinghua University, the University of Michigan, MIT, Harvard University, Carnegie Mellon University, and several other institutions. The title oversells it; the benchmark does not. What this paper actually delivers is a controlled measurement of how fast frontier agents fall apart when you withdraw human methodological guidance, and that measurement is worth your time.

The premise: today's AI mostly learns, compresses, and applies existing human knowledge. Benchmarks test whether systems produce correct answers from learned knowledge or complete tasks under heavy human specification. Nobody jointly measures innovative exploration—choosing how to attack an open research problem—and autonomous scientific execution across general domains, within one project, with guidance progressively removed. Existing options each cover a slice: Humanity's Last Exam is academic question answering; Terminal-Bench is terminal tasks; ScienceAgentBench is scientific analysis under largely fixed workflows; PaperBench is research replication; MLE-Bench is machine-learning engineering; RE-Bench is AI R&D; DiscoveryBench and SciCode cover data-driven discovery and scientific coding. All provide a fixed task specification. None asks whether the agent could have constructed the workflow itself, none spans general scientific domains at project level, and none varies guidance within the same research problem—so a high score can mean genuine discovery or faithful procedure-following, and the two are indistinguishable in the aggregate.

ASI-Bench contains 60 project-level research tasks across 11 scientific domains: mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, astronomy, materials science, earth science, medicine and biostatistics, computer science, robotics, and electrical engineering. Each task is a genuine investigation requiring problem understanding, method selection, implementation, experimentation, failure diagnosis, iterative refinement, and validated artifacts—verifiable research outputs rather than multiple-choice answers. Agents must handle interdependent open-ended decision points: choosing among alternative methods, interpreting intermediate results, recovering from failed attempts, and revising later actions accordingly. Across all 60 tasks that means more than 2,600 interaction turns, 2,400 execution steps, and over 35 hours of agent execution per full pass. Construction was not cheap: more than 1,300 candidate ideas screened from traceable scientific sources, five review rounds, over 1,100 review assignments, over 2,000 task revisions, more than 1,500 sandbox validation runs, and upwards of 31,000 human hours from over 40 experts. Review covered scientific logic, task specification, information leakage, unintended shortcuts, reference artifacts, and scoring code; every retained task passed end-to-end sandbox execution verifying runtime stability, reference reproducibility, artifact generation, and scoring consistency.

The instrument is a four-level guidance gradient applied to the same project. B1 provides complete methodological guidance—background, method, equations, procedural steps; the agent implements a prescribed approach. B2 removes the procedure but names the method class and suitable numerical approaches; the agent must translate method into working solution. B3 removes methodological guidance entirely: only observed data, the scientific objective, and required outputs—the agent must infer the underlying model, choose numerical methods, implement, and validate. A concrete appendix example runs one nonlinear two-dimensional dynamical-system task through all four levels: in B1 the governing PDE, numerical formulation, and solver procedure are given outright; in B2 only the PDE class and candidate approaches remain; in B3 the agent receives spatio-temporal observations alone and must recover the model, select a scheme, implement it, and validate predictions. B4 keeps B3 and adds plausible but irrelevant information to test distraction robustness. Research objective, data, required artifacts, and evaluation criteria stay fixed across levels, which is what makes the gradient interpretable rather than four different benchmarks in a trenchcoat.

Evaluation uses gates and weighted scorers over required scientific outputs, macro-averaged over tasks and three independent runs per configuration unless flagged otherwise. Contributors submit each task with a scientific question, four prompt variants covering B1 through B4, task data, a reference-generation script named generate_gt.py, defined evaluation gates, and weighted scoring criteria; local testing records execution time, environment provenance, and scorer-replay evidence before reviewers examine formulation, gradient construction, reference generation, scoring logic, and runtime. Eighteen state-of-the-art agent-model configurations were evaluated, pairing harnesses like Codex CLI, Claude Code, Kimi Code, and MiMo Code with models including GPT-5.5, GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 4.8, GLM-5.3, GLM-5.2, DeepSeek V4 Flash and Pro, Kimi K3 and K2.7, and MiMo V2.5 Pro—all without external tool access on the main table, so scores reflect model-plus-harness reasoning and execution only.

The numbers. Average score across all 18 configurations drops from 50.91 under full guidance in B1 to 29.10 in B2 when only the method is named, then to 26.62 in B3 when agents must pick the method themselves. Decompose that: removing the detailed procedure costs 21.82 points; removing the method itself costs only 2.48 more. That asymmetry is the paper's central finding. The bottleneck is not method selection—it is operationalizing a method into a complete, correct research pipeline. Distractors barely matter: B4 scores 26.99 against B3's 26.62. Current systems fail at doing, not choosing.

The per-model table reinforces the pattern rather than softening it. GLM-5.3 falls from 63.05 in B1 to 33.09 in B3; Claude Opus 4.8 from 52.48 to 31.73; Kimi K3 from 56.16 to 28.15; DeepSeek V4 Flash from 51.85 to the mid-twenties. No configuration escapes the cliff, and the B2-minus-B1 column is negative for every single row—typically minus sixteen to minus twenty-eight points—while B4-minus-B3 wobbles within roughly plus or minus three points around zero. Guidance dependence is universal across labs and scales; distraction robustness is already solved by comparison.

Individual spread is wide. GPT-5.6 Sol at ultra compute reaches 71.78 in B1 and 51.60 in B3, the best B3 score, at roughly 1,550 dollars per run; GPT-5.6 Sol at xhigh manages 62.75 falling to 40.86. DeepSeek V4 Pro drops from 42.97 to 19.20. Harness effects are real and uneven: MiMo V2.5 Pro improves from 16.17 with its native MiMo Code harness to 23.25 under Claude Code, and Kimi K2.7 rises from 19.72 to 27.34, yet Kimi K3 barely moves between Kimi Code and Claude Code at 36.22 versus 37.09. Capability lives in the model-harness pair, not the model alone—a caution for anyone reporting model-only numbers.

Cost data deserve attention because they undercut a comfortable assumption. B2 consumes 6.9 million tokens per task against B1's 4.4 million—59 percent more—and needs 49.7 minutes of average execution time versus 37.8, even though B2 tells the agent which method to use. B3 lands in between at 5.4 million tokens and 45.9 minutes. Naming the method without the procedure leaves the agent exploring more, not less. Cost-to-score is roughly linear across configurations with no bargain tier visible: Codex paired with GPT-5.6 xhigh reaches a B3 score near the high thirties at a few hundred dollars per run, while the ultra-compute tier buys its leading 51.60 only at roughly 1,550 dollars per run.

Now the skeptical assessment, which for a benchmark means auditing the measurement itself. Scoring relies on contributor-written reference-generation scripts plus gates and weighted scorers; the authors report scorer validation, sandbox replay checks, and cross-review covering leakage, but judge-model dependence is inherent to scoring open-ended research artifacts at this scale, and agreement statistics live in appendices rather than headline claims. Sixty tasks is small; domain-level cuts will be noisy, so treat per-domain rankings as provisional. Three runs per configuration helps, and reported standard deviations mostly run one to five points—GPT-5.5 shows plus or minus 5.74 in B1—but the B1-to-B3 gap dwarfs them, so the main conclusion is robust to run variance even where individual rankings are not. One configuration result comes from a single run and is flagged as such. The name invites ridicule and the framing invites hype; ignore both. What remains is a carefully built instrument showing a 24-point collapse between following instructions and conducting research unaided.

For teams building research agents: use this to localize failure. The design distinguishes insufficient knowledge, method misselection, tool-execution errors, result misinterpretation, error-correction failures, and instability—the diagnostic breakdown you need before spending compute. Because the same project appears at all four guidance levels with fixed data and criteria, you can measure whether your system's gains come from better execution of given methods or from genuine autonomous method construction—two very different claims that aggregate benchmarks conflate. The benchmark is open with a public submission pipeline at the project site, so teams can contribute tasks or test systems directly. Re-verify any single-domain strength claim on your own tasks first, and read the scoring code before comparing your system's score against anything published elsewhere.

Bottom line: ASI-Bench is a serious measurement of guidance dependence dressed in grandiose branding, and its finding—that current systems are bottlenecked on executing methods rather than selecting them—should redirect agent engineering effort accordingly. Skip the superintelligence framing; keep the B1-through-B4 gradient.
