ASI-Bench: At the Dawn of Artificial Superintelligence. Core authors Junwei Zhou, Zhen Sun, and Binyu Li, from a large collaboration spanning Tsinghua University, the University of Michigan, MIT, Harvard University, Carnegie Mellon University, and several other institutions. The title oversells it; the benchmark does not. What this paper actually delivers is a controlled measurement of how fast frontier agents fall apart when you withdraw human methodological guidance, and that measurement is worth your time.

The premise: today's AI mostly learns, compresses, and applies existing human knowledge. Benchmarks test whether systems produce correct answers from learned knowledge or complete tasks under heavy human specification. Nobody jointly measures innovative exploration—choosing how to attack an open research problem—and autonomous scientific execution across general domains, within one project. Existing options each cover a slice: Humanity's Last Exam is academic question answering; Terminal-Bench is terminal tasks; ScienceAgentBench is scientific analysis with fixed workflows; PaperBench is replication; MLE-Bench and RE-Bench are machine-learning engineering and R&D. All provide a fixed task specification. None asks whether the agent could have constructed the workflow itself.

ASI-Bench contains 60 project-level research tasks across 11 scientific domains: mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, astronomy, materials science, earth science, medicine and biostatistics, computer science, robotics, and electrical engineering. Each task is a genuine investigation requiring problem understanding, method selection, implementation, experimentation, failure diagnosis, iterative refinement, and validated artifacts. Across all 60 tasks that means more than 2,600 interaction turns, 2,400 execution steps, and over 35 hours of agent execution. Construction was not cheap: more than 1,300 candidate ideas screened down, five review rounds, over 1,100 review assignments, over 2,000 revisions, more than 1,500 sandbox validation runs, and upwards of 31,000 human hours from over 40 experts. Every retained task passed end-to-end sandbox execution verifying reference reproducibility, artifact generation, and scoring consistency.

The instrument is a four-level guidance gradient applied to the same project. B1 provides complete methodological guidance—background, method, equations, procedural steps; the agent implements a prescribed approach. B2 removes the procedure but names the method class; the agent must translate method into working solution. B3 removes methodological guidance entirely: only observed data, the scientific objective, and required outputs—the agent must infer the underlying model, choose numerical methods, implement, and validate. B4 keeps B3 and adds plausible but irrelevant information to test distraction robustness. Research objective, data, and evaluation criteria stay fixed across all four levels, which is what makes the gradient interpretable.

Evaluation uses gates and weighted scorers over required scientific outputs, macro-averaged over three independent runs per configuration. Eighteen state-of-the-art agent-model configurations were evaluated, pairing harnesses like Codex CLI, Claude Code, Kimi Code, and MiMo Code with models including GPT-5.5, GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 4.8, GLM-5.3, GLM-5.2, DeepSeek V4 Flash and Pro, Kimi K3 and K2.7, and MiMo V2.5 Pro.

The numbers. Average score across all 18 configurations drops from 50.91 under full guidance in B1 to 29.10 in B2 when only the method is named, then to 26.62 in B3 when agents must pick the method themselves. Decompose that: removing the detailed procedure costs 21.82 points; removing the method itself costs only 2.48 more. That asymmetry is the paper's central finding. The bottleneck is not method selection—it is operationalizing a method into a complete, correct research pipeline. Distractors barely matter: B4 scores 26.99 against B3's 26.62. Current systems fail at doing, not choosing.

Individual spread is wide. GPT-5.6 Sol at ultra compute reaches 71.78 in B1 and 51.60 in B3, the best B3 score, at roughly 1,550 dollars per run. DeepSeek V4 Pro manages 42.97 dropping to 19.20. Harness effects are real and uneven: MiMo V2.5 Pro improves from 16.17 with its native MiMo Code harness to 23.25 under Claude Code, and Kimi K2.7 rises from 19.72 to 27.34, yet Kimi K3 barely moves between Kimi Code and Claude Code at 36.22 versus 37.09. Capability lives in the model-harness pair, not the model alone—a caution for anyone reporting model-only numbers.

Cost data deserve attention because they undercut a comfortable assumption. B2 consumes 6.9 million tokens per task against B1's 4.4 million—59 percent more—and 22 percent more wall-clock time, even though B2 tells the agent which method to use. Naming the method without the procedure leaves the agent exploring more, not less. Cost-to-score is roughly linear across configurations with no bargain tier visible.

Now the skeptical assessment, which for a benchmark means auditing the measurement itself. Scoring relies on reference-generation scripts plus gates and weighted scorers defined by contributors; the authors report scorer validation and four rounds of cross-review covering leakage and shortcuts, but judge-model dependence is inherent to scoring open-ended research artifacts at this scale, and inter-scorer agreement figures live in the appendix rather than headline claims. Sixty tasks is small; domain-level cuts will be noisy, so treat per-domain rankings as provisional. Three runs per configuration helps, and reported standard deviations are mostly one to five points, but the B1-to-B3 gap dwarfs them, so the main conclusion is robust to run variance. The name invites mockery and the framing invites hype; ignore both. What remains is a carefully built instrument showing a 24-point collapse between following instructions and conducting research unaided.

For teams building research agents: use this to localize failure. The design distinguishes insufficient knowledge, method misselection, tool-execution errors, result misinterpretation, and instability, which is precisely the diagnostic breakdown you need before spending compute. Re-verify any single-domain strength claim on your own tasks first, and read the scoring code before comparing your system's score against anything published elsewhere.

Bottom line: ASI-Bench is a serious measurement of guidance dependence dressed in grandiose branding, and its finding—that current systems are bottlenecked on executing methods rather than selecting them—should redirect agent engineering effort accordingly. Skip the superintelligence framing; keep the B1-through-B4 gradient.
