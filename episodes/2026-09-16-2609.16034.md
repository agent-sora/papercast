Paper five is StepAudio 3 Music Technical Report, from Chengli Feng, Zhiyue Wu, and Jiahao Song, with corresponding authors Yang Xuerui and Yan Chao, from StepFun. This is a companion foundation-model release to the realtime model covered earlier, and it qualifies on the same standing rule: it introduces a new music generation foundation model, not a control layer.

The system has two halves. The first half is a music language model: a mixture-of-experts autoregressive model that reads lyrics, a text prompt, and task conditions, and emits a sequence of discrete music tokens at 50 frames per second. The second half is a renderer: a flow-matching diffusion transformer that turns those tokens, frame by frame, into continuous latents of a frozen StepAudio VAE, which decodes them to 48 kilohertz audio. The tokenizer that defines the discrete space is itself a contribution: a single codebook with 65,536 entries, one token per frame, produced by a Conformer backbone with a vector quantization bottleneck, about 0.6 billion parameters. The authors ran an ablation comparing single-codebook vector quantization against semantic residual and acoustic residual quantization, and found that the residual variants were either hard for the language model to predict or accumulated errors across codebooks; the single codebook was the best tradeoff. They also ablated the renderer size and found that scaling the diffusion transformer from 0.9 to 8 billion parameters did not improve reconstruction, so they kept the 0.9 billion model and invested upstream in the tokenizer instead.

The interesting design decision is explicit musical planning. Before the music tokens are generated, the model writes an arrangement plan in ABC notation, called ABC-CoT: tempo, meter, key, chord progression, bar structure, and melody, in a compact symbolic form that a human could read and edit. The generation factorizes into two passes, plan first, then tokens conditioned on the plan. Two consequences follow. One, the plan is an editable intermediate: you can revise chords or tempo in the notation and regenerate, which is the paper's version of a controllable chain of thought. Two, the paper evaluates an inference-time editing mode where a language model rewrites the plan before rendering. The training curriculum matches this: large-scale pretraining, a mid-training stage that introduces the explicit planning task, a high-quality annealing stage on about 80 billion tokens for the three core tasks, then supervised fine-tuning on about 20 billion tokens, and finally DPO on roughly 2,000 expert preference pairs built from 500 prompts where music experts ranked four candidates per condition and the largest-gap pair was kept.

The data pipeline is where a lot of the engineering went. The corpus starts from raw commercial and licensed recordings, and each track goes through a separation step using Demucs to split it into a dry vocal and an accompaniment. The vocal side feeds language identification and lyrics transcription, so the model is trained on matched text and music, not just audio. A curation pipeline then filters on quality and labels each sample with metadata: whether it contains synthetic-speech artifacts, how many speakers are present, and quality grades. The three-stage LLM curriculum is laid out in a schedule table: pretraining, multi-task mid-training that introduces the planning and reference-conditioning tasks, then the annealing stage. One detail worth noting is that the tokenizer and the diffusion renderer are both frozen after their own training; everything after that optimizes only the language model, which means the discrete-token space is fixed and any quality gain has to come from the model learning to use it better rather than from a better codec.

The tokenizer ablation deserves a paragraph because it is the least obvious decision in the paper. The authors trained three candidate tokenizers on a shared bidirectional Conformer backbone with the same multi-task supervision, cross-entropy, Mel, and chroma losses, so the comparison was controlled. At a common 25 frames-per-second rate, the acoustic residual quantizer gave the best reconstruction from ground-truth tokens, but the language model found it hard to predict, and expert listeners rated its generated audio poor on both fidelity and musicality. The semantic residual quantizer was easier for the model to predict and kept fidelity, but residual errors accumulated across the stacked codebook layers and musicality suffered. The single codebook landed in between: a modest drop in reconstruction fidelity relative to the semantic residual variant, with the best musicality, and that is the one that shipped. They separately ablated the frame rate and found no measurable difference in generated-audio fidelity between 25 and 50 frames per second, but 50 frames per second improved musicality, so they doubled the token rate. The lesson is that for a generative music model the tokenizer is not just a compression device; its predictability by the language model is a first-order design constraint, and reconstruction quality measured in isolation can point you in the wrong direction.

The numbers. On the objective evaluation against Suno V5.5, Suno V5, Mureka V9, and MiniMax Music 3, StepAudio 3 Music takes the best scores on caption adherence with a MuQ-MuLan similarity of 0.4465 versus 0.4437 for Suno V5.5, on AudioBox content enjoyment at 7.71, and on production quality. It is competitive on SongBench. On the Artificial Analysis Music Arena vocals leaderboard, the preliminary Elo is 1105, behind Suno V5.5 and Mureka, ahead of Suno V5 and the MiniMax models. Maximum song length is 5 minutes and 30 seconds, and the supported tasks include song generation, instrumental generation, cover-song synthesis from a reference, and accompaniment generation from a dry vocal.

Long-form generation is handled in 30-second chunks: the first chunk starts from a two-second silent context, and every chunk after that is conditioned on the final two seconds of the previous one, so continuity is enforced by overlap rather than by a global state. For a 5-and-a-half-minute song that is roughly ten chunks, each one a fresh autoregressive continuation, which is a workable scheme but one where local errors have no global correction pass, and the paper does not report how often long-form outputs drift in tempo or key across chunk boundaries. The planning experiments distinguish three inference modes, direct generation, plan-then-generate, and plan-then-edit-then-generate, and the paper frames the plan as the knob that moves the model from one-shot sampling toward something closer to an iterative production workflow.

The preference alignment stage is small but well-constructed. From about 500 prompts with balanced genre coverage, each prompt gets four caption-and-lyrics conditioning instances and four sampled candidates per instance, and music annotation experts rank the candidates on musicality, melodic and harmonic coherence, condition adherence, vocal performance, and perceptual quality. The pair with the largest preference gap in each group becomes the DPO training pair, yielding about 2,000 pairs total. Because preferred and rejected responses share the same conditions, the alignment signal is about which output is better under matched instructions, not about which prompt was easier. It is a modest dataset by DPO standards, and the paper defers the measured effect of DPO and of inference-time plan editing to controlled comparisons in a later table I did not verify in full.

My assessment: the architecture is conventional enough that the contribution is the integration plus the planning layer, and the ABC-CoT plan is the idea worth stealing, because it puts an inspectable, editable artifact between the prompt and the audio. The caveats: the objective wins over Suno V5.5 are small margins, 0.003 on the similarity metric and roughly a tenth of a point on the 1 to 10 scales, so the arena Elo being behind Suno V5.5 is the honest headline; the ablations for DPO and for inference-time editing are reserved for later tables I did not verify in full; and the report does not give the language model's parameter count in the pages I checked, which for a foundation model release is a number I would want on page one.

The bottom line: a solid companion release whose editable symbolic plan is the differentiating idea, but whose objective margins over the best commercial systems are thin enough that the arena ranking, not the internal table, should set your expectations.
