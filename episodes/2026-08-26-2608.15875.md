GigaBrain-0.7: Scaling Embodied Foundation Models to Emergent Capabilities with a Three-System Architecture, by Angen Ye, Axiang Sun, and Can Jin — the first three names of a large alphabetically ordered team at GigaAI. The paper describes an embodied foundation model built as three cooperating systems: a vision-language planner, a flow-matching action generator, and a world-model-based predictor that supplies imagined subgoal images and value signals to the controller. The team pretrained it on roughly thirty-seven thousand hours of robot and human manipulation data plus about two hundred seventy-two million vision-language samples, and argues that scale alone starts producing reliable behaviors that small models do not show.

The framing question is worth restating because the whole paper hangs on it. Vision-language-action models have become the default recipe for generalist robots, but most systems are trained and evaluated at modest data scale on narrow task suites. The authors ask two things at once: does architectural design still matter once you scale heterogeneous pretraining data, and does real-robot competence improve smoothly or discontinuously as data grows. Their answer is that both hold: architecture choices change what scaling is worth, and several capabilities they care about — deformable-object folding, sustained contact tasks — barely work at small data fractions and become dependable only near full scale.

The architecture splits responsibilities cleanly. System 2 is a PaliGemma2 model with three billion parameters that reads the current image and the task instruction, produces a short chain-of-thought description of the scene, and emits one concrete subtask, for example turning "hang the item on the hanger" into "pick up the red hat". System 1 is the controller: another PaliGemma2 backbone paired with a dedicated action expert of half a billion parameters in a Mixture-of-Transformers layout, generating continuous action chunks by flow matching. The two streams keep separate feed-forward weights; the vision-language stream attends causally over its own tokens while the action expert attends bidirectionally over the concatenation of both streams, which grounds control in full semantic context without letting action gradients contaminate the language pathway directly. Region grounding and spatial-layout supervision get extra weight in the vision-language mixture; captions and free-form descriptions dominate the rest. Gradient coupling goes through Soft Knowledge Insulation: instead of blocking action gradients into the vision-language backbone entirely, the method attenuates them, which preserves captioning and question-answering ability during long robot-data training runs.

System 3 is the differentiator. It is an independent five-billion-parameter world value model based on GigaWorld-1 that exposes two signals to the controller. First, conditioned on the current observation, robot state, and the planner's subtask, it imagines a short video aligned with the subtask horizon; the last frame becomes a subgoal image fed to System 1 as an extra visual condition. Notably the prediction horizon extends past the length of one action chunk, which the ablations show matters on complex tasks. Second, the same backbone estimates a scalar progress value, collapsed into a binary advantage token placed in the controller's prompt — essentially "progress is increasing" or "not increasing" for the current segment. At inference nothing scores candidate actions; the policy simply runs conditioned on positive progress.

Training data engineering occupies a third of the paper, and it is the part an implementation team should read closely. The trajectory corpus totals 37,256.98 hours after cleaning: 55.12 percent real robot across sixteen platforms, 22.15 percent handheld Universal Manipulation Interface demonstrations, 7.68 percent egocentric human video, 3.90 percent simulation, and 11.15 percent generated by their world models. Everything converts to LeRobot version 3.0 with a canonical state-action dimension ordering — left arm, right arm, head, waist, base — and missing degrees of freedom masked rather than modeled separately. Cleaning has four stages: first and ninety-ninth percentile outlier clipping per robot type and action dimension; removal of stationary segments by normalized state-change magnitude; end-effector pose standardization against each robot's kinematic description; and finally loss-guided filtering, where trajectories producing persistently abnormal training losses get traced back to source video and annotations and dropped. Instructions are rewritten into a canonical format with an LLM and annotated with subtask boundaries, with human spot checks behind the automation.

Do the scaling claims survive scrutiny? Partly. On the optimization side the evidence is clean: validation loss falls steadily as the data fraction grows from twelve and a half to one hundred percent, with curves still separating at two hundred thousand steps. On behavior, fruit picking improves smoothly with scale while chaotic clothes folding climbs steeply only at larger fractions, which is the paper's evidence for emergence — though this is a two-task observation, not a systematic capability curve. Source-mixing experiments start from three thousand hours of robot data and add equal amounts of human handheld and egocentric data; either addition helps and the combination is best, and the benefit survives task-specific post-training, suggesting the human data changes the prior rather than the immediate policy.

The backbone study produced the counterintuitive result. Among PaliGemma2, Qwen3.5, and Gemma 4 candidates evaluated on real robots, the largest model was not best overall: Gemma 4 at eight and a half billion parameters hit one hundred percent on desk cleaning and ninety-two on fruit picking but zero percent on shirt folding; PaliGemma2 was the only backbone with nonzero folding success at thirty percent. Coupling mattered as much: restricting cross-attention to the backbone's final layer cut inference latency from zero point two two one seconds to zero point zero seven three but collapsed folding to zero percent, while the dual-stream design won every task. The reported trade-off is honest about cost.

System 3's ablations are the strongest controlled evidence. On clothes folding all variants reach one hundred percent success, yet average score rises from sixty-eight point three to eighty-eight point three percent and completion time drops from one hundred seven to seventy-five seconds as prediction and value conditioning stack. Gift wrapping shows the base policy failing outright — zero percent — while subgoal images alone give twenty percent, value alone sixty, and both together eighty, with completion time falling to fifty-five seconds. Cube sorting is messier: value conditioning alone lags image conditioning on success, forty-five versus fifty percent, while the combination tops out at fifty-five percent. Read generously, imagination helps most exactly where closed-loop perception is hardest; read skeptically, the binary advantage token is doing heavy lifting for a signal derived from a scalar estimate.

Zero-shot evaluation tempers the foundation-model story. In-distribution, the single pretrained checkpoint averages ninety-four and a half percent on language-following tasks on one platform and fifty-five point eight on their humanoid; out-of-distribution those averages fall to thirty-six point five and sixteen point seven percent respectively, and complex manipulation drops similarly. The authors flag this gap themselves, which is to their credit, but the deployable claim is narrower than the title implies. After task-specific post-training the picture is competitive: average language-following success of ninety-one point five percent against eighty-eight point eight for pi-zero-point-five on the two-armed platform, and eighty-four point two versus a sixty-nine point six predecessor baseline on the humanoid; on complex manipulation it posts eighty-four point nine percent average with clear margins on rice sweeping and playdough kneading. Evaluations run ten to twenty trials per configuration, so single-digit differences deserve replication before belief.

For an adopter, the transferable pieces are the data pipeline — the percentile filtering, stationary removal, loss-based rejection loop, and instruction normalization generalize beyond this lab — the Soft Knowledge Insulation trick, and the observation that cheap final-layer coupling erases precisely the hardest skills. What deserves re-verification before reliance: the emergence narrative rests on two tasks, the world model's contribution is measured mostly on GigaAI's own platforms, and code and weights are promised public but the announcement is the current evidence. The bottom line: this is a credible, well-instrumented demonstration that coordinated planning, imagination, and control scale better together than either alone, with genuinely useful negative results on architecture. Treat the emergent-capability headline as a hypothesis supported by clothes folding, not a law.
