LeWAM: End-to-End World and Action Modeling with JEPAs, by Minghao Fu, Tavis Siebert, and Eryk Halicki, from UC San Diego, ETH Zurich, the University of British Columbia, and Brown University. This is a robotics paper, but it is squarely in the world-model topology lane, because it changes how a latent world model and a policy are put together. The standard JEPA recipe for control is staged: first learn visual representations, then learn action-conditioned dynamics on top of them, and only then bolt on a control policy or an online solver like the cross-entropy method. LeWAM's claim is that this staging is what loses control-relevant information, and that a single end-to-end training stage, jointly learning the representation, the latent dynamics, and a stochastic action policy, is both more accurate and dramatically faster to plan with. The result it leads with is a 32.3 times speedup in planning throughput against the prior JEPA baseline, plus large jumps in success rate on contact-rich manipulation.

The architecture is a Mixture-of-Transformers with two branches over a shared masked self-attention. The encoder is a ResNet-18 with spatial softmax pooling and an MLP, and the same encoder processes history frames, future target frames, and the goal image. The state branch predicts the next latent state at a frameskip of K environment steps from history latents and clean actions, using a learned query token. The action branch is a flow-matching model over action chunks: given a recorded chunk, it interpolates between Gaussian noise and the real chunk with a flow timestep, and regresses the path velocity. Both branches share one attention but keep separate projections per modality, and the masking is carefully chosen: the state query reads history and clean actions, the noisy action tokens read history and their own causal prefix, and history latents attend only to themselves, which blocks clean-action leakage from the dynamics branch into the policy branch. The whole thing is about 17 million parameters, which is small by foundation-model standards and large enough to be taken seriously as a single trainable unit.

The training objective is the sum of three terms. A mean-squared-error loss on next-latent prediction, a mean-squared-error loss on the flow-matching velocity, and a SIGReg distributional regularizer on the latent states, which enforces an isotropic Gaussian constraint using an Epps-Pulley test statistic over random unit directions to prevent collapse without a stop-gradient target encoder. That is the key design point: both the dynamics objective and the action objective train the same shared visual representation in one pass, so the representation is forced to be useful for both predicting consequences and generating actions, rather than being frozen and then post-trained around.

Planning is where the design pays off, and it is a hybrid of sampling and gradient descent that I have not seen in this form before. LeWAM supports direct policy execution, where it integrates the learned velocity field from noise over the unit interval to produce an action chunk and executes the first K actions. But it also plans: it samples a population of N candidate plans from the learned action distribution, each by alternating action sampling and latent dynamics rollouts over a horizon that is a multiple of the frameskip, and then it refines every candidate with a few steps of gradient descent through the frozen latent dynamics, minimizing the cost, which is the mean squared error between the predicted final latent state and the goal embedding, plus a small proximity penalty to discourage large departures from the sampled plans. It picks the lowest-cost iterate, executes the first K actions, and repeats in receding-horizon fashion. The reason this is fast is that the learned policy distribution is a strong prior, so the search starts near viable candidates instead of from a broad Gaussian, and the refinement is a small number of backpropagations through a 17 million parameter model, which parallelizes trivially on a GPU.

The evaluation spans ten heterogeneous simulated navigation and manipulation environments, mixing the four benchmarks from the LeWM baseline with six new contact-rich and long-horizon tasks, trained on a mix of expert demonstrations, random-policy interactions, and mixed-quality data. Against LeWM with a cross-entropy-method planner, LeWAM averages 93.5 percent success across the ten tasks versus 50.9 percent, a 42.7 point gap, winning on every task. The largest gaps are on contact-rich manipulation: ToolHang at 94.7 versus 35.4, and Transport at 83.3 versus 2.8. The contact-rich headline, where success jumps from 28.6 percent to 89.7 percent, is exactly where population-based search struggles, because contact transitions switch the dynamics between modes and a broad sampling prior wastes rollouts in infeasible regions. On long-horizon planning at a 100 step goal offset across four tasks, LeWAM reaches 46.2 percent mean success against 10.9 percent. On the compute side, it plans at 52.5 plans per second against 1.62 for LeWM, the 32.3 times figure, and on robot inference compute efficiency it delivers 3.7 action chunks per teraflop versus 0.28 for a 0.5-scale flow-matching policy baseline, a 13.3 times improvement.

On real hardware, LeWAM is evaluated by direct policy execution on a physical robot for Pick-and-Place-T, Push-T, and Tape Stack, trained on the same teleoperation data as the baselines, Diffusion Policy and a 0.5-scale vision-language-action policy. It is competitive with both, improving success by 16 percentage points on Transport, and its full action chunk costs 270.2 gigaflops against 3,603.6 for the larger baseline, which is the 13.3 times compute-efficiency figure. The interpretability results are a nice touch: linear probes show that adding the action modeling objective improves decodability of physical state, proprioception, and actions by roughly 0.2 in R squared over the dynamics-only model, and the SIGReg ablation is the clearest single result in the paper. Removing the regularizer drops planning success by 25.3 points on Push-T, and the paper shows that without any regularizer the latent-only variant collapses to zero R squared on every probe. The authors' reading is that action supervision acts as an anti-collapse signal because observed actions anchor the representation to targets independent of the encoder, while SIGReg on top of that retains the latent structure planning actually needs.

The skeptical read. First, this is a simulation-heavy result: ten environments, each trained separately, single task, single embodiment, and the authors say so plainly in the limitations. The 93.5 percent average is over a small suite where the method wins everything, which is encouraging but not a generalization claim. Second, the planning speedup is partly a comparison artifact: it is against a cross-entropy-method planner that samples from a broad Gaussian, and the fair question is how it holds up against a well-tuned population-based solver with the same policy prior, which is not the comparison made here. Third, the contact-rich gains are the most interesting and the least transferable, because contact mode switching is exactly where the dataset coverage matters most, and the dataset is limited. What I would trust is the core architectural finding: that jointly training dynamics and a flow-matching policy on a shared representation, with SIGReg, beats the staged JEPA pipeline at both accuracy and planning throughput, and that the 17 million parameter scale makes this cheap to experiment with. If you are building latent world models for control, the MoT masking scheme, the Epps-Pulley regularizer with its stated hyperparameters, and the sample-then-gradient-descent planner are all reproducible from the paper.

The bottom line: LeWAM is the clearest evidence yet that end-to-end joint training of a JEPA world model and a flow-matching policy beats the staged recipe, with a 32 times planning speedup and large contact-rich manipulation gains that follow from the design. I would re-verify the long-horizon and physical-robot numbers on my own tasks before betting a deployment on them, since the suite is small and single-task per environment.
