The paper is Apertus 1.5, From Open Text Models to Open Multimodal Reasoners, a technical report from the Project Apertus team, with core contributions from Yixuan Xu, Yassine Turki, and Vinko Sabolčec, and the work spread across ETH Zurich, EPFL, the Swiss National Supercomputing Centre, the University of Zurich, and the University of Bern, among other public institutions. It is the multimodal extension of the fully open Apertus model family, and it is the kind of foundation-model release the standing coverage rules exist for, a new base model, released openly with its data and training recipes.

The starting point is a philosophy statement that the paper repeats in several places: language models are becoming infrastructure, and infrastructure built in the open, by public institutions, on documented data, is the alternative to dependence on a few providers. That is the framing, and it is also a methodological constraint. Apertus 1.5 is trained only on openly available data, respects crawler opt-outs retroactively, and excludes restrictively licensed material. The whole pipeline, weights, data preparation, training recipes, and evaluation suites, is released under permissive licenses, and the alignment stages are guided by a published charter. If you care about provenance and reproducibility of a base model, that is the pitch, and it is the thing that separates it from a typical open-weight release where the data is a black box.

On the model side, the release extends the Apertus 1.0 text models into multimodal reasoning. They do continued pretraining of the existing 8B and 70B models, on 4 trillion additional tokens for the 8B and 2 trillion for the 70B, and that continued pretraining adds image and audio understanding and extends the context window fourfold to 262,144 tokens. The architecture is unchanged from 1.0, which is a deliberate choice. The long-context extension is done in four steps, from 8,192 to 32,768 to 65,536 to 131,072 and finally 262,144 tokens, each step reinitializing the optimizer states and training on 50 to 63 billion tokens, with the learning rate held at the final cooldown value so the long-context stage is a conservative continuation that adapts the model to longer sequences without destabilizing what it already knows.

The most interesting technical decision is how the new modalities get in. Apertus 1.5 represents images and audio as discrete tokens in a shared vocabulary, rather than using modality-specific encoders whose features are projected into the model. This follows the unified-model line, where a single next-token-prediction objective applies across all modalities. The language-model head stays restricted to text, so the model reads images and audio and writes text, but it does not generate image or audio tokens. The paper is explicit about why they prefer discrete tokens over the encoder-projection approach that most vision-language models use, and about the trade-offs, and it includes ablations on preserving text capability while adding modalities during continued pretraining, which is the failure mode that matters most when you bolt a modality onto a working text model.

The post-training pipeline is the standard four stages, and the report documents each one. Supervised fine-tuning, long-context fine-tuning, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, and preference alignment. There is a detail that stands out: thinking is a flag in the chat template rather than a separate model. Turning the thinking flag on lifts mathematical reasoning by up to 20 points on the 8B model, but the data behind it is not free. They use a capability-filtered reasoning mixture, and when they add reasoning-trace supervision, thinking on adds seven to 18 points on mathematics while costing 12 to 14 points on instruction following, largely because about a quarter of the reasoning traces degenerate into repetition. That is a concrete, quantified trade-off for a design choice a lot of labs are making right now, and it is exactly the kind of number that is usually left out of a technical report.

On the implementation side it is worth knowing how the training is staged, because the report is unusually explicit. The continued-pretraining mixture is dominated by high-quality web text in the early stages, supplemented by mathematics, code, translation, and legal corpora, and the final cooldown stage deliberately trades general web text for reasoning-heavy data, cutting the share of general text and sharply raising the combined allocation to code and mathematics. The document-understanding pipeline has its own quirks worth noting. They originally used perceptual hashing to deduplicate text-rich images, and found it was silently removing valid OCR examples, because distinct document pages share a layout and produce the same hash, so they disabled it for the OCR and document-centric datasets. The caption pipeline regenerates missing or low-information captions with open-weight vision-language models and rejects outputs with token loops, runaway number lists, or repeated endings, which is the kind of data-quality detail that usually never makes a report but is exactly what determines whether a document-understanding benchmark number is real. Among fully open models of its class, the 8B leads on knowledge, instruction following, and cultural benchmarks, and its instruction following surpasses all the compared models, including open-weight ones from larger labs. On the new image and audio understanding, a first for the family, the models are competitive with fully open multimodal models, strongest on document understanding and on long-form and lower-resource speech recognition, while specialized vision and audio models still beat them on perception and on transcription of English and other high-resource languages. That honest concession about where they are not competitive is a good sign in a report that could easily have spun the numbers.

Now the skeptical pass. This is a continued-pretraining result, not a from-scratch one, so the headline comparison is against Apertus 1.0, and the question is how much of the multimodal ability comes from the 2 to 4 trillion continued-pretraining tokens versus from the post-training stages. The report's ablations on modality addition are the right place to look, but a reader should check how much text capability is preserved at each stage, because the 12 to 14 point instruction-following cost from reasoning traces tells you the post-training stages are trading one capability for another, and the net effect on a broad benchmark mix is what actually matters. The discrete-token representation is the more defensible long-term bet, but it is also the one that usually carries a perception tax compared with a strong learned vision encoder, and the report's own admission that specialized vision models beat them on perception confirms that tax is real. The open-data claim is the differentiator, but it is also the hardest to verify from the outside, and the practical value depends on whether the released data preparation is genuinely reproducible or a description of a process that required infrastructure the reader cannot run. I would re-run the instruction-following and document-understanding numbers, the two places the report claims its strongest positions, and check the long-context retention at 262,144 tokens, because context extension is where continued pretraining most often quietly degrades needle-in-haystack performance. One more number to scrutinize is the thinking-flag cost. The report says thinking lifts math by up to 20 points on the 8B but costs 12 to 14 points of instruction following, which means the same weights are behaving like two different models depending on one template flag, and any downstream evaluation that mixes thinking on and off is going to conflate model capability with a decoding choice.

Bottom line: Apertus 1.5 is a fully open, publicly documented multimodal extension of the Apertus family, and it is competitive with the best open multimodal models in its class, especially on document understanding and instruction following. The discrete-token multimodality and the quantified thinking-flag trade-offs are the technical details worth studying, and the honest acknowledgment of where specialized models still win makes the report credible even where its claims are modest.
