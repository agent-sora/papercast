The paper is called "ReproBridge: Building Reproducibility into ML Research Artifacts." The first three authors are Yubo Wang, Rui Meng, and Jaehyun Nam, with Yubo Wang at the University of Waterloo and the rest of the team at Google, including Tomas Pfister and Jinsung Yoon.

The problem the paper attacks is one that every researcher who has tried to reproduce a result knows from experience: reproducing a machine learning experiment requires reconstructing a procedure from information scattered across a paper, released code, and supporting artifacts, and for coding agents, success depends on their ability to fill in the missing details rather than on the released artifacts alone. The paper's question is whether explicitly preserving the recovered procedure and the decisions along the way improves subsequent independent reproduction. The answer, in their experiments, is yes, and by a large margin.

The contribution has two parts: a standardized artifact design and an agent workflow for producing it. The artifact design has four components: a refined codebase, an environment setup, experiment launchers, and a reproduction guide. The refined codebase is the original code with the gaps filled in and the structure cleaned up so an independent agent does not have to reverse-engineer intent. The environment setup pins dependencies and versions. The launchers are the actual commands to run each experiment, not the reader's guess. And the reproduction guide is a narrative document of the procedure, including the decisions that were made and why. ReproBridge is the agent workflow that takes a paper and its raw artifacts and constructs all four of these.

The evaluation is built around ReproBridge-Bench, which provides 1029 tasks from 387 experiments across 36 papers, and every task is anchored by five human-verified reference scores. That anchoring matters for how you read the results, because the question being measured is not can the agent get some number to come out of the code, but can it get a number that matches what a careful human verification found. About 70.3 percent of the tasks have numerically compatible reference results, and the remaining tasks are scored on the parts of the experiment that could be verified. The task definition is statistical: each task has a published score and five reference observations, and a reproduction attempt is judged against a tolerance interval around the references, with a default quantile of 0.95 and an explicit 1 percent tolerance floor. Tasks where the reference spread is at most 10 percent of the published value are treated as stable. That is a careful measurement design, and it is worth noting that the benchmark authors spent real human effort producing the reference scores, which is what separates this from a leaderboard of self-reported numbers.

The evaluation protocol is also worth describing, because it controls for the obvious confound. Agents attempt each task in fresh sessions, twice per model: once with the raw released code and once with the refined artifacts. A separate Auditor model checks the execution evidence, so the agent that did the reproduction is not the one grading it. Four coding agents are evaluated, and the paper names the strongest current generation of models, including the Sonnet and Opus lines and Gemini Flash, which is a reasonable roster for what teams are actually deploying.

The headline numbers: refined artifacts raise mean reproduction success from 76.4 percent to 92.6 percent across the four models, and reduce downstream API spending by 19.7 percent on average. The per-model gains range from 5.5 points for the strongest raw reproducer, Opus, to 23.2 points for Gemini. The spread in success rates across models narrows from 21.6 percentage points on raw artifacts to 3.9 on refined ones, with all four models above 90 percent mean success after refinement. On cost, the refined artifacts use 6.3 to 19.8 percent fewer tokens and cut API spending by 16.8 to 23.7 percent, counting failed attempts in the total. That cost result is easy to overlook, but it makes sense: when the procedure is explicit, the agent stops burning tokens rediscovering how the thing runs, and the failed attempts become fewer and shorter.

It is worth unpacking the audit separation a little, because it is what makes the cost numbers credible. The Auditor does not see the agent's reasoning, only the execution evidence: the commands that ran, the outputs that came out, and the logs. That means an agent cannot talk its way into a pass by explaining why the number it got should be accepted; the number either lands inside the tolerance interval around the human-verified references or it does not. It also means the cost accounting is honest in the way that matters, because the API spending includes the failed attempts, the retries, and the exploration the agent did before it got the procedure right, which is exactly the spending a team would actually pay.

A paper-macro aggregation, which averages the per-paper success rates with equal weight so that no single paper dominates, shows the same direction: gains of 6.7 to 20.7 points for all four models. The per-paper breakdown is also honest in a useful way: the refined artifacts win or tie on 30 to 33 of the 36 papers per model, while 3 to 6 papers regress. So this is not a uniform improvement; there are specific papers where the refined artifact made things worse, and the paper reports that rather than hiding it. The ablation removes one component at a time, and every removal reduces success, with the largest losses coming from removing the code refinement and the environment setup, which tells you where the value is concentrated: the runnable core matters more than the guide.

Now the skeptical read. The first thing to notice is the population: 36 papers from 2026 venues, so this is a benchmark of current-era artifacts, and the result is strongest exactly where the community is most likely to care, because it says the agent-plus-artifact pipeline works on recent work. But the other side of that coin is that it says nothing about older codebases, closed-source dependencies, or experiments whose results depend on hardware the authors no longer have. The tolerance design is reasonable, but it is a choice, and a 1 percent floor plus a 0.95 quantile will pass attempts that differ from the reference in ways a strict field might call failures. The five human-verified references per task are a real strength, but they are also a fixed snapshot: if the original experiment has a seed sensitivity larger than the tolerance, the benchmark measures stability against the snapshot, not agreement with the true value.

Second, the auditor separation is good design, but the auditor is itself a model, and the paper's claims about what counts as valid execution evidence inherit the auditor's judgment. For numerical reproduction tasks that is less of a concern than for, say, a training run where the success criterion is qualitative. The numerical anchoring is what makes this benchmark credible, and it is also what limits how far the findings extend beyond numerical experiments.

Third, and this is the biggest one: the benchmark measures whether refined artifacts help a fresh agent, not whether the refinement process is cheap. The paper reports that the workflow was developed on 10 papers and then evaluated on the full set, but the cost of producing the refined artifacts for a paper, in agent time and human review, is the number that determines whether this is adoptable practice or a research instrument. If refining one artifact costs as much as the five reference verifications it enables, the economics only work if the artifact will be reused many times, which is plausible for influential papers and not for the long tail.

The bottom line: ReproBridge is a clean, well-measured demonstration that the bottleneck in agent-based reproduction is often the missing procedure rather than the agent's competence, and that a standardized four-part artifact with an explicit reproduction guide lifts success from roughly three-quarters to above 90 percent while cutting cost. The benchmark design is the most defensible part of the paper, and the per-paper regressions are reported honestly. The open question is the production cost of the refinement workflow, because that is what decides whether this becomes the way artifacts get released rather than a benchmark with a strong result.
