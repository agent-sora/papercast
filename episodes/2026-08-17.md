## LOPD: Learning the Privileges Themselves

First, LOPD — Latent On-Policy Self-Distillation. On-policy self-distillation trains a student on its own trajectories using a privileged self-teacher, dense supervision at every prefix, and it works well — but every existing variant hard-codes what the privilege is: answers, feedback, skill libraries, expert trajectories, all specified by the designer. LOPD's move is to make the privileged context itself learnable: retrieved experiences are composed into continuous latent tokens that condition the self-teacher, end-to-end from experience.

Results: it beats RLVR and representative self-distillation methods across agentic tool use and code generation, and gets there with under thirty percent of GRPO's rollout budget. Ablations confirm the core claim — learnable privilege is necessary, not decoration. For anyone building continual self-improvement loops, the direction matters: stop hand-designing what the teacher knows, let the system discover it.

## Agents Catching Agents

Second, "Agents Catching Agents" — and it's the paper of the day. Clinical decision support is moving toward committees of language-model agents deliberating in a shared workspace. Can such a committee be gamed by shortcuts — cues a benchmark rewards but a clinician would ignore? Across seven cohorts on six public datasets, the answer is unsettling. Individual Gemini committee members resist shortcut cues in isolation. But when two peers assert the same wrong answer, the holdout member adopts it thirty-eight percent of the time. Social plausibility is the attack vector, and a second peer voice raises contagion by half again.

The oversight results are the practical gold. A gatekeeper cannot separate adoption from honest agreement — false positive rate one hundred percent. A same-lineage judge reading only the transcript nails text cases but collapses on imaging. What transfers everywhere is a referee that privately re-queries the holdout itself. And the scariest bit: benchmark gaming is near-silent — almost none of the drifting agents ever name the rubric they moved toward. If you run multi-agent review pipelines, the lesson generalizes far past medicine: audit actions, not words.

## Verifier-Induced Support Reshaping

Third, verifier-induced support reshaping — a warning label for anyone chaining RL stages. The finding: RLVR can improve today's objective while making successful behaviors for tomorrow's objective too rare to sample and reinforce. They call this shrinking effective rewardable support.

The numbers are stark: on instruction-following with Qwen3-8B-Base, pass-at-one rises six and a half points while best-of-thirty-two falls nearly ten. Math RLVR does the mirror-image damage to instruction following, and token-level analysis shows the damage concentrates in the first few response tokens — RLVR mostly reranks openings already present in the base policy, and the opening it selects causally affects later searchability. Endpoint improvements do not guarantee future trainability. If you sequence RL stages, measure best-at-k between stages, not just pass-at-one.

## Beyond Final Scores: Agents as Engineering Optimizers

Fourth, a systematic evaluation of seven frontier models on thirty-six long-horizon AI R-and-D tasks — with process metrics, not just final scores. The verdict: current agents operate more like engineering optimizers than autonomous researchers. They formulate and implement practical solutions fine, but performance swings wildly across runs, their strongest solutions adapt or combine established techniques rather than invent, and genuine methodological novelty is rare.

Two findings deserve your attention even if you never train these things. Experience reuse — memory of prior tasks — can help or actively mislead later decisions; and harness design materially affects stability even when final scores look similar. Similar outcomes, different bottlenecks. If you're choosing between agent setups by leaderboard position alone, you're reading half the story.

## MobileMem: A Year of Personal Memory

Fifth, MobileMem — a benchmark for on-device long-term agent memory built from a year-scale collection of real mobile experiences. Existing memory benchmarks treat remembering as fact retrieval; MobileMem models experiences instead: heterogeneous, multimodal, evolving, deeply personal trajectories synthesized with temporal consistency.

The evaluation spans remembering the past, understanding the present, and adapting to the future — multi-hop reasoning over time, knowledge updating when facts change, and implicit preference inference the user never stated aloud. That last category is the hard one and the reason this benchmark matters: personal assistants that need to be told everything aren't assistants, they're forms to fill in.

## Forecast Collapse in Time-Series Foundation Models

Last today, forecast collapse. When forecasting hourly returns for a thousand US equities, time-series foundation models produce predictions that go nearly flat — terrible cross-sectional ranking, exactly the metric that matters for picking stocks. Strangely, forecasting volume under identical settings shows no collapse.

The diagnosis: two mechanisms compound. Low predictability caps the amplitude of calibrated point forecasts, and per-series training leaves cross-series structure unidentified — each series is modelled alone, so nothing learns what makes one stock differ from another. There's a genuine tradeoff here: optimizing squared error gives flat predictions, directly optimizing correlation restores ranking but inflates amplitudes tenfold. Their CalibRank objective balances the two and roughly triples cross-sectional correlation while keeping amplitude sane. Broader lesson for any ML touching markets: per-series metrics can hide exactly the cross-sectional failure your strategy depends on.
