FrameForge: Harnessing Priors of Video Foundation Models for Unified Image Generation, by Yijing Lin, Zhendong Mao, and Mengqi Huang, from the University of Science and Technology of China with FrameX.AI, uses a pretrained video diffusion model as the backbone for a single image model that handles subject-driven generation, canny-to-image, and instruction-guided editing in one system. The premise is that a video model already encodes how a visual input transforms across time, so instead of treating conditional image generation as a fixed layout problem, you can treat the relationship between the condition and the target as a variable temporal relationship and let the model pick which temporal scale applies to each task. The base backbone is HunyuanVideo, a video diffusion model, which gives the image generator the visual-transform priors that an image-only backbone never saw.

The problem with prior unified conditional generation is a mismatch. Existing approaches typically assign the condition and the target to fixed temporal positions in a short video, so the gap between them is always the same, but condition-target relationships vary a lot across tasks. In subject-driven generation the target is a new scene that keeps the reference subject, in canny-to-image the target is the same structure with new appearance, and in editing the target is close to the input except for the edited region. A fixed temporal offset cannot represent all of those, and forcing one offset means the model is always solving a slightly wrong problem, either treating an edit as too large a transformation or a subject-driven generation as too small a one. The reason a video backbone is the right home for this is that during pretraining it has seen real visual change over time at every scale, from a camera pan to an object morphing, so the temporal dimension already carries a gradient of transformation magnitudes that an image model has to invent from scratch. FrameForge's answer is input-adaptive virtual temporal relations, and it models two complementary aspects of that relation with two mechanisms: how far the target is from the condition, and how the condition becomes the target.

The first mechanism is MoTE, Mixture of Temporal Experts, which models how far the target lies from the condition. Instead of one fixed positional encoding, the model has temporal RoPE experts with different virtual intervals, a short-interval expert, a medium-interval expert, and a long-interval expert, and a router dynamically selects among them. The virtual interval is expressed as an equivalent video-frame index, so a short interval means the target is close to the condition in the temporal sense and a long interval means the transformation is more substantial. The ablation is the detail that matters here: increasing the expert count from one to three improves canny-to-image generation and editing while holding the subject-driven score, but five or seven experts hurt, because under joint training the supervision gets spread too thinly across the experts. Three is the sweet spot they land on. They also show that expert selection is not a fixed per-task assignment; over the denoising steps, averaged over 500 cases, the routing shifts from the long-interval expert toward the short-interval expert as the target content emerges from noise, and editing makes that transition earlier and stronger. That is evidence the router is responding to the evolving target representation rather than memorizing a task schedule.

The second mechanism is CoTR, Chain of Temporal Reasoning, which models how the condition is transformed into the target. It inserts a latent intermediate state between the condition and the target and uses a reasoning-bottleneck attention mask that encourages information to flow condition, intermediate, target, rather than letting the target attend to the condition directly. The single reasoning latent is used by default, and that is a deliberate choice: without direct intermediate supervision, adding more intermediate latents just enlarges the information bottleneck without adding signal, so one unsupervised reasoning latent is the minimum that works. This is the part of the design that is most likely to transfer to other conditional-generation setups, because it is a generic way to force a model to pass through an explicit transformation state, and the cost of the bottleneck is one extra latent and a masked attention pattern, which is cheap relative to the backbone. The two mechanisms are complementary in a specific way: MoTE decides how much change the target should undergo, and CoTR decides what that change looks like as a process, so a long-interval routing with a strong bottleneck corresponds to a large, structured transformation, while a short interval with a weak bottleneck is a light edit. The paper does not deeply ablate the interaction between the two, which is the main open question about whether the gains come from MoTE, from CoTR, or from their combination.

The results are on three benchmarks. On DreamBench for subject-driven generation, FrameForge gets the best overall score of 0.739, improving over ChronoEdit by 1.37 percent, though EasyControl and IP-Adapter score higher on perceptual quality, which the authors acknowledge. On COCO 2017 for canny-to-image, FrameForge has the highest SSIM of 0.447, a 7.2 percent relative improvement over the second-best EasyControl, with a CLIP-T of 0.323 comparable to ControlNet and RealGeneral and an FID of 23.47. The authors are careful to note that FID is backbone-dependent and can reflect appearance statistics rather than perceptual quality, so they read it alongside SSIM, CLIP-T, and qualitative results. On GEdit for instruction-guided editing, among open-source methods FrameForge gets the best overall score on both Motion Change and the full eleven-category average, improving over ChronoEdit from 5.76 to 6.84 on Motion Change and from 5.08 to 6.12 overall, and its Motion Change score is already comparable to closed-source systems, surpassing Gemini 2.5 and Seedream 4.0 and approaching GPT-4o. They also run a cross-backbone check on HunyuanVideo 1.0 against a shared-LoRA baseline to show the strategy is not tied to one specific backbone.

The ablation structure is worth laying out, because it is where the paper earns its keep. Table 4 isolates the two mechanisms, comparing a task-specific LoRA per task against the unified shared LoRA with MoTE and CoTR enabled, and it is the shared-LoRA-plus-MoTE-plus-CoTR configuration that wins, which is the claim that one model with adaptive temporal relations beats several specialized adapters. Table 5 is the expert-count ablation, one, three, five, and seven temporal RoPE experts, and the curve is non-monotonic, it rises from one to three and falls from three to seven, which is the empirical signature of the supervision-spreading problem, too many experts and each one sees too few training examples to specialize. Table 6 compares candidate virtual intervals through their equivalent video-frame indices, which is how the virtual temporal offset is actually tuned, by matching it to a frame distance in the base video model's temporal space. The CoTR-only configuration, single latent with the bottleneck mask but no expert routing, is included as a control, and the MoTE-only configuration without the latent chain is the other half, so the reader can see how much each mechanism contributes in isolation, though the interaction term, the full system minus the sum of the parts, is the number I would most want to see reported explicitly.

A few things I would want to re-verify before trusting the headline. The FID comparison across different backbones is not apples-to-apples, and the paper concedes this, so the canny FID number should not be read as a quality win by itself. The DreamBench perceptual-quality gap to EasyControl and IP-Adapter is real and unexplained, and the overall-score win is driven by structural and subject scores, not perceptual quality. And the cross-backbone result is on HunyuanVideo 1.0, an older model, so the generalization claim is narrower than it reads.

My verdict is that FrameForge is a clean and well-motivated use of a video backbone's temporal priors for conditional image generation, and the input-adaptive temporal relation, with the MoTE router and the single-latent CoTR bottleneck, is a genuinely reusable design. But the quantitative case is mixed, the FID numbers are backbone-confounded, and the perceptual-quality gap on subject-driven generation is unaddressed, so this is a solid architecture paper with a modest empirical edge rather than a clear win across the board.
