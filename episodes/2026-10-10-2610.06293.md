"VepAgent," by Qiutong Chen, Yuchan Guo, and Zhenlong Yuan, from Nankai University, Carnegie Mellon University, and Xiaohongshu Inc. The task is video event prediction: given a partially observed video, predict what happens next. The failure mode the authors target is specific and interesting. A multimodal language model watches the frames, summarizes what it has seen, and then projects the future from that summary. The problem is that the future is not in the summary. The critical evidence is the unfinished transition, the part of the action that is still in progress, and retrospective summarization has a tendency to round it off into something already completed.

Their motivating example is a cooking video. The model sees butter being spread on bread. By the time it writes its summary, it has described the cooking stage as if it were done, and it predicts the next event as whatever follows a finished stage, which is wrong, because there is still an unbuttered slice sitting on the counter. The vanilla model's error is not a perception error. It saw the butter. Its error is a causal-transition error: it failed to model the logical progression from the terminal observed state to the future event.

The framework has three parts. The first is a supervised fine-tuning dataset called futurebench-4K, a chain-of-thought corpus built to bridge the causal-logic gap. The annotations do not just say what the next event is. They structure the deduction of the unobserved intermediate states, forcing the reasoning to name the state that has been reached, the transition that is in progress, and the state it is heading toward. This is a deliberate anti-shortcut design. A model that learns to predict from surface co-occurrence in the caption is penalized; a model that learns to reconstruct the in-progress state is rewarded.

The second part is a diagnostic tool library, and this is where the agent framing becomes real rather than decorative. During inference the model can call three tools. State transition tracking reconstructs the observed progression so far, the sequence of states the video has already passed through. Frame retrieval pulls additional frames from the video, which matters because the standard protocol samples a fixed number of frames, and the critical moment can fall between samples. And region magnification zooms into a specific part of a frame, which is how the model recovers the fine-grained visual state, like which slice of bread still lacks butter, that got lost when the frame was downscaled to fit the context. The tools exist to recover missing spatio-temporal evidence and to resolve visual ambiguity, and the model decides when to call them.

The three tools each target a specific hole in the standard pipeline, and understanding which hole explains when the framework earns its cost. Frame retrieval addresses sampling. The standard evaluation protocol gives the model a fixed budget of frames, thirty two in the main results, and a video's decisive moment is often a transient event, a hand completing a motion, an object leaving a frame, that falls between the sampled instants. The tool lets the model request additional frames around the region of interest, effectively converting a fixed-resolution timeline into a queryable one. Region magnification addresses resolution. Even when the right frame is present, the fine-grained evidence, which slice of bread is still unbuttered, whether the bread has fully entered the toaster, is often below the effective resolution after the frame is encoded for the model's input. The tool zooms into a bounding region and re-encodes it at higher fidelity. State transition tracking addresses reasoning state. It reconstructs the ordered progression of states the video has passed through, so the model reasons from "we have reached state B and the transition to C is in progress" rather than from a bag of observed events. The order of the ablation results, state tracking costing the most when removed, is consistent with this: the reconstruction of the in-progress transition is the piece the passive model most often loses.

The third part is a composite reward for the reinforcement learning stage, and it is the part I find most thoughtful. The reward has three terms. The accuracy reward for getting the final prediction right. A causal coherence reward for the intermediate chain-of-thought, so the reasoning has to be causally consistent, not just end in the right answer. And an anti-prior reward that penalizes language-prior shortcuts, the behavior where the model predicts from textual plausibility rather than from what the frames actually show. The anti-prior term is doing real work. In the ablations, accuracy reward alone gets an average of seventy four point six two percent. Adding the causal term lifts it to seventy six point four two. Adding the anti-prior term instead lifts it to seventy six point three three. All three together reach seventy eight point seven nine. The two non-accuracy terms are nearly additive, which suggests they are addressing two different failure modes, sloppy reasoning and ungrounded reasoning, rather than the same one.

The training recipe is modest and reproducible. The backbone is Qwen3-VL-4B. SFT runs for one epoch with an effective batch size of sixteen and a learning rate of five times ten to the minus six, on roughly a four thousand sample chain-of-thought set. Then reinforcement learning, using GRPO, runs for two hundred fifty steps with eight rollouts per sample at a learning rate of one times ten to the minus six, on about five thousand validated samples. The whole thing fits on eight RTX A5000 GPUs with twenty four gigabytes of memory each, which is a real constraint for a video reasoning paper and makes the result more credible, because it is not a compute-flexing exercise.

The main results. On FutureBench, against reported baselines under the same thirty two frame budget, VepAgent at four billion parameters reaches an average accuracy of eighty one point four four percent, up from sixty six point eight six for the strongest reported baseline, which is the thirty billion parameter Qwen3-VL-30B. That is a fourteen point five eight percentage point gain over a model roughly seven and a half times larger. Against its own four-billion backbone without the framework, the gain is twenty two point three five points. On NEPBench the result is even larger. VepAgent reaches seventy zero point two zero percent overall, up from forty seven point five zero for the strongest reported baseline, a seventy two billion parameter Qwen2.5-VL, a gain of twenty two point seven zero points.

The ablations on FutureBench are informative. Starting from the raw four-billion backbone at fifty nine point zero nine percent average, SFT alone takes it to seventy one point five nine. Adding reinforcement learning reaches seventy five point zero nine. Adding the tools reaches seventy eight point seven nine. The authors note that reinforcement learning without the prior SFT guidance struggles to navigate the causal reasoning space, which is why the two-stage order matters. Removing individual tools, state tracking costs the most at three point thirteen points, then frame retrieval at one point eight zero, magnification at zero point four eight, and visual description evidence at one point three three. And scaling the GRPO rollouts from two to eight raises the average to eighty one point four four, the final operating point.

Why the two-stage order matters is worth pausing on, because it is the design decision that makes the whole recipe work. Supervised fine-tuning on the chain-of-thought data installs a prior over causal-transition reasoning. The model learns the shape of the argument, name the reached state, name the in-progress transition, name the target state, before it is ever asked to optimize for it. Reinforcement learning then sharpens that prior against the actual reward signal. The ablation in row (c) versus row (b) shows the interaction: SFT alone reaches seventy one point five nine average, and adding reinforcement learning without the tools reaches seventy five point zero nine, a solid four point five gain. But the authors' reading is that reinforcement learning is doing its best work inside a space that SFT already narrowed. Without the structured reasoning foundation, the policy gradient has to discover the causal decomposition from scratch, and the exploration is too expensive over a two hundred fifty step budget. This is the same lesson that has recurred across reasoning-model training: the curriculum sets the search space, and the optimizer refines inside it.

The honest limits are stated plainly. The evaluation covers FutureBench and NEPBench, two video prediction benchmarks, and broader video understanding settings are untested. The tool-augmented inference also means the reported latency is higher than a single forward pass, and the paper does not quantify that tradeoff in a way I would rely on for a deployment decision. The gains are also measured against reported numbers for the baselines, so protocol mismatches are possible even with the stated frame budget.

My verdict is that this is a clean demonstration that a small model plus a structured causal reasoning curriculum plus a handful of cheap visual tools can outperform much larger passive video models on forward-looking prediction, and the composite reward with an explicit anti-prior term is the transferable idea. The framework is a template for any video task where the decisive evidence is an in-progress transition that summarization erases.
