Democratizing Robot-Use Agents: A Unified Harness with Open Models, by Jiarui Yuan, Yuxin Zuo, and Xiaoyu Huang, from a large Tsinghua University and UC Berkeley team with Pieter Abbeel and Ning Ding among the authors, is an argument that robot-use research has been quietly walled off behind proprietary models, plus a system that breaks the wall. Robot-use agents, or RUAs, are the agentic take on robot manipulation: instead of training a vision-language-action policy per task, you put a general-purpose model in a loop with a robot, giving it tools, perception, and feedback, and letting it figure out the task. The catch, which the paper states bluntly, is that every strong RUA result so far has been built on frontier, closed-weight models. You cannot fine-tune what you cannot see, so the entire research direction of improving robot agents through further training has been stuck in the hands of the labs that own the weights. The paper's contribution is uni-robot-agent, a unified, open harness that makes open-weight models competitive, so that the training loop, the part of the research that actually compounds, can run on models anyone can download.

The headline result is the one I would have doubted a month ago: an out-of-the-box, zero-shot Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, an open-weight model with no privileged inputs, no depth map, no action token tuning, driven by this harness, scores 31.39 on RoboDojo with a 25.00 percent success rate. That beats the published result for GPT-6 Astra with the RoboProbe harness, which stands at 28.97 score and 22.48 percent success, and it clears a field of vision-language-action and world-action-model baselines that sit around 21.39 percent success and 27.11 score. The ablation that makes the claim honest is the harness swap: take that same Qwen model and run it through other published harnesses, and it loses 8.89 percent of success rate and 10.38 points of score. So the result is not just a good model, it is a good model plus a harness that matters, and the harness is the part that is reusable. The paper also runs GPT-6 Astra through uni-robot-agent and gets the best result in the table, which is a nice control: the harness design benefits closed models too, so the contribution is not a hack tuned to one open model's quirks.

What the harness actually is, underneath the name, is a set of shared tools, execution, and feedback interfaces that connect any model to any environment adapter. The modularity is the point: the same tools, the same execution loop, the same feedback channel, work across simulation and real hardware, and across model families. That is what makes the system a platform for further training rather than a benchmark entry. The unified interaction interface is the specific design choice that makes the modularity real: the model sees one interface, the same tool schema and the same feedback format, whether it is driving a simulated arm or a physical one, so a policy or a prompt that works in simulation does not have to be re-derived for the real robot. That is a small-sounding detail that is, in practice, the difference between a benchmark harness and a training platform, because further training requires the signal to be stationary across environments, and a model that has to relearn the interface every time the hardware changes is not trainable, it is just being re-prompted. The evaluation is two-part, and the second part is the more interesting one. In addition to RoboDojo in simulation, the team runs nineteen real-world tasks, scored across capabilities like spatial understanding, each out of twenty points for a total out of one hundred. On the real robot, the open model performs competitively against the closed GPT-6 Astra system, which is the claim that the democratization is not just a simulation story. Simulation success is known to overstate what transfers to hardware, and a result that holds in the physical world is a different weight of evidence.

The analysis section is where the paper earns its "democratizing" framing. The authors look across model generations, families, and sizes, and they link effective robot use to four general capabilities rather than to any single robot-specific trick. The specific numbers in that analysis are the kind that a capability-focused reader will want to keep: the failure analysis shows that smaller or older open models fail in a distinctive way, with errors in their internal calculations and spatial grounding occurring in a large fraction of executed trials, while the strongest models cut those error rates by an order of magnitude. The practical implication is a training recipe in disguise: if robot use decomposes into four general capabilities, then future open RUA training should develop them jointly, not bolt on a manipulation head and hope. That is a genuinely useful framing for anyone planning to train an open robot model, and it is the part of the paper that will outlive the leaderboard numbers.

The honest caveats. This is a zero-shot comparison, so the open model is being judged on its base capabilities, and the paper's own argument is that the interesting result is what happens after further training, which is exactly what the harness enables and what the paper does not yet show. The RoboDojo leaderboard is young and the baselines are a moving target, so the margin over the closed-model reference is a snapshot, not a stable gap. And the real-world evaluation, while welcome, uses a small number of trials per task, so the capability scores carry more noise than the simulation numbers do. None of that undermines the core claim, but it does mean the strongest version of the paper, an open model trained in this loop that beats a closed model head to head, is the next paper, not this one. There is also a question of how much of the RoboDojo margin is the model and how much is the harness, and the paper's own numbers suggest the split is close to even, because the harness swap alone costs nearly nine percent of success rate. That is a healthy result for the harness, but it also means the headline number is only reproducible with the whole system, not with any single piece of it, which is a real constraint on how other groups will try to match it.

My verdict is that this is the right paper at the right time for the robot-agent field: it removes the closed-weight dependency that was quietly gating the entire direction, it does so with a model that genuinely beats the published frontier result, and it reframes robot use as four trainable general capabilities. The harness matters as much as the model, the real-world results hold up, and the capability analysis gives the next generation of open robot training a concrete target. This is a platform result, and the platform is the contribution. The broader implication is worth stating plainly, because it is the reason the paper matters beyond the leaderboard. For a year or more, the robot-agent literature has been split into two camps that were not talking to each other: the closed-model camp, which had impressive zero-shot results but no way to improve them, and the open-training camp, which had the training loop but models too weak to matter. This paper collapses the split, because the open model in the harness now clears the closed reference, which means the training camp inherits the strongest starting point in the field. If the four-capability decomposition is right, then the next two years of open robot-agent research have a shared roadmap for the first time, and that is a bigger deal than any single score on RoboDojo. The paper will also be remembered for what it did not do: it did not release a trained open robot model, it released the conditions under which one can be trained, which is the rarer and more durable contribution.
