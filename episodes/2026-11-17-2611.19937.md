Today's paper is AllocFlow: End-to-End Portfolio Trading Action Generation from Raw Market Data, from Annan Wang, Yuchen Xie, and Yu Cen, working across Nanyang Technological University, as an independent researcher, Stanford University, and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The short version: instead of predicting prices and then building a portfolio from those predictions, this system learns to emit the whole target portfolio in one shot, directly from raw market bars, and it does that with a flow-matching head sitting on top of a frozen financial foundation model.

The problem setup is the standard split in quantitative trading. One pipeline learns a forecasting model — an alpha model, a next-bar predictor, something of that kind. A second stage, usually an optimizer or a hand-built heuristic, turns those forecasts into positions under capital constraints. The authors' complaint is that the two stages optimize different things. The forecaster has no idea that its errors get compounded by the position sizing, and the position builder can never fix a bad forecast, only mitigate it. They want one system trained end-to-end on trading outcomes: in, raw multi-asset market history; out, signed position weights for every instrument in the book, simultaneously.

Here is the architecture, because the implementation details are where this paper earns its keep. The policy has three pieces. First, a frozen encoder for market context. They take Kronos, a financial foundation model pretrained to predict the next bar from tokenized open-high-low-close-volume sequences, and — this is an important detail — they do not use its forecast. Kronos's own price forecasts lose money after fees, zero-shot and after a LoRA adaptation; the paper shows that in a table. What they keep is Kronos's internal representation. For each asset, normalized OHLCV plus a derived amount get tokenized into coarse and fine IDs; one forward pass through Kronos yields two hidden states, and they mean-pool the last eight steps of each stream to get one coarse market token and one fine market token per asset. So the policy consumes representations, never predicted prices. That's a clean way to decouple "the market encoder knows something about markets" from "the market encoder is a good price forecaster."

Second, the action head is a DiT-style transformer. The per-asset token pairs get interleaved — coarse then fine, for asset one, then asset two — and a mean of everything is appended as a shared market token, so the head sees two times N plus one tokens. A set of continuous allocation-state tokens is mixed in; the output of the token for asset i is the target weight for asset i. That design lets the head compare opportunities across assets while generating all positions jointly, which is the whole point of "joint" allocation. At inference the asset roster can change — names can enter or leave the universe — and the head has no fixed per-asset output slots, so it maps whatever the current roster is.

Third, the generation mechanism is flow matching over the continuous action vector. Rather than regressing one point estimate of the book, the head learns to sample from a distribution over complete books, so it can propose alternative whole portfolios and the training objective can be financial, not squared error on positions.

Training is two-stage, and this is the part I'd scrutinize first. Stage one is strategy-supervised imitation: they have a teacher strategy — a hand-built or existing allocation rule — and they train the flow head to imitate the teacher's books, which shapes the distribution of candidate portfolios into something structured before any reward enters the picture. Stage two is group-relative policy optimization, the same RL flavor you see in reasoning LLM training: roll out a group of candidate books from the policy, score each with a portfolio-level financial reward, and update against a frozen reference of the policy. The reward is the actual ledger — returns after fees, with execution that rounds the target weights and applies them one bar later. The ledger is a fixed-notional book: the model learns the desired weights, and the ledger derives orders and fees from changes in those weights.

Results, on hourly cryptocurrency perpetual futures. The imitation-only policy posts a 27.79 percent annualized net return with a Calmar ratio of 0.955. After the RL stage, the same system posts 51.38 percent annualized net return and a Calmar ratio of 4.043 — and the paper claims it surpasses the teacher strategy on every metric, which is a strong statement for an imitator to make about its teacher. On six months of unseen 2026 data, held out and no retraining, they report a Calmar ratio of 4.90.

Ablations worth knowing. Swap the frozen Kronos encoder for a frozen iTransformer, keep everything else: the imitation policy loses 141.58 percent annualized, the RL version loses 119.29 percent. The feature quality of the market encoder dominates the headline. Flow-head depth: a 12-layer head gives 29.37 percent imitation and 46.74 percent after RL; 32 layers gives the headline numbers. So the deeper head matters, but the shallow one is not broken. And there are case studies showing the RL stage changes timing, not just scale: on one asset the RL policy turns short roughly 11 hours before a peak where the teacher and the imitator stay long through the decline, and on another it covers the short and turns long about 11 hours before a bottom. The authors are careful to say these windows illustrate a mechanism, not that it happens often across the portfolio.

Now the skeptical part, because the numbers here are exactly the shape that should make you lean back. First, the venue is crypto perpetual futures on one-hour bars, a market with fat tails, funding rates, and regime shifts that are almost worse than equities. A 51 percent annualized net return and a Calmar near 4 are extraordinary in any market; the rolling Sharpe figure in the paper's first figure stays positive through the sample, but one out-of-sample window at Calmar 4.90 is six months. Six months of crypto is one regime. I would want the full 2026 out-of-sample curve, the drawdown profile, and a cost sensitivity that moves the fee assumption, before I treated any of this as anything but a very good backtest.

Second, the reward is portfolio-level and the RL stage is group-relative. That means the policy is being pushed to look good on the realized reward of the specific universe and window it was trained on, with a frozen reference providing only mild regularization. The paper's own analysis admits the mechanism windows are sparse. The question I would re-verify: does the RL gain survive when you perturb the reward — different fee levels, different position limits, a slightly different asset roster — or is the 23.6-point return jump tied to the exact training reward?

Third, "end-to-end" is doing more work than it should. Kronos is frozen and was pretrained for forecasting, not trading; the system inherits whatever that model encodes about markets, and the iTransformer comparison tells you the choice of encoder is doing a huge amount of the heavy lifting. So the transferable idea here is narrower than the title suggests: a frozen market-representation encoder, a joint-portfolio flow head, and RL on a real ledger. The flow-matching-over-books piece is the genuinely novel part, and it's a reasonable one — treating the whole book as one continuous object with dependencies is more honest than regressing each position independently.

Engineering gotchas if you try to reproduce this. You need a working fixed-notional ledger with fees and one-bar execution lag before you touch the policy; most of the failure modes in this space are ledger bugs, and a backtest that quietly credits fills at the wrong price can manufacture a Calmar of 4 out of nothing. The Kronos representation path is specific to a particular tokenization of OHLCV; if you swap encoders, you are re-running the iTransformer-style ablation on your own data, and the paper suggests the gap can be catastrophic. The RL stage needs a reference policy frozen at the imitation stage, group rollouts scored on the ledger, and the two-stage split — the paper's own numbers show imitation shaping the distribution first is what makes the RL stage stable.

Bottom line: AllocFlow is a coherent, well-instrumented demonstration that you can skip the forecast-then-allocate split and train the portfolio itself with flow matching plus ledger-based RL, and the ablations are unusually honest about how much of the result is the frozen market encoder. I would trust the method and the failure-mode analysis, and I would not trust the headline returns without a much longer out-of-sample window, a cost sensitivity, and a second market before believing the strategy generalizes beyond the exact universe it was trained on.
