Score Centering Stabilizes Off-policy Reinforcement Learning, by Martin Marek and Max Ryabinin of Together AI. If you train language models with reinforcement learning today, you live with an uncomfortable secret: the policy gradient identity that justifies your entire loss only holds if the engine that generates your rollouts and the engine that computes your gradients produce bit-identical outputs. In practice they never do. Training clusters roll out samples on quantized, continuously batched inference engines running asynchronous checkpoints, while gradients flow through a trainer in a different precision with different kernel orderings. Every one of these small differences between the sampler and the trainer is collectively called the training-inference mismatch, and under enough of it, RL runs collapse outright. The standard playbook is importance sampling: reweight every token by the ratio between what the trainer believes and what the sampler believed, usually clipped or masked because raw ratios explode on rare tokens. This paper argues that most of that machinery is aimed at the wrong target. The instability is driven by drift, a bias term in the update that accumulates step after step exactly because the sampler is a moving biased copy of the trainer, and it can be canceled with an additive correction that involves no ratios, no clipping, and no masking.

The analysis starts from a diagnosis question: why is RL so much more sensitive here than supervised fine-tuning, which trains happily on data from completely different models with no correction at all? The authors strip RL down to its SFT-similar form, a policy gradient with plus-one-or-zero rewards on fully offline data, and selectively reintroduce the two remaining differences, the reward mode and the online feedback. Under synthetic mismatch on Qwen3 at one point seven billion parameters on the Countdown puzzle, the pattern inverts: offline training is stable only with positive rewards, while online training is least stable precisely with positive rewards and most stable with group-centered rewards that mix signs. The conclusion is that the online nature itself, the moving teacher, is the dangerous part. The mechanism is a covariance identity: decompose the expected policy gradient at any prefix into a drift term, the expected reward times the expected score, plus a signal term, the covariance between reward and score. The signal term is actual learning. The drift term, by contrast, knows nothing about which rollouts succeeded, it depends on the reward only through its mean, and it points in the direction of the negative gradient of cross-entropy loss against the sampler itself. Off-policy, vanilla policy gradient is quietly distilling the trainer toward its own biased sampler at every prefix. Distillation toward a fixed teacher would be harmless, it converges to a maximum-likelihood fit. But this teacher moves: after every step the trainer's weights are synced back into the sampler, so each update recomputes the mismatch from a slightly worse model, and the bias compounds in a feedback loop instead of converging. Equivalently, distillation usually works because the teacher is fixed while the student moves; here the teacher chases the student and the error never gets a chance to settle. This reframing also resolves a literature fight the paper calls out by name: one group found FP8 rollouts stable with token-level importance sampling, another saw divergence in BF16 despite careful engineering, and both observations are consistent with drift accumulating step by step, because mild mismatch destabilizes training too, but only after enough steps.

The fix is one line of algebra. The policy gradient identity relies on the fact that, on-policy, the expected score of any token is zero, because the total gradient of your own probabilities must vanish. Under mismatch the expectation is taken under the sampler instead of the trainer, and it stops being zero. Score centering simply subtracts that expectation from every score: since both terms are evaluated against the same distribution, the centered score has exactly zero mean under the sampler at every prefix, which erases the drift term regardless of how large the mismatch is or where it came from. What remains of the update is the covariance between reward and score measured under the sampling distribution, and that is as close to the on-policy update as one can get without importance ratios. And unlike importance sampling, this correction is deterministic given the prefix and evaluated exactly, with no variance penalty and no dependence on which rare token got sampled. It also composes cleanly with importance sampling: you reweight the scores with whatever clipped or masked ratio your system prefers, take the expectation of the reweighted score, and subtract that. Vanilla importance sampling reappears as a sanity check, where the centering term vanishes because exact ratios already have zero mean.

Implementation is where practical papers earn their keep, and this one is unusually considerate. Storing the sampler's full next-token distribution, with a vocabulary over a hundred and fifty thousand tokens and rollout batches in the thousands by tens of thousands of tokens, would need tens of terabytes, so they log only the top one hundred twenty-eight sampler logprobs and reconstruct the tail by rescaling the trainer's distribution to match the sampler's tail mass. The expected-score sum then only touches the head tokens, exploiting that the full-vocabulary score expectation is exactly zero, so the tail contributes algebraically. An ablation shows top thirty-two matches full-vocabulary centering in every tested setting, the custom sampler finishes within one percent of baseline wall-click, and vLLM or SGLang expose the needed logprobs natively. The whole method is a scalar loss with a stop-gradient, no hyperparameters, with reference JAX code a screenful long and shared on GitHub. The paper also notes the relation to classical control variates: subtracting zero-mean score is a classic varlance-reduction shape, but replayed as a bias correction, and unlike classical off-policy RL, where the correction needs an intractable expectation over actions, language models make the expected score exactly computable as a weighted sum over one token's logprobs. That unusual structure is the entire opening for the method.

The evaluation, from zero point six billion up to thirty billion parameters, is built around deliberately severe mismatch because mild mismatch takes too many steps to separate methods within a handful of GPU-hours: under small weight noise the best methods overlap and nothing significant can be read. With a synthetic fixed Gaussian offset on the sampler weights, three noise scales show the survival ladder: plain policy gradient and its clipping cousins collapse progressively earlier as mismatch grows, down to step twenty at the strongest noise, and only score centering, alone or composed, trains stably. Under the mildest noise the correction methods overlap, under the middle one the truncated and masked importance-sampling variants still survive, and at the harshest only score-centered variants hold. On the realistic sources, the sampler is quantized while the trainer stays BF16: under FP8 weights, activations, and KV cache, uncorrected policy gradient still trains stably to fifty-eight percent and score centering reaches fifty-two, effectively tied, while under FP8 weights with FP4 KV cache policy gradient collapses inside two hundred steps and score centering stays stable at fifty-two against fifty-one for truncated importance sampling, and under INT8 with an INT4 cache, score centering reaches thirty percent while importance sampling reaches twelve and everything else ends below five. Under an intentionally extreme staleness regime where the sampler is frozen for sixty-four steps, the composition dominates and plain score centering is visibly weaker, exactly as the theory predicts, since score centering measures the covariance under the sampler rather than the trainer, and importance sampling partially corrects that distribution first. Notably, PPO and DAPO survive staleness but collapse under quantization, which the authors attribute to their clipping being built for ratios that arise from policy movement rather than for ratios that arise from numerical error. That asymmetry, clipping designed for drift between policy versions failing on drift between engines, is a crisp observation that any RL engineer should take home independently of adopting the correction.

The skeptical read. First, the headline gaps come from mismatch far heavier than any well-run pipeline tolerates, mild mismatch degrades stability too but only over many training steps, and the paper is explicit that this severity is a proxy for long runs, used because multi-seed significance ablations over hundreds of steps were unaffordable: six thousand one hundred and eighty GPU-hours already went into these figures on H100s, mostly on the thirty-billion runs, and those thirty-billion runs are single-seed for nearly every method, with extra seeds only where the gap was largest. Second, everything uses a shared plain objective with SGD rather than each system's recipe, memory-efficient and informative for isolating corrections, but real recipes bundle other choices and could behave differently; the setup also uses short sequences, up to five hundred and twelve tokens for the small models, while mismatch grows with sequence length, so long-context agentic workloads only get indirect evidence. Third, on the mixture-of-experts model, routing is not replayed, though the authors themselves flag router replay as preferable in practice. Fourth, score centering needs the sampler's logged top-k logprobs at rollout time, an interface cheap in this codebase and natively available in mainstream samplers, but any serving stack that does not surface it needs some work. Fifth, the staleness result weakens the pure-method story, and the abstract's composition claim carries the weight in the regime closest to production practice. What I trust: the drift decomposition is clean, the ablation isolating score centering across seeds and noise scales is careful, and the implementation is credible without new infrastructure. What I would re-verify at scale: whether five to forty percent accuracy gaps survive at real training scales with realistic mild mismatch and full recipes, and how the correction behaves when replaying the router in a real distribution over experts. Bottom line: the paper reframes training-inference instability as a compounding distillation drift and shows that a deterministic, hyperparameter-free correction term erases it, matching or beating every importance-sampling design from a fourfold-larger family of methods in the severe-mismatch regimes; the hidden mechanism of moving-teacher failure is worth internalizing regardless, since it explains observations across an entire cluster of recent stability reports.
