MC-Sparse: Deconstructing and Closing the Dense-Sparse Attention Gap in Diffusion Transformers, from Jiarui Chen, Zeqiang Lai, and Jiangshan Wang at Fudan University, Tencent HY, and the Shanghai Innovation Institute. The paper attacks the most expensive part of running diffusion transformers on long sequences, the quadratic attention over tens to hundreds of thousands of video and 3D tokens, by making sparse attention actually close the fidelity gap to dense attention. The headline: a training-free method, Meta-Cached Sparse Attention, that at fifteen percent attention density gets an eighteen percent relative speedup of about one point eight times on MiniMax H3 Base for video, and two point three two times on their internal 3D asset model, with negligible visible quality loss.

The way the paper earns its keep is the diagnosis, which is the more durable contribution. Existing sparse methods for diffusion transformers almost all work at block granularity, a group of queries shares a selection of whole key-value blocks, and block importance is estimated from mean-pooled query and key vectors. That design entangles two errors, and the authors use controlled oracle comparisons at matched attention density to untangle them. An oracle selector has access to the exact dense-attention probabilities and simply keeps the entries with the largest mass; they compare three oracles that differ only in granularity. The block oracle picks whole key-value blocks per query group, the token oracle picks individual key-value tokens but shares one selection per query group, and the per-query oracle lets every query pick its own tokens. Stepping through these oracles at the same density isolates three named error sources. Structural binding error, which has two parts: KV binding, where keeping a block drags in the irrelevant neighbors of an important token, and query binding, where queries with genuinely different attention patterns are forced to share one selection. Selection error, which comes from the approximate mean-pooled scoring, because the softmax of a pooled query is not the average of the softmaxes, so pooled scores can miss tokens that carry large cumulative mass. And discarded tail error, which is what remains even for the per-query oracle, because the tokens you throw away still carry nonzero attention mass. Each measured gap between successive oracles in their Wan 2.1 1.3 billion 480 pixel experiment is one of those three errors, and it gives the method three matching design principles: finer-grained selection, accurate attention scoring, and compensation for the discarded contributions.

The method implements all three, and the clever bit is amortization across denoising steps. For structural binding, MC-Sparse selects individual key-value tokens across block boundaries, and it groups similar queries into equal-size tiles that match the kernel's query tile size. The grouping is a median-split version of principal direction divisive partitioning, which recursively projects each group onto its principal direction and splits at the median until every group has exactly the tile's number of queries, so you get within-group similarity without the padding waste that variable-size clustering causes. For selection error, instead of scoring with pooled vectors they solve the exact objective, pick the set of key-value tokens that maximizes the group's cumulative dense-attention mass, which is the top-K tokens ranked by the sum of the group's attention probabilities. But computing exact attention scores at every denoising step is expensive, so they lean on temporal stability: the attention patterns barely move between nearby steps, and their experiments show that reusing exact selections from an earlier anchor step actually beats recomputing mean-pooled selections every single step. For discarded tail error, they cache the residual between the dense and sparse outputs at the anchor step and add it back at reuse steps, because that residual changes far less across steps than the attention output itself, even more so under exact selection. So at an anchor step they do the full expensive work, group the queries, compute exact scores, pick the top tokens, and record the residual, and at subsequent steps they reuse all three cached pieces while recomputing attention with the fresh query, key, and value matrices. They also build the supporting engineering, a fast GPU implementation of the divisive partitioning, a two-pass exact selector, and a token-sparse attention kernel that gathers the selected keys and values online without repacking them into blocks. The two-pass exact selector is worth a beat, because it is what makes the exact objective affordable. FlashAttention does not expose the attention logits, so they implement a two-pass kernel that computes the scores directly for selection, and the QK-only pass costs roughly half a dense attention evaluation in GEMM operations. With the first anchor overlapping the final warm-up step, that extra dense evaluation plus the selection passes amortize to an overhead equivalent to only about five to six percent of attention density across all denoising steps, below the combined clustering and variable-length kernel overhead of the SVG2 baseline. The anchor also offloads its cached residuals and selected key-value indices to CPU memory, and because the per-layer transfer takes less time than the sparse attention itself, prefetching one layer ahead hides the transfer latency. So the expensive exact machinery happens a handful of times over the whole denoising trajectory rather than at every step, which is the difference between a method that sounds good on paper and one that actually returns the speedup it advertises.

On results, at fifteen and twenty-five percent attention density on MiniMax H3 Base at seven hundred sixty-eight pixels, they report PSNR of twenty eight point four four and twenty seven point three zero against the dense attention reference, SSIM of zero point eight nine nine and zero point eight eight two, LPIPS of zero point one six one and zero point one seven six, and image quality and background consistency scores that are flat or slightly better than dense. The full-attention baseline takes one thousand sixty two seconds of denoising, MC-Sparse at twenty-five percent takes six hundred sixty seconds for a one point six one times speedup, and at fifteen percent five hundred ninety seconds for one point eight times. On their 3D asset model the speedup is two point three two times at the same density. The comparison baselines are Sol-Attn and PISA, both of which they beat on fidelity at matched density.

The skeptical read. The diagnosis is clean and I trust the oracle decomposition, that is genuinely useful work for the whole sparse-attention-for-diffusion line. The concerns are about the claims around it. One, everything hinges on the temporal stability assumption, that query groups, exact selections, and especially the additive residual persist across denoising steps, and that is measured on a handful of models at specific resolutions; if you change the sampling schedule, add classifier-free guidance strength, or go to longer videos where the scene changes more between early and late steps, the cached residual could drift, and the paper's refresh schedule is a hyperparameter that trades accuracy against the speedup it is trying to claim. Two, the fidelity metrics are all computed against the dense output of the same model, so the speedup is real but the quality claim is relative to dense, not to what a human sees, and at fifteen percent density the frames look close, but that is a single model at one resolution. Three, the additive residual correction is doing a lot of quiet work: part of the reported fidelity is the cached dense-minus-sparse difference being added back, which means you paid for a full dense attention pass at the anchor steps, so the effective cost is not the fifteen percent density everywhere, it is fifteen percent plus an anchor overhead, and the reported end-to-end speedup bakes that in but a reader comparing against other methods needs to remember it. Four, the 3D result is on an internal model, so it is not independently reproducible.

Bottom line: MC-Sparse is a strong, well-diagnosed training-free method, and the three-error decomposition of the dense-sparse gap is the result I would cite for understanding why block-sparse attention degrades at high sparsity. The practical speedups, one point eight times on video and two point three times on 3D at fifteen percent density with negligible visible loss, are credible. I would want the refresh schedule stress-tested across sampling schedules and longer videos, and I would want the anchor-step overhead broken out separately, before calling the fidelity numbers general rather than model-specific.
