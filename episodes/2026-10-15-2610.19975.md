The paper is LatentLoop: Scaling Looped MoE Models with Asymmetric Compute Allocation, from Zefeng He, Yunkai Chai, and Xiaoyang Chen, with the work spread across Shanghai Jiao Tong University, the Shanghai AI Laboratory, the University of Science and Technology of China, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, and Nanyang Technological University. It is squarely a reasoning-model-topology result: it takes a looped mixture-of-experts transformer, a design that reuses the same block of layers over and over to get extra depth without extra stored parameters, and shows that you can get a free win by giving the two loop visits genuinely different amounts of compute.

The setting is looped MoE. A looped transformer applies a shared set of layers multiple times. Each pass, or visit, adds effective depth without storing a separate weight matrix for that depth, which is attractive because it decouples the number of stored parameters from the amount of computation per token. The recent line of work, in particular something called SMELT, combines this looping idea with a mixture-of-experts backbone, so you get parameter reuse plus sparse computation, and it shows that looping can actually beat a non-looped model of matched size under equal resource budgets. The authors start from that SMELT recipe, which applies the middle half of the layers twice using shared parameters, and they ask a sharper question: even when the two visits share the exact same weights, do they do the same job? The answer they are looking for is not a yes or no, it is a measurement, and the residual-coefficient probe is designed to read it out of the trained model rather than assume it.

Their probe is elegant. They add a learnable residual coefficient to each visit and optimize it together with the weights. In a looped block the residual connection adds the block's output back to the input, and scaling that addition lets each visit learn how hard it is allowed to push on the representation. What they find, at both model sizes they study, is a clear asymmetry: the first visit learns a substantially smaller coefficient, while the second stays close to one. They confirm this is real and not a training artifact by freezing the coefficients and sweeping them. When they shrink the first visit's residual scale, validation negative log likelihood holds steady or even improves. When they shrink the second visit's scale by the same amount, it gets worse. So the first pass is, in effect, a gentle refinement that the model can afford to underweight, and the second pass is where the heavy lifting happens. That asymmetry is the whole motivation for what follows.

The proposal, LatentLoop, exploits that asymmetry by allocating compute unevenly across the two visits. The first visit processes a compressed, latent version of the sequence rather than the full token sequence, and the second visit operates at full resolution. Compressing the first pass saves computation, and instead of throwing that budget away they reallocate it to make the model wider, increasing the hidden size. This is a topological change in a real sense: the two visits now see sequences of different lengths, so the shared block is being applied to two different resolutions of the same data, which is something a naive loop never does.

Two design questions follow from that. The first is how to write the compressed first-visit update back to full-resolution states. They try a few options and land on mean aggregation with a learnable writeback coefficient, calibrated with a causal alignment so the latent increment lands where it should. The second is whether the different resolutions should change what parameters are shared. Untying the mixture-of-experts blocks would throw away most of the parameter reuse, because the experts hold the bulk of the stored parameters. So they share the MoE weights across visits and only untie the attention blocks, since attention over a compressed sequence and attention over a full sequence are genuinely different computations. They offset the extra attention parameters by slightly reducing the number of stored experts while keeping the number of active experts per token unchanged, so the per-token compute stays matched.

On the method side it is a from-scratch training study, not a fine-tune. They run fifteen matched-budget configurations spanning 1.3B, 3B, and 7B parameter models, with the largest training budget exceeding 10 to the 21 FLOPs, and they hold parameter count, key-value cache size, FLOPs per step, and total training FLOPs within 1 percent of the baselines. That matching is the part that makes the comparison fair, and it is exactly the discipline these efficiency papers need.

The results. Across all fifteen configurations LatentLoop consistently lowers validation negative log likelihood. The budget-matching procedure is worth describing, because it is what keeps the comparison honest: for each size tier they pick a reference token budget, 40B for the 1.3B tier, 70B for 3B, and 140B for 7B, and they match the baselines to LatentLoop within 1 percent on parameter count, key-value cache footprint, per-step FLOPs, and total training FLOPs. That means the width increase funded by the first-visit compression is paid for by a small reduction in stored experts, so the model is not simply bigger, it is reshaped. At a fitted NLL of 1.988, the predicted compute savings are 19.0 percent relative to a vanilla MoE model and 7.9 percent relative to the looped MoE baseline, at the same loss. On downstream evaluation, using that reference token budget, LatentLoop posts the highest average score across nine benchmarks in every size and shot setting, both zero-shot and five-shot. The gains over the looped baseline are modest, on the order of a little under a full point in five-shot and a few tenths in zero-shot, but they are positive everywhere, and the gains over the non-looped MoE base are larger, up to 1.82 percentage points in five-shot at the largest tier.

The ablations are informative. When they compare the four ways you could reduce the first visit's compute, cutting width, cutting expert intermediate width, cutting active experts, or compressing sequence length, sequence compression gives the lowest NLL, at 2.0303 against 2.0426 for the MoE base, with the final LatentLoop design at 2.0199. The likely reason is that compression preserves the full width and the full expert activation at each latent position, so you keep the representational richness while paying less to get there, whereas cutting width or active experts removes capacity that the second, full-resolution visit still needs. And within the compression design, the mean-aggregation writeback with independent attention blocks is the best of the encoding and writeback options they test, beating both direct writeback and a convolutional variant.

Now the skeptical pass. The downstream deltas are small, under a percentage point in most settings, so the practical payoff depends heavily on the compute-efficiency framing, which is a fitted NLL curve rather than a single headline number. If your workload is dominated by inference FLOPs, a 19 percent saving at matched loss is a lot; if you are parameter-bound or memory-bound in a different way, it is less clear. The budget matching within 1 percent is the right thing to do, but I would want to see the raw FLOP and memory accounting per configuration, because a wide-and-compressed model can shift memory pressure onto the key-value cache in ways the aggregate NLL does not show. I would also re-run the downstream average on the specific benchmarks I care about rather than trusting the nine-benchmark mean, since a small uniform gain can come from a single easy benchmark doing a lot of the work. The conceptual contribution, that loop visits are not symmetric and that the first visit wants to be cheaper, is the durable idea here and it generalizes beyond MoE.

Bottom line: a clean, well-controlled study showing that looped MoE models have a real asymmetry between their loop visits, and that compressing the cheap first visit and spending the savings on width beats a symmetric loop at matched compute. The downstream gains are modest, so treat it as a compute-efficiency play, but the finding that the visits should not be treated identically is a useful prior for anyone building on looped architectures.
