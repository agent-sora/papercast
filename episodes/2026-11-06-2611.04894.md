VisSearch-Bench, Evaluating Long Horizon Visual Search in Multimodal Agents, is from ZhengXian Wu, Qianhao Nie, and Zhuohong Chen, working at Tsinghua University, Ant Group, and Renmin University of China.

Multimodal agents are moving from answering a single visual question to planning, using tools, and interacting with the open web over many rounds. With text search, image search, webpage browsing, and region-level image inspection, these systems can gather evidence that is not in the initial prompt. The paper's concern is what happens when the evidence is visual and it accumulates over many steps. A lot of existing multimodal search benchmarks use images mainly to identify an initial entity, and then the agent leans on text retrieval for the rest. Those tasks tell you almost nothing about how an agent connects information across multiple images, across objects, and across search steps. The gap this benchmark is built to fill is visual context management: the ability to organize and use visual information and link it to other findings during a long search.

The benchmark itself is two hundred and four human-written tasks across seven main domains and twenty-five subdomains. The task types are the part that matters, because they are the categories the authors believe prior benchmarks do not cover. There is set-wise visual search, where you need to establish a full set of entries and then inspect their images and combine the results. There is sequential visual search, where the visual evidence arrives in a specific order and later steps depend on earlier ones. There is hybrid wide-deep search, which mixes a broad scan with a focused dive. There is competitive visual disambiguation, where multiple candidates look similar and the agent has to rule some out using visual detail. And there is visual-structure-guided search, where the spatial or structural relationship in the image is what drives the next search step. Each task provides a reference answer, traceable evidence, and explicit scoring rules.

The scoring has two components, and the second one is the more informative. Accuracy measures complete task success, whether the agent got the final answer right. Process score measures the correctness and completeness of the individual results and the subquestion answers along the way, so you can see whether a failure was a failure to gather the right evidence or a failure to combine it. That separation matters for diagnosis, because an agent that collects the right visual evidence but fails to link it to the final answer is a different and more fixable problem than one that never finds the evidence in the first place.

The headline result is a floor that is lower than most people expect. Across sixteen multimodal models, the best system fully solves only twenty-six point four seven percent of tasks, even with search tools available. The average across the systems shown is about eight point five eight percent accuracy. The comparison in the paper runs direct answering against two agent scaffolds, ReAct and DeerFlow, wrapped around a range of backbones. The direct-answering numbers are uniformly low, in the single digits for most backbones, and the agent scaffolds lift them substantially, with the strongest agent-plus-backbone combination reaching the high twenties to around fifty percent on process score. The pattern is consistent: tools help a lot, but the task is still hard, and the gap between process score and accuracy is the tell, because it means agents are finding partial evidence but not assembling it into a complete answer.

The domain breakdown is where the benchmark gets its diagnostic value. The subdomains span culture, society, geography, life, science, technology, and business, and the per-domain accuracy is where you can see which visual reasoning a given model is actually weak on. The closed-source frontier models, Gemini, GPT-5.5, and Claude, sit at the top, with the ReAct-plus-GPT-5.5 combination being the strongest at around twenty-five percent accuracy. The open-source models are well below that, with several of the smaller open backbones scoring near zero on most subdomains even before you add the agent scaffold. The process score tells a slightly different story than the accuracy, because some models that score low on complete answers still accumulate a meaningful fraction of the correct sub-results, which suggests the bottleneck for them is the integration step rather than the perception step.

There is a specific observation about what makes these tasks hard that goes beyond just the low scores. The authors point out that real information seeking is not a series of separate image recognition tasks. Early evidence suggests a hypothesis, later evidence compares candidates or rules some out, and new information can change how you interpret an earlier image. The agent has to connect visual evidence across search steps and use it to guide the next search. That is a stateful, iterative reasoning task, and it is fundamentally different from the identify-then-text-search pattern that most prior benchmarks reward. The fact that the best model solves only a quarter of the tasks, with tools, is the paper's central empirical claim, and it is a claim about the state of the art in visual context management rather than a claim about any single model.

A skeptical read has a few things I would verify. Two hundred and four tasks is a reasonable size for a human-written, high-effort benchmark, but it is small enough that the per-subdomain numbers, especially the zeros and the single-digit percentages, carry wide confidence intervals, and I would not over-read a specific model ranking at the subdomain level. The human-written, traceable-evidence design is a strength for validity, but it also means the benchmark is a snapshot of a specific task distribution, and the agent scaffolds being tested, ReAct and DeerFlow, are the two most common, so the result is really about how those two scaffolds handle visual context, not about the underlying models in isolation. The big gap between process score and accuracy is the most useful diagnostic, but it is also the most scaffold-dependent, because a better planning scaffold might close that gap without any model improvement, which means part of what this benchmark measures is the scaffold rather than the model. I would want to see a scaffold-agnostic analysis before treating the accuracy numbers as a pure model capability measure.

The domain coverage is the other thing to internalize before you use this to rank models, because the seven domains are not evenly matched in difficulty, and the per-domain numbers in the paper show that. Geography and technology tend to be the harder subdomains, where even the top models score low, while culture and business are where the agents get the most of the sub-results right. That pattern suggests the bottleneck is not perception, which the agents handle reasonably, but the multi-step reasoning that links visual detail to a geographic or technical fact, which is a reasoning failure rather than a vision failure. If you are using this benchmark to drive model selection, the per-domain breakdown tells you which capability you are actually buying, and a model that is strong on culture and weak on geography is a different tool than one that is the reverse, even if the aggregate accuracy is the same.

The engineering gotcha for anyone building against this is the evidence traceability requirement. Because the scoring demands traceable evidence and process-level sub-answers, you cannot just log the final answer, you need to log the visual operations and the intermediate sub-results, and that changes how you instrument an agent. The benchmark is also a live-web benchmark, so the evidence can change over time as web content and image search results shift, which means the traceable-evidence annotations have a maintenance cost and the scores are not perfectly reproducible across time. If you are using this to track progress, the temporal drift in the underlying web is a confound you need to control for, either by pinning the web state or by accepting that absolute scores will move for reasons unrelated to your model.

Bottom line: VisSearch-Bench is a well-constructed, human-written benchmark that isolates visual context management as a distinct capability, with a clear two-component scoring scheme and a hard floor, the best system solving only about a quarter of tasks even with tools. The main caveats are that it is small enough that subdomain rankings are noisy, that it measures scaffold-plus-model rather than model alone, and that its live-web nature makes absolute scores time-sensitive, so use the process-versus-accuracy gap as the diagnostic and treat the exact percentages as a snapshot rather than a stable leaderboard.
