NorthCUA Technical Report: Turbocharging Computer-Use Agents through Experience at Scale, by Chaoyun Zhang, Bingxuan Li, and Pinxue Guo, from the NorthCUA team at Shanghai Qiji Zhifeng, is a full system report for a computer-use agent family, and the through-line is the one CUA-Master is chasing from the other direction: computer-use agents are limited by experience, specifically by experience that connects individual decisions to complete outcomes. The problem with most agent training data is that it is a bag of actions, a screenshot and a click, with no notion of whether the sequence of actions actually finished the job. NorthCUA's answer is an Experience Flywheel that feeds a harness which runs long interactions, records the decisions, and checks whether the task actually succeeded, and then curates the verified trajectories back into the training set. For tasks the agent could not solve, a recursive self-improvement pass evolves reusable skills from the attempt and preserves the verified milestones, the parts that did work, so the next attempt starts further along instead of from zero. That milestone preservation is the most distinctive idea in the report: it treats a failed long-horizon attempt as a partial credit source rather than waste, which is exactly the right move for a domain where tasks are long and failures are frequent.

The training is staged, and the staging is the method. The curated experience is combined with grounding data from the flywheel to build GUI competence, first through mid-training and then through long-horizon supervised fine-tuning. After that, the policy learns from fresh attempts in the same harness through joint desktop and web reinforcement learning, which closes the loop: the environment that produced the experience is the same one the policy improves in. That closed loop is the architectural claim of the report, and it is the reason the authors can talk about a family rather than a model. There is a detail in the loop that is easy to miss and worth naming: the reinforcement learning is joint across desktop and web, meaning the same policy is optimized against two different kinds of environment, the local desktop with its files and applications and the web with its pages and forms, in a single training run. That is a non-trivial choice, because the action spaces and the visual distributions of the two environments are different, and training them jointly forces the policy to factor out the parts of computer use that are common to both, the grounding, the long-horizon planning, the recovery from a wrong action, rather than learning two separate specializations. It is the same move the data flywheel makes at the data level, and the report is consistent in its bet that the reusable structure of computer use is what should be learned, not the surface of any one application. The family is Nano, Mini, and Pro, plus a fourth variant, NorthCUA-Ultrafast, which is a quality-latency tradeoff: it pairs direct action selection, picking from a menu of likely actions, with open-ended generation, free-form action output, on the pre-reinforcement-learning Nano. The result is a model that answers faster by sometimes refusing to generate, which is a practical design for web workflows where a slow, thoughtful agent is a broken agent.

The numbers are strong for an open-source family, and the report's own framing is state-of-the-art among open-source models under the reported settings, which is the honest way to say it. On ScreenSpot-Pro, the GUI grounding benchmark, NorthCUA-Pro scores 93.3 percent, against 84.5 for Qwen3.8-Max, 67.7 for Kimi-K3, 60.1 for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, and 56.7 for GLM-5.3-Flash. That is a nine-point gap over the next model on grounding, which is the basic skill of looking at a screen and finding the right element, and grounding is the floor that everything else in computer use stands on. On OSWorld-G, the desktop execution benchmark, Pro scores 91.0 against 71.6 for Qwen3.8-Max, and on OSWorld-Verified it is in the same range, around 88.6, versus 86.1 and 84.8 for the other strong models. The Ultrafast variant is where the latency story lands: it completes matched successful WebArena-Verified tasks 2.3 times faster on average than the full generation variant, which is the measured cost of the direct-selection-plus-generation split. Those numbers together, top grounding, top desktop execution, and a three-times-faster web variant, are a coherent product story rather than a single benchmark win.

It is worth separating the two claims hiding inside those numbers, because they come from different parts of the system. The grounding result, the 93.3 on ScreenSpot-Pro, is mostly a data and mid-training story. Grounding is a perception-plus-localization skill, and the flywheel's verified trajectory data is full of exactly the screen-state-to-target pairs that grounding training needs, so a nine-point lead over the next open model is what you should expect from a system whose data pipeline is tuned for precisely that distribution. The desktop-execution result, the 91.0 on OSWorld-G, is a different claim, and a harder one, because OSWorld tasks are long-horizon: they are multi-step workflows where a single wrong click derails the task, and that is where the staged learning, the long-horizon supervised fine-tuning and the joint desktop-web reinforcement learning, have to do their work. The fact that the same model family leads on both the short-horizon grounding benchmark and the long-horizon execution benchmark is the more impressive statement, because it says the experience flywheel is not just producing good examples, it is producing good sequences, and sequence quality is the property that separates a demo from a system.

What I would want from this report, and what the technical-report format gives you less of than a method paper, is the ablation of the flywheel itself. The experience-at-scale claim is load-bearing, but the report does not, in the parts I can see, isolate how much of the Pro result is the verified-trajectory curation, how much is the milestone preservation, and how much is the joint desktop-web reinforcement learning. Those are three separate mechanisms, and a reader deciding which one to copy needs the split. The recursive self-improvement claim, that skills evolved from failed tasks transfer to later attempts, is the most ambitious and the least directly measured in the summary; the milestone-preservation mechanism is plausible and the near-miss argument is sound, but I would want to see the transfer number, the success rate on previously-failed tasks after the skill evolution, rather than the aggregate leaderboard result. The report is 123 pages, so those details may well be in the appendices, and I would read them before trusting the mechanism over the headline. There is also the usual open-model caveat: the state-of-the-art claim is among open-source models, and the closed frontier models are not in the comparison set, so the gap to the best proprietary computer-use agent is not measured here. The broader picture the report is arguing, and the part I think is the real thesis, is that the computer-use model race is converging into an experience race, and experience is not just data, it is a pipeline. A model with a flywheel is not the same thing as a model with a dataset, because the flywheel compounds: every deployed task produces new verified trajectories, which train a better model, which handles harder tasks, which produce harder trajectories. The Staged Learning recipe is what turns that compounding into a stable training process, because naively adding a flywheel's data to pretraining would poison the distribution, and the staging, GUI competence first, then long-horizon, then reasoning, is the load-bearing detail. The right read is not that NorthCUA wins every table, but that a small open-source family, trained with a documented experience pipeline, is now within touching distance of the frontier on the tasks that matter for real computer use, and that the gap is measured in pipeline quality rather than in compute.

My verdict is that NorthCUA is the most complete open-source computer-use system report of the batch, with the Experience Flywheel and its milestone preservation as the ideas worth stealing and grounding and desktop numbers that lead the open field by a real margin. But it is a system report, and the flywheel's individual contributions are not cleanly ablated in the visible portions, so the right move is to treat the leaderboard numbers as solid and the recursive-improvement mechanism as promising-but-unverified until the appendices say otherwise.
