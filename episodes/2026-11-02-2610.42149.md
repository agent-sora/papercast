Latent Reasoning in Continuous Space for Unified Multimodal Models, by Byungwoo Jeon, Yoonwoo Jeong, and Hyunseok Lee, from KAIST and POSTECH with RLWRLD, introduces LARC, a framework for making unified multimodal models reason in continuous hidden space rather than in discrete token space, specifically to fix image generation. The diagnosis is a modality mismatch: existing reasoning-augmented multimodal models do their chain-of-thought in discrete token space, which is what large language models do, but image generation operates in a continuous space, so the reasoning never actually touches the thing it is supposed to control, and as a result these models fail to inherit the test-time scaling and fine-grained controllability that language models have. The proposed fix is to move the reasoning itself into the continuous space, reasoning over hidden states instead of tokens, which removes the token-level bottleneck and lets the reasoning act directly on the representations that drive generation.

The training is two stages, and the structure is the point. Stage 1 is curriculum supervised fine-tuning, which gradually converts token-level reasoning into latent reasoning. The curriculum matters: you do not just replace the token chain with a continuous one in one shot, you anneal it, training the model to do part of its reasoning in tokens and part in latents, shifting the balance over the course of training until the reasoning is fully latent. That is a nontrivial training trick, because the naive move, take a model that reasons in tokens and freeze it into latent reasoning, would break the supervision signal, since you no longer have token labels for the reasoning steps, and the curriculum is how you keep a learnable target while you change the space. Stage 2 is information-gain reinforcement learning, which self-evolves the latent reasoning without any ground-truth supervision. The reward is an information gain, measuring how much the latent reasoning reduces uncertainty about the target, and it requires no external critic, no human labels, no separate verifier. That is the part that makes the approach scale, because the whole problem with reasoning in a continuous space is that you cannot label the intermediate reasoning steps, and the information-gain reward is a way to get a training signal for the reasoning process itself without labels.

The base model is BAGEL, a unified multimodal model, and they instantiate LARC on it with one million interleaved samples drawn from the public training sets. The results are across four task families, GenEval for text-to-image generation, DPG-Bench and OneIG-Bench for compositional generation, CompBench for image editing, and MIRA for visual reasoning. The headline is state-of-the-art text-to-image performance with a seven-percent relative gain over BAGEL on GenEval, where the overall score moves from 0.840 to 0.887, and the result they claim as the real contribution is test-time scaling: LARC is the first reasoning-augmented unified multimodal model to show clear test-time scaling for image generation, outperforming token-based generation under both parallel and sequential test-time scaling strategies. The per-task detail is where the claim has teeth: on DPG-Bench, the overall prompt-following score moves from 85.07 to 90.04, a plus five point eight gain over BAGEL, and on CompBench editing the local CLIP text score, LC-T, moves from 20.434 to 22.509, a plus ten point two percent relative gain, with pixel-level quality improving too, PSNR up from 24.3 to 25.7 and LPIPS down from 0.069 to 0.049, the single biggest relative improvement in the paper, and the paper reads that as evidence the continuous latent space lets the model plan spatially grounded edits, particularly for localized attribute modification. The training-stages ablation is the one that matters: Stage 1 alone, the curriculum SFT, lifts MIRA visual reasoning from 7.5 to 9.7 and the full two-stage system takes it to 11.7, so the conversion to latent reasoning is itself beneficial and the improvement is most pronounced on visual reasoning, and the training-strategy ablation shows the specific latent-reasoning variant that works, with the note that one variant slightly degrades visual reasoning compared to the baseline, which is the kind of honest negative result that tells you the method is not free.

A few things I would want to check. The seven-percent GenEval gain over BAGEL is a strong claim, and I would want to see the absolute numbers and confirm they are not coming from a harder-to-baseline setting, and more importantly I would want the test-time-scaling curve itself, because the claim that LARC is the first reasoning-augmented model to show test-time scaling for image generation is only as good as the scaling plot. The paper is specific that under budget forcing, doubling the number of latent steps produces the scaling behavior, and that is the right way to test it, but I would want to see how much additional inference compute that doubling requires, whether the curve plateaus, and whether the scaling holds under the sequential strategy as well as the parallel one, since the two put different pressure on the latent steps. The information-gain RL stage is the load-bearing piece and also the hardest to verify, because an information-gain reward with no external critic is the kind of thing that can look like it is training and quietly not be, and the reward is defined as how much each latent step improves the prediction, which is computed on the model's own hidden states, so I would want the reward curve and the evidence that the latent reasoning is actually changing, not just that the generation scores went up. And the MIRA visual-reasoning result cuts both ways, the curriculum SFT helps it, moving it from 7.5 to 11.7, but one latent-reasoning variant hurts it, so the method is not uniformly beneficial and the interaction between the reasoning space and the downstream task is not fully understood.

My verdict is that LARC is a clean and important idea, the modality-mismatch diagnosis of why token-space chain-of-thought does not transfer to image generation is right, and moving the reasoning into continuous space with a curriculum plus an unsupervised information-gain reward is a credible way to close that gap. But the evidence is thin, a seven-percent benchmark gain and a test-time-scaling claim that I would want to see plotted, and the unsupervised RL stage is exactly the part that is hardest to audit, so this is a promising direction with a plausible mechanism that I would want the scaling curves and the reward dynamics before I trusted that latent reasoning actually works the way the paper says.
