From Pattern Recognizers to Personalized Companions: A Survey of Large Language Models in Mental Health. The first three authors are Yuchen Li, Zihan Ma, and Yixiang Sun, from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and the Central South University, with a full team of eleven, including Peng Zhang, Jinfeng Zhou, and Xiang Wan.

This is a survey, and it is the kind of survey that earns its place by organizing a field that has been growing without a shared vocabulary. Large language models are being deployed across mental health: as screening tools, as conversational support systems, as therapeutic agents, as data annotators, and as research instruments for studying the language of mental states. But the field's literature is scattered across clinical psychology, computer science, and human-computer interaction, and the same system gets called a chatbot, an AI companion, a digital therapeutic, and a large language model agent depending on which journal it appears in. This survey's contribution is a three-phase taxonomy that maps the field's actual progression. Phase one is pattern recognition: models that classify or detect mental-health conditions from text, the depression-detection-in-social-media line of work, which predates the LLM era and now runs on LLM-based classifiers. Phase two is empathetic conversation: systems that hold a supportive dialogue, detect crisis signals, and respond with clinical-grade empathy, the Woebot and Replika family of products and their academic descendants. Phase three, which the survey argues is where the field is actually heading, is longitudinal personalized companions: stateful agents that maintain a persistent model of a specific user over time, with memory, personalization, and the ability to adapt their interventions as the user changes. The survey organizes this progression along four architectural axes, profile, which captures what the agent knows about the user; memory, which captures how that knowledge persists and is updated; reasoning, which captures how the agent decides what to say; and planning, which captures how it sequences interventions across a session or a treatment course.

The dataset and benchmark section is the part a practitioner will actually use. The survey catalogues the evaluation landscape and finds it in rough shape relative to the architecture research. Most Phase one systems are evaluated on small, single-condition classification datasets, often from social media, with the usual problems of label noise, demographic skew, and no holdout from a different population. Phase two conversation systems are evaluated on empathy benchmarks and crisis-detection benchmarks that are, by construction, single-turn or short-horizon, so a system that is excellent at a five-turn empathetic exchange and completely broken over five weeks is indistinguishable in the published numbers from one that works both ways. The Phase three evaluation problem is the most acute: longitudinal personalization is almost entirely unevaluated, because there are no public longitudinal datasets of mental-health users that would clear ethics review, so the field is evaluating stateful systems with single-session proxies. The survey lays out the open problems honestly. Safety is the dominant one: a mental-health agent that hallucinates a clinical assessment, or that misses a crisis signal in turn forty of a long session because its context window has compressed the early turns, is a different category of failure from a code-generation agent that hallucinates an API. The survey notes that most deployed systems use a rule-based crisis escalation to a human, and that this handoff is where the LLM's uncertainty, which it cannot reliably verbalize, meets a protocol that assumes it can.

The architecture section, organized by the four axes, is where the survey's synthesis is most useful. On profile, it distinguishes systems that build an explicit user model, a stored representation of the user's history, goals, and triggers, from systems that rely on implicit context, the rolling conversation window doing the work. The explicit-model camp has the advantage that personalization survives session boundaries and can be audited, at the cost of a maintenance problem: the profile is a lossy compression of everything the agent has learned about the user, and a wrong fact that persists in the profile is worse than a wrong fact in the current context, because it poisons every future session. On memory, the survey maps the spectrum from no memory, to sliding-window memory, to episodic stores that retrieve relevant past interactions, to consolidated long-term representations, and notes that almost no published mental-health system implements anything past the sliding window, which means the longitudinal-companion claim in most product marketing is not supported by the underlying architecture. On reasoning, it separates the clinical reasoning a system does to interpret a user's state, which is the part that needs domain grounding, from the response-generation reasoning, which is the part the base model is good at, and argues that conflating the two is why systems are good at sounding supportive and bad at being clinically appropriate. On planning, the survey is the most critical: almost no system in the field plans interventions across sessions at all, which is the gap between a chatbot that is kind and a therapeutic agent that treats, and it frames this as the defining open problem of Phase three.

The skeptical read of a survey is whether its taxonomy is useful or merely taxonomic. Here the three-phase framing is genuinely organizing, because it separates the detection work, which is a classification problem, from the conversation work, which is a dialogue problem, from the companion work, which is a stateful personalization problem, and those three problems have different evaluation needs and different failure modes, and the field has been blurring them. The four-axis architecture decomposition is less novel, it is essentially the standard agent-architecture decomposition applied to a clinical domain, but it is applied with domain specificity that a generic agent survey would not provide, and the honest treatment of the evaluation gap, that longitudinal personalization is almost entirely unevaluated, is the survey's most valuable paragraph, because it tells practitioners that the strongest published claims in the field are not supported by the strongest published evidence. The limitations are the usual ones for a fast-moving field: the taxonomy will need updating as Phase three systems actually ship, the safety discussion is normative in places where it should be empirical, and the survey, being a survey, does not itself resolve any of the open problems it catalogues, including the profile-persistence problem, the evaluation gap, and the crisis-handoff design. Bottom line: this is a well-organized map of a field that needed one, with a defensible three-phase taxonomy and an honest accounting of where the evidence does not support the claims, and it is the right starting point for anyone building a longitudinal mental-health agent, because it tells you precisely which architectural components are missing from every system you will find in the literature, and which evaluation you cannot do yet with public data.

One practical note on the datasets section: the survey is explicit that the field's best evaluation asset is not a benchmark but a handful of small clinical trial datasets, and that the gap between those, which measure outcomes with human raters over weeks, and the automated benchmarks, which measure turn-level quality with model or crowd raters, is the reason a system can score well on every published benchmark and still fail in a deployment, or the reverse. It also flags a methodological trap specific to this domain: empathy benchmarks reward warmth, and warmth is cheap to produce, so a benchmark optimized for empathetic tone will systematically favor systems that are supportive but clinically vague, which is the exact failure mode a mental-health deployment cannot afford, and no current benchmark penalizes it.

The accompanying GitHub repository, which the survey links and maintains, collects the datasets, benchmarks, and systems it catalogues, and is the survey's most durable contribution given how fast this field moves. For a builder, the practical sequence the survey implies is: pick a phase, find the gap in that phase's evaluation, and design the longitudinal study or benchmark that would close it, because the architecture components are the easy part, and the missing evaluation infrastructure is where the field's next real progress will show up.
