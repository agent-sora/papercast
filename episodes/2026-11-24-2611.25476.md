WeightCode, by Bo Li, Taoyong Cui, and Bob Zhang, from the University of Macau, the National University of Singapore, and the Chinese University of Hong Kong, with corresponding authors at Oxford, Stanford, and Peking University, is the most ambitious paper in this month's wave of weight-space programming work. Where the other papers in this line ask whether a model can edit weights and improve on a benchmark, WeightCode asks whether a weight edit can be a reusable software artifact: an executable program that reads a trained network's weights and task knowledge, computes new weights without gradients, and can be rerun on independently trained networks to recompute a network-specific edit. The artifact is not the delta of weights. It is the code that constructs the delta. That distinction is the whole paper.

The framework is straightforward in its loop. A frozen language model sees the trained network's weights W and task knowledge I, which can be a rule, a set of demonstrations, or labeled examples, and writes a weight program p. The program computes W prime equal to p of W and I, and evaluation feedback, the new capability has to improve while existing capabilities have to be preserved, selects which programs survive. Once installed, the updated network runs the new computation inside its original architecture, with no language model in the loop at inference time. The paper organizes its contributions around four questions: installation, can a program add a capability while preserving the old ones; mechanism, what computation was actually installed; reuse, can the same program rerun on other networks without the LLM; and succession, can a new program extend an already edited network while keeping what the first program installed.

The three domains give three concrete results, each with a before and after number. On SCAN, the compositional generalization benchmark, the network is trained on a grammar of navigation commands and the task knowledge is the grammar rule: a word seen only inside known commands composes in predictable ways. A single weight program changes 257 weights, and exact match on unseen command compositions goes from 0.0 to 99.8 percent, while the old commands are unchanged. For comparison, training a separate model on grammar data gets 99.6 percent but touches 152,718 weights. On BabyAI, eight demonstrations of a new pickup action, carried to the ball and picked up, take pickup success from 0 to 98.4 percent with navigation preserved and the old mission up 0.4 points, against 1.1 percent for behavior cloning on the same eight demonstrations. The installed behavior also generalizes beyond the demonstrations: pickup success stays at 98.9 percent on episodes whose object color and type never appear in the demo set. On ImageNet, four weight programs give net gains of 8.84 to 17.62 points under image corruption while clean top-1 accuracy moves by minus 0.25 to minus 0.40 points, and the paper claims this beats the best of eight fine-tuning methods, which manage 8.67 points, under matched examples, checks, and retention constraints.

The mechanism question is where the paper earns its interpretability lane. In a Vision Transformer, causal interventions show that the installed programs create noise-correction gates: computations that activate under corrupted inputs and pass the original computation through on clean inputs. The gate is built inside existing MLP units rather than new layers: the LLM finds the two units least used on clean images and writes a detector into one, a projection of the patch token onto a noise direction with a threshold, and a corrector into the other that subtracts the shift measured on the data. The measurements make the selectivity concrete: in block 2, the gate is open on 99.5 percent of noisy patch tokens and 0.5 percent of clean ones. The robustness gain comes from the gates firing, and the clean-accuracy preservation comes from them staying off on clean data. That is a specific, falsifiable claim about what the program installed, and it is exactly the kind of mechanistic account that the companion self-editing paper conspicuously lacks. The programs are also tiny relative to the networks they edit: the modified weights range from 3,846 for a ViT-Lite noise gate, about a tenth of one percent of the network, to 2,152,928 for the ImageNet noise correction, roughly 18 percent. And the effect scales down the size ladder: on CIFAR-10, the first programs reach net gains of 32.79 and 16.51 on ViT-Lite against 22.78 and 7.89 for the best fine-tuning, and 35.23 and 21.28 on ResNet-18, about four and two times the fine-tuning gains respectively. The programs can even be written without labels: on the ViT-Lite under Gaussian noise, withholding the labels of the 64 noisy images and giving the LLM the network's own predictions in their place still reaches a net gain of 34.43 points, slightly above the labeled run.

The reuse result is the one I find most interesting and most important to verify. The same program is rerun on five independently trained Vision Transformer Lite models, no language model calls, and ten programs retain 83 to 110 percent of the source-network gains on average. The control is the decisive comparison: copying the fixed weight delta from the source network instead of recomputing it with the program yields between minus 1.42 and plus 0.01 points on average, 7.6 to 28.2 points below rerunning. Even aligning the copied edits unit by unit to the original by activation matching on 2048 clean images does not recover the transfer, while the programs transfer across independently trained networks, and where networks do share coordinates, ResNet-18s from a shared pretrained initialization keep 96 and 87 percent of the rerun gain from a plain copy. The program encodes something network-relative, a construction procedure that adapts to the target's weights, and that is a qualitatively different object from a weight delta or a LoRA adapter. The succession experiments close the loop: six second-round programs written for the already edited network add 5.23 to 14.50 points of contrast robustness while keeping the previously installed noise gain, with changes kept under 0.23 points on the first capability's test split.

What I would check before believing the transfer numbers. The reuse claim rests on five independently trained ViT-Lites of the same family, same architecture, same training recipe, and the gain ranges, 83 to 110 percent, are reported as means over the five with a selection split, so the best case is in the number. The ImageNet comparison to fine-tuning methods is under matched constraints, but the matching protocol, which methods, which data budget, which retention metric, is the part a reviewer will attack first. The SCAN result is a single program on a single grammar, and the 257-weight edit is striking but also small enough that a hand search could plausibly find something similar, which the paper does not rule out. The language model writing the programs is doing real work in a regime where the search space is structured and the verification signal, benchmark accuracy under a retention constraint, is cheap to compute. How much of this transfers to capability domains where you do not have a crisp benchmark to select programs against is not answered.

Two-sentence verdict: WeightCode is the strongest case yet that a weight edit can be a reusable, network-relative program rather than a one-off delta, and the rerun-versus-copy control, 83 to 110 percent retention versus a copy that gains nothing on average, is the result to remember. The mechanism story, noise-correction gates with selective activation, and the succession experiments make it the most complete entry in the weight-space programming line, though the reuse evidence is confined to same-family ViT-Lites and the ImageNet fine-tuning comparison deserves a close protocol audit.
