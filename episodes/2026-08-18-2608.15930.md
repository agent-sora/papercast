## Cold Open

Today's paper is "UI-Mate: A Self-Evolving Companion for Computer-Use Agents," by Zihan Ding, Longxu Dou, Qi Gao, and twenty-six colleagues spanning Tencent's Hy Frontier team — twenty-nine authors in all. The pitch matches the name: rather than train yet another larger computer-use model, they built a companion that rides along with one, watches it operate, steps in the moment it stalls, and remembers every rescue so the next episode starts smarter. The headline claims are striking — 77.0 percent on OSWorld-Verified, reportedly the best open-weight result going, and a single demonstration doubling an agent's odds on tasks it currently fails. Both deserve scrutiny, so let's take them apart.

## The Premise

A computer-use agent is a language model driving a graphical interface the way you do: it reads a screenshot of the screen, decides what to do next, and then clicks, types, or scrolls. Benchmarks such as OSWorld and WindowsAgentArena grade these systems on genuine office work — editing spreadsheets, filing mail, changing system settings — inside live operating systems rather than toy replicas. Scores keep climbing, but the failure signature in the traces is remarkably stable: the agent behaves competently for twenty steps, then loops on the same wrong click, wanders off-target, or quits partway through, and it essentially never recovers by itself. Restarting the task from scratch throws away every correct step already taken — and each retry re-rolls every downstream decision, so a long task can burn attempt after attempt failing somewhere new each time. The usual responses are more parameters, longer chains of reasoning, and heavier orchestration around the model. This paper starts somewhere else: a stuck agent typically needs exactly one corrective nudge — a demonstration of the right move — after which it can finish the job on its own. That reframes the problem architecturally. What if a second system supplied that nudge automatically whenever it was needed, and wrote it down afterward?

## How It Works

UI-Mate runs alongside a base agent as a monitor and intervener. While the primary model works through a task, the companion watches the unfolding trajectory for trouble signals — repeated identical actions, wandering between unrelated interface elements, progress that stops advancing. When it sees a stall, it intervenes, and its most powerful intervention is a demonstration: the companion briefly takes control, performs the correct sub-procedure on the real interface, and hands control back for the base agent to continue. Because the takeover plays out on the same live screen, what gets banked is an executable trace of real clicks and keystrokes rather than verbal advice the base model must reinterpret — the rescue is replayable, not paraphrased. Crucially, the demonstration is not thrown away. It is stored in a persistent memory indexed by task pattern and application, so subsequent episodes can retrieve it before trouble even starts. Memory turns incidents into instincts: what cost an intervention today costs nothing tomorrow, because the fix arrives before the stall does. That retrieval loop is what earns the "self-evolving" label: the system's effective capability grows with hours worked rather than with additional pretraining. The team also built a demonstration-conditioned variant, DemoCUA, whose action generation is explicitly grounded in retrieved examples rather than improvised from the raw screen. The two modes trade immediacy for integration: vanilla UI-Mate lends a hand mid-episode, while DemoCUA folds retrieved precedent directly into action generation. The appendix walks through real deployments on production surfaces — a Salesforce-and-Slack workflow is spelled out step by step — so this is live software operating real applications, not a simulated desktop. The deployed task mix matches that breadth — office suites, browsers, mail clients, code editors, operating-system settings, plus enterprise surfaces like the Salesforce-and-Slack workflow reconstructed click by click in the appendix.

## The Numbers

The flagship results: 77.0 percent on OSWorld-Verified and 66.2 percent on WindowsAgentArena, the latter ahead of Kimi K2.6's 63.3 percent and described as the strongest open-weight showing on that suite — a 2.9-point margin over the nearest open rival. On OSWorkerBench, a long-horizon office benchmark, at the 27-billion-parameter scale the system posts 41.00 percent strict success against 76.86 percent task progress — a 35.86-point chasm between doing the work and closing it out. Progress acts as partial credit here, rewarding whatever share of the required work survives at cutoff, which is precisely why this gap is diagnostic rather than merely embarrassing. The authors' own failure analysis blames late-stage omissions: the agent executes nearly the entire workflow, then skips a final confirmation or submission step, and the strict evaluator scores the whole thing as a miss. The demonstration effect is the most interesting number in the paper: adding one demonstration lifts strict success from 17.2 to 35.4 percent. At the small end the effect is dramatic — a 9-billion-parameter model climbs from 5.05 to 34.00 percent, close to a sevenfold gain bought with a single worked example. The gradient runs one way: the weaker the base policy, the more a lone corrective example buys — consistent with the companion supplying exactly the procedural knowledge the small model never learned, rather than lending generic confidence. Results are broken out across parameter scales — nine-billion and twenty-seven-billion backbones among them, each reported with and without retrieved demonstrations — which is what lets the demonstration deltas read as causal rather than cosmetic. On cost, median decision turns come in at 71 for UI-Mate, 68 for Kimi K2.6, and 42.5 for GPT-5.6-Sol: comparable budgets to the nearest open competitor, and the larger counts against the frontier system partly reflect persistence, since an agent that abandons difficult tasks early looks wonderfully efficient on average.

## What Holds Up — and What Doesn't

Credit where due: this team interrogated its own pipeline harder than most. They audited the benchmark evaluators they were being graded by and published what they found, including the admission that even after their corrections roughly eighteen percent of the audited checks still misjudged task outcomes. They report that reinforcement-learning finetuning ran into entropy collapse — the policy distribution sharpening until exploration effectively stopped — meaning naive RL on top of a competent supervised agent can freeze behavior rather than improve it. And they disclose that one efficiency-focused component bought latency improvements without moving success rates at all — their PCM efficiency module sped up decisions while doing nothing for accuracy, and they say so plainly. Those are unfashionable results, and printing them builds trust. The skepticism lands elsewhere. First, that evaluator audit undermines the whole genre: if roughly one audited check in six still judges wrongly, every cross-paper leaderboard comparison — theirs included — carries noise nobody prices in, and "open-weight state of the art" is really a claim about harness plus judge, not just the model. Second, the self-evolving mechanism in the very title is the thinnest-evidenced piece: the variant-demonstration study offers no aggregate benchmark number at all, only a ten-task pilot that the authors themselves decline to treat as evidence, calling it not yet sufficiently stable for a main benchmark claim. For a mechanism marketed in the system's own name, that is a conspicuous evidentiary hole. That is admirably honest and scientifically unsatisfying in equal measure — the boldest idea in the paper is currently carried by anecdotes. Third, the demonstration gains, while large, are measured within the authors' own harness and judge; given point one, independent replication matters more than usual here — until somebody swaps harnesses and judges across labs, rankings in this entire genre deserve a provisional asterisk.

## Verdict

UI-Mate makes a persuasive case that companionship — watching, intervening, remembering — squeezes far more capability out of fixed weights than another scaling round, and the single-demonstration result is the cleanest evidence yet that one human-quality correction can double an agent's success rate. The self-evolution story remains promise rather than proof, resting today on a ten-task pilot its own authors will not defend, so read this as a strong engineering result carrying a genuinely interesting open question.
