ToolSpace: Scaling Tool-Centric Environment Synthesis for Long-Horizon Agents, by Guanting Dong, Zhichao Sun, and Xinyu Yang, with equal contribution from a large group across Renmin University of China, Fudan University, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University, is a method for synthesizing training environments for long-horizon tool-using agents. The starting observation is that existing ways to scale up model context protocol environments are data-centric: you first synthesize a database and a scenario, and then you derive the tools and the tasks from them. The consequence is that the tool interfaces may not match the real ones, the tool compositions are not grounded in anything that actually executes, and the tasks rarely span more than one environment. ToolSpace reverses the order. It is tool-centric, and it builds the environments out of observed tool composition rather than inventing them from scratch.

The pipeline works in stages. First it mines execution traces from real model context protocol services and open agent corpora, and from adjacent calls in those traces it extracts data dependencies, state dependencies, and retry dependencies using deterministic rules rather than large model judgment. That is a deliberate choice, and the paper returns to it in the ablations. Concretely, if one tool's output value appears as an input to the next call in a recorded session, that is a data edge; if the next call is a retry of the same operation after a failure, that is a retry edge; and if the second call reads or writes state that the first call changed, that is a state edge. Those three edge types form a weighted tool-composition graph, which is then clustered into environments. Databases are synthesized only for environments that mutate state, while query tools and stateless tools just replay recorded returns, which keeps the synthetic environment cheap and grounded. The core generation step is a state-aware random walk with rollback over the graph, and each walk is decompiled into a natural-language task with assertion-based checks. Only samples that replay successfully from the initial state are kept, so every retained task is executable end to end. The assertions can be binary pass-fail or graded, which lets the pipeline express partial-credit tasks without inventing a rubric by hand.

The scale numbers are concrete. Across the mined services they find 804 environments with assigned tools, and they separate 453 operational tools, which actually mutate records, from 1589 non-operational tools that only query. Document and design is the largest domain, accounting for 27.4 percent of environments. The tasks that come out are genuinely long-horizon: with the default settings, at least 66.2 percent of trajectories involve two or more environments, which is the cross-environment property that the data-centric baseline methods largely fail to produce. They report that 69 percent of the mined edges are retry edges, 25 percent are data edges, and 6 percent are state edges, so the composition graph is dominated by tools that get retried, which is a realistic property of real agent traffic.

The headline result is that the synthesized data makes the benchmarks harder and the models better. The full pipeline synthesizes 34,376 trajectories in total, from which they derive 34,376 supervised fine-tuning samples and 653 reinforcement learning samples. The reinforcement learning candidates undergo an additional Pass@16 screening with the 35-billion model, keeping only candidates with one or two correct rollouts, because those are the informative ones, the tasks that are solvable but not trivially so. Applied to AutomationBench and the banking suite of tau-cubed-bench, the pipeline produces harder Pro versions of those benchmarks, with AutomationBench Pro coming out to 300 tasks, on which seven frontier models drop by up to 38.2 points. That is the evaluation-side finding: the synthesized tasks expose a real gap. The mechanism is visible in the tool-call counts, which rise sharply for every model on the Pro versions, confirming that the Pro tasks genuinely require more tool use rather than just being harder to parse. On the training side, across seven long-horizon tool-use benchmarks, ToolSpace raises a 35-billion-parameter Qwen3.6 model from an average score of 23.0 to 41.0 with supervised fine-tuning alone, and to 43.2 with reinforcement learning on top. A GLM-5.2 model goes from 44.6 to 56.6. Their largest model, ToolSpace-744B-A40B, surpasses GPT-5.6 Sol in average score at 56.6 versus a lower figure for the baseline. On AutomationBench specifically the 35-billion model rises by more than 35 points, from 8.2 to 45.8, which is the single largest number in the paper. The training configuration is modest and reproducible: the 8-billion and 35-billion models are trained for one epoch on 16 NVIDIA GPUs, and the 744-billion model for one epoch on a larger pool, so the gains are not a function of an exotic training budget.

The ablations are where the engineering judgment shows. Execution-witnessed edges, meaning dependencies that were actually observed in a real execution trace, clearly outperform edges that were judged by a large model, which validates the decision to use deterministic rules for mining. They also stress-test the generation mechanism itself: the chains must be witnessed by execution rather than merely plausible, and the rollback plus edge-weighted sampling is what keeps the walks from wandering into infeasible states. Performance keeps improving as the data and environment scale grow, and they show an AutomationBench gain of about 4 points per doubling of data beyond the one-eighth fraction, despite a plateau, which means the pipeline has headroom but is starting to hit diminishing returns at the scale they tested. They also report the inter-rater agreement for their human task validation is strong, with Fleiss's kappa at 0.82 and observed agreement of 93.1 percent, which supports the claim that the synthesized tasks are coherent and not just mechanically valid. Reinforcement learning adds a further 2.2 points at the 35-billion scale on top of supervised fine-tuning, so the gains are not purely a data artifact. One comparison worth noting: they benchmark against other environment-scaling systems, including EnvScaler-8B, AWM-8B, and a Simulator-style baseline, and ToolSpace holds the lead within each same-scale pool, which rules out the simplest explanation that the gains are just a function of using more data.

The skeptical read has a few teeth. The whole method is a synthesis pipeline, and the quality ceiling is set by the execution traces it mines, so it inherits the distribution of whatever real services and agent corpora it is fed. If those traces are dominated by a handful of document and design workloads, the resulting environments will be too, even if the clustering looks diverse. The operational-tool split is a warning sign on this point: only 22.2 percent of the mined tools actually mutate state, so the bulk of the environment is query-heavy, and the tasks, while long-horizon, are still anchored in the read-mostly behavior of real traffic. The Pro-benchmark drops of up to 38.2 points are impressive but they are a statement about their own harder tasks, not about general agent capability, and a benchmark that is harder because it is more adversarial to the models that built it is a weaker claim than it reads. The cross-model transfer, from a 35-billion model to a 744-billion model, is the most valuable result for a reader who does not want to train a 744-billion model, because it suggests the data is doing real work across scales. But the absolute scores on the seven benchmarks are what they are, and 41.0 average for a 35-billion model on long-horizon tool use is strong but not a solved problem.

Bottom line: ToolSpace is a well-motivated reversal of the data-centric synthesis order, and the deterministic, execution-grounded mining plus the replay-only-what-executes filter are the right instincts for building trustworthy agent training data. The scale-up results and the cross-scale transfer are the parts to trust, while the 38-point Pro drops should be read as a capability probe rather than a universal leaderboard. If you are training long-horizon tool agents, this is a pipeline worth studying closely, but the ceiling is the trace distribution you feed it.
