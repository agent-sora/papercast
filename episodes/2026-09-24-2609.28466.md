The Past Frames the Future: Memory for Autoregressive Video Generation. The first three authors are Harold Haodong Chen, Rongjin Guo, and Disen Lan, from HKUST, Carnegie Mellon University, and New York University, among a roster that runs to twenty-five people with core supervisors at NVIDIA and elsewhere.

Autoregressive video generation extends a clip one step at a time: predict the next frame or chunk from everything generated so far, append it, repeat. It is the natural paradigm for anything long-horizon, interactive, or open-ended, and for a few years it looked like the only serious route to video that goes on forever. Then the field ran into its fundamental bottleneck, and this survey is an attempt to organize the response to it. The bottleneck is memory in the strictest sense. A practical model has a bounded context window, bounded storage, and bounded compute. Critical historical information, the identity of a character, the layout of a room, the state of an object after it was knocked over, the consequences of an intervention, routinely leaves the active context long before it stops mattering. The survey's operational definition is clean: a state functions as memory when its removal or modification changes later outputs, even after the originating evidence is no longer locally available. Note what that definition does not do. It does not draw a line between memory and long context. Recent tokens densely consumed at every step are active context; as information is selectively retained, compressed, retrieved, consolidated, or revised beyond the local window, it becomes memory. The boundary is functional, not a window size, which is the right call, because the field has been using the word memory for mechanisms that differ enormously.

The organizing structure is five perspectives. Forms: what carries the memory. Functions: what it preserves. Operations: the lifecycle. Learning: how the behavior is optimized. Evaluation: how you diagnose whether a model actually has memory or is just coasting on long context. The forms taxonomy is the most useful part. Visual memory lives either in pixel space or in VAE space, meaning the model caches rendered frames or their compressed latent versions. Implicit state memory is the transformer-native option: attention cache states, the rolling KV cache and its retention variants; recurrent and state-space states, which is where SSM and linear-attention style architectures show up; and encoded history states, a dedicated encoder that compresses the past into a fixed-size summary. Explicit state memory is the structured camp: entity-centric states that track individual objects and their attributes, and spatial or geometric states like scene graphs and 3D structure. Finally, adaptive parametric memory, where the memory lives in the weights themselves, either as internal plasticity or as modular plug-in parameters. The functions side names five preservation targets: identity, spatial, dynamic, semantic, and causal, and the survey insists these are coupled, so a system that nails identity while dropping dynamics is not a memory system, it is a lookup table. The operations section walks the full lifecycle, writing, reading, updating, managing, and integrating, and the learning section is where the honest engineering lives: teacher forcing on ground-truth history, history augmentation, and self-rollout, where the model trains on its own generated past, which is the only regime that matches deployment, because at inference time the history is the model's own output and the error compounds.

The evaluation section is the part I would actually go read first. The authors argue that most current evidence of memory in video models is weak, because the standard protocol, show a model a prompt and watch it drift, does not isolate memory from competence. They lay out levels of memory evidence, from benchmark setups where the relevant information is still inside the context window, which tests nothing about retention, through setups that force the evidence out of the window, to validity controls that check the model is not just pattern-matching on the prompt. They also flag the attribution problem: a score improvement after adding a memory module could come from extra compute, extra parameters, or extra context, not from the memory per se. The open challenges they close with are specific: composable and resource-aware memory architectures, because the current menu is either an ever-growing cache or a blunt compression; trustworthy state updating, because a memory that cannot be revised is a liability; self-rollout learning at scale; and standardized evaluation, which they admit does not exist yet. There is a companion GitHub repository tracking the literature.

The skeptical read. This is a survey, so the bar is not a result, it is whether the taxonomy does work that you could not do yourself with a folder of PDFs, and on that I give it credit. The five-perspective frame is genuinely useful, the operational definition is defensible, and the insistence that evaluation must force evidence out of the local context is the single most important sentence in the paper, because it is a statement about why a lot of recent memory-in-video claims are unfalsifiable. What it does not give you is a leaderboard, a benchmark, or a method, and it does not resolve the central trade-off, which is that every form on the menu pays somewhere: caches grow, state-space states lose fine detail, entity tracking needs an object detector upstream, and parametric plasticity is still the least understood option. If you are building long-horizon or interactive video generation, this is the right map to hang on the wall, but treat the open-challenges section as the actual content and the rest as the tour. Verdict: a well-organized, genuinely useful map of a field that has outgrown its terminology, with the sharpest contribution in its evaluation critique rather than its taxonomy.

A few concrete threads from the taxonomy are worth pulling out, because they map directly onto design decisions you would make this quarter. The attention-cache camp, the largest in practice, ranges from naive rolling windows that discard old key-value pairs, through retention schemes that score each cache entry and evict the least useful, to hierarchical caches that keep a small set of recent frames at full fidelity and a compressed archive of the rest. The survey's observation is that retention scores are almost always learned weakly, either with a proxy loss or with no training at all, which means the eviction policy, the single biggest lever on long-horizon consistency, is typically hand-tuned. The entity-centric camp has the opposite failure mode: it depends on a perception stack upstream, an object tracker that identifies, localizes, and re-identifies entities across frames, and every error in that stack is a permanent corruption of the memory. The survey catalogs a growing middle path, where the generative model itself emits lightweight structure, predicted boxes or segment masks, that feeds its own entity store, but notes this is still early and the structure is usually noisier than the perception stack it replaces.

The learning section deserves more than a sentence, because it is where the gap between papers and products is widest. Teacher forcing, training on ground-truth history, is cheap and stable, but it trains on a distribution the model never sees at inference time. Self-rollout, where the model generates its own history and conditions on it, is the honest protocol, and the survey documents the standard failure: errors in generated frames become the conditioning for the next frame, so the training signal degrades as the rollout gets longer, a version of exposure bias with a much larger blast radius, because a wrong frame is not a wrong word, it is a corrupted world state that every subsequent step builds on. History augmentation, mixing ground truth and self-generated history, is the pragmatic middle, and the survey's framing is that no one has yet established how much self-rollout a model needs before its memory behavior stabilizes, which is an empirical question the community has not answered. The memory-aware learning subsection lists four skills a trained system should exhibit: learning to retain, deciding what is worth keeping; learning to compress, collapsing the rest; learning to retrieve, finding what is kept when it becomes relevant again; and learning to adapt, revising stored state when new evidence arrives. The last one, adaptation, is the least developed, and the survey is explicit that most systems can write and read but cannot edit.
