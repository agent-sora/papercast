RoughCut-Bench, by Ruisi Zhao, You Li, and Sihan Chen, from Zhejiang University and ByteDance, is a benchmark for a specific and practical stage of video production that large vision-language models have not been properly tested on: the rough cut. A rough cut is the first pass that organizes raw media materials into an initial cut, selecting segments and arranging them into a coherent sequence before the later fine-cut stage adds captions, music, and transitions. The paper argues that automatic rough cut is underexplored because there is no comprehensive benchmark for it and no mature workflow for producing executable cut plans, and it introduces both. The headline finding, reported plainly, is that current large vision-language models remain limited in producing expert-aligned cut plans.

The benchmark covers two practical settings. The first is a visually driven setting over video cases, which covers highlight detection and selection, ordering of source videos, and adherence to user requirements when they are provided. The second is a speech driven setting over automatic speech recognition transcripts, which covers filler removal, expression deduplication, and trimming of low-value content. The scale of the visual setting is 101 visually driven cases comprising 831 raw videos with 1,476 professional highlight annotations, spanning six real-world scenarios like outdoor vlogs and daily-life scenes. Each case is annotated with highlight timestamps and a reference source-video order, and 39 of the cases additionally include user requirements such as a target duration, a preferred source order, or specified highlights to keep or remove, which enables requirement-constrained evaluation. The speech setting has 84 cases derived from talking-head videos, each an ASR transcript with annotated cleanup spans.

The evaluation design is where the paper is most careful, and it is worth describing because it addresses a real weakness in how video models are usually scored. For the speech-driven setting, annotation-based metrics are well suited, because the cleanup decisions map directly to span annotations. For the visual setting, annotation-based metrics are not enough, for two reasons the paper states clearly: they evaluate the structured cut plan without ever looking at the rendered video, and a single professional reference does not represent all reasonable cuts. So the paper introduces a human-aligned agentic evaluator that assigns each rendered cut a visual quality score while considering the user requirements, and it iteratively refines that evaluator by comparing its scores and reasons against those given by professionals. That is a reasonable design, and the human-alignment step is the part that makes it defensible rather than just another model scoring model output.

The method side proposes agentic workflows that guide large vision-language models to produce structured cut plans. For the visual setting, there is a video-understanding agent that identifies candidate highlights with reasons and visual descriptions, and a cut-planning agent that reasons across those candidates to produce the final plan, including highlight selection and source ordering. The split exists because a single model asked to handle long multi-video contexts and the compositional ordering decision at once does not do well. For the speech setting, a transcript-cleanup agent reads each complete ASR transcript and outputs structured cleanup records with reasons and a cleaned transcript. The paper reports ablation studies that validate the workflow design, and a professional user study.

Now the skeptical read, and for a benchmark paper the skepticism has to be aimed at the benchmark itself. First, the scale is modest: 101 visual cases and 84 speech cases is a diagnostic suite, not a large-scale evaluation, and the conclusion that "current LVLMs remain limited" is a statement about a narrow set of professionally annotated scenarios. The benchmark is useful for diagnosing the specific capabilities of highlight selection, ordering, and requirement adherence, but it is not a broad test of video understanding, and a model that scores poorly here might still be strong on the temporal grounding and highlight detection benchmarks that already exist. The paper acknowledges this is a complementary evaluation, not a replacement. Second, the human-aligned agentic evaluator is a double-edged tool: it is necessary because cut quality cannot be fully captured by annotation metrics, but it means part of the score is produced by an LLM that has been tuned to agree with professionals, and the residual gap between that evaluator and true professional judgment is exactly the uncertainty the paper cannot eliminate. The iterative alignment loop mitigates it but does not close it. Third, the benchmark is built around a specific production workflow, the rough-cut of short-form personal content, so its transfer to other video editing tasks is an open question.

That said, the paper is doing something the field actually needed, which is to turn a real production stage into a testable, annotated, and workflow-grounded evaluation. The two-setting structure, separating the visual compositional problem from the spoken-content cleanup problem, is the right decomposition, and the requirement-constrained subset of 39 cases is a nice touch that tests instruction-following on top of content selection. The honest negative result, that current models are limited, is the kind of finding that sets up future work, and the fact that the authors release the benchmark, code, and prompts means it is immediately usable.

The evaluation protocol is where the paper earns its keep, and the two settings are scored differently for a stated reason. In the speech-driven setting, the cleanup decisions map directly to annotated spans, so the annotation-based metrics are a fair and complete measure: did the model remove the right fillers, deduplicate the repeated expressions, and trim the low-value segments. In the visual setting, the same metrics are necessary but not sufficient, because a cut plan can be structurally correct on paper and still look wrong when rendered, and because a single professional reference cut does not capture all the reasonable ways to assemble the same materials. The human-aligned agentic evaluator fills that gap by scoring the rendered cut, including whether it honored the user requirements in the 39 requirement-constrained cases, and the alignment is done iteratively: the evaluator's scores and reasons are compared against professional judgments and the evaluator is refined until the two agree more closely. That is the right design for a subjective-quality dimension, and it is the part of the benchmark that will do the most work in future comparisons. The workflow side has a matching two-agent structure for the visual setting, a video-understanding agent that proposes candidate highlights with visual descriptions and a cut-planning agent that makes the compositional selection and ordering decisions, which the ablations are designed to validate, and the speech setting uses a single transcript-cleanup agent that outputs structured cleanup records with reasons. The professional user study rounds out the evaluation, and the authors release the benchmark, the code, and the prompts, which is what makes the whole thing usable rather than just a paper. The six visual scenarios, including outdoor vlogs and daily-life scenes, and the talking-head scenarios in the speech setting, like beauty and fashion and lifestyle content, are drawn from real production workflows rather than synthetic data, which is the property that distinguishes this benchmark from a collection of randomly assembled clips. The 101 cases and 831 raw videos cover the full spread of production material, from travel and outdoor vlogs to food and daily-life content, and the 1,476 highlight annotations are the professional ground truth that the cut plans are scored against.

My verdict: RoughCut-Bench is a well-constructed, honest benchmark paper that fills a genuine gap in the evaluation of video-production models, and its main finding, that current large vision-language models are not yet reliable at expert-aligned rough cutting, is a useful and correctly-scoped negative result. The limitations are the usual ones for a benchmark of this size, but they are stated rather than hidden. Worth using if you work on video editing or media generation pipelines, and worth citing as the reference point for rough-cut evaluation going forward.
