JIT-Agent, from Guibin Zhang, Leo Lu, Fangzhou Xie, and colleagues at the LV-NUS Lab in Singapore — a paper whose one-line pitch is an equation: agent capability equals model times harness, so stop hand-designing harnesses and train a model to write them. The authors observe that harness engineering has become the field's most labor-intensive craft: Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, and yes — Hermes, which their related-work table lists among thirteen representative scaffolds they re-implemented as seed data — are all fixed, ahead-of-time artifacts, tuned once and shipped to every task. The alternative this paper builds is called Model-as-a-Harness: take an off-the-shelf agentic LLM and train a meta-agent that synthesizes a task-specific, executable harness at deployment time, compiled fresh per task like just-in-time code. Hence the name.

The formalism earns its keep. A harness is defined as a composable four-tuple: memory module, planning structure, action space, and capability orchestration, all under a fixed protocol. Classic ReAct becomes full-context memory, no planner, direct actions, monolithic orchestration. Codex-style coding agents become compacted memory plus a todo-list planner. Recursive multi-agent frameworks become subproblem memory, decomposition planning, recursive actions, routing orchestration. Thirteen real scaffolds are factorized into coordinates in this design space and serve as the seed bank. The meta-agent's job is to navigate that space: given a task description, generate a harness instance — actual executable code for memory, planning, tool dispatch — rather than pick one off the shelf. Two inference modes follow: static mode generates several candidates and selects one before execution; streaming mode lets each completed task feed experience back into a growing archive bank.

Training proceeds in three stages on a Qwen 3.6 twenty-seven-billion-parameter base, producing JIT-Agent-twenty-seven-B. Stage one, customization: a teacher model synthesizes task-adapted harnesses, then supervised fine-tuning plus a preference objective where one candidate beats another only if reward rises while latency and cost do not worsen, with at least one strictly better — a reference-anchored DPO-style formulation that refuses to trade quality for efficiency blind. Stage two, repair: failed generations become bounded repair trajectories — compiler errors, interface mismatches, runtime failures get structured diagnostics, a teacher emits patches, and only fixes validating within two rounds survive into training data. That repair loop matters more than it sounds, because generated harnesses that crash on first contact teach the model nothing except despair. Stage three, evolution: Evo-GDPO groups candidate harnesses against the incumbent champion from the archive, normalizes three channels separately — reward, latency, cost — gates efficiency bonuses behind actually matching the incumbent's reward bar, and applies PPO-clipped updates with KL anchoring to the stage-two checkpoint. Bank retention policy: a new harness joins only if it matches or beats the reward frontier and strictly improves at least one dimension. At deployment the model itself freezes; only the bank evolves.

That frozen-model arrangement deserves a slower look because it reverses the usual division of labor. Conventional routes to a better agent either fine-tune weights — expensive, finicky, and prone to forgetting last month's skills — or lean on humans to hand-patch the scaffold between releases. JIT-Agent splits the problem: cognition lives in frozen weights, plumbing lives in the evolving bank, and every completed task can deposit evidence about what worked into that bank. In streaming mode the next task arrives already served by harnesses shaped by everything that came before, so the system keeps improving in deployment even though no gradient ever touches the base model again. The static mode has its own clever trick: generate several candidate harnesses and pick one before execution buys test-time scaling benefits without spending any extra environment rollouts — the expensive exploration happens in cheap code space rather than in slow task trials.

The evolution objective also repays scrutiny, since multi-objective training is where most agent-RL papers quietly cheat. Collapse reward, latency, and cost into one weighted scalar and the obvious failure mode is a lazy optimum — tiny harnesses that spend nothing and accomplish little. Evo-GDPO defends against this with two-level normalization: candidates are first compared within their generation group, then across a batch, so no single dominant batch skews the scale of any channel. Efficiency bonuses are gated behind actually matching the incumbent's reward bar — you collect the cost dividend only after clearing the quality hurdle — and the KL anchor back to the stage-two checkpoint keeps evolution from wandering into reward-hacking detours. Combined with the monotone retention rule, the archive behaves like a ratchet: quality floor never drops, some dimension must strictly improve, and junk can enter only by outperforming a champion.

The four-tuple is also what makes synthesis tractable in the first place, a point worth dwelling on for anyone who has actually maintained an agent codebase. Without coordinates, "design a harness" is open-ended creative writing — the kind of task language models approach with confident incoherence, producing plausible Python that fails to compose. With memory, planning, actions, and orchestration as typed slots under one fixed protocol, generation becomes structured: the meta-agent composes known primitives into novel arrangements rather than inventing machinery from raw text. It inherits the same lesson that made neural architecture search work — constrain the space hard enough that search becomes meaningful. And because all thirteen seed scaffolds compile against that same protocol, everything in the bank stays interoperable by construction; any generated harness can invoke another's memory module as a component, which is how the recursive designs recombine rather than just coexist.

Results span nine benchmarks across four workload types: deep research — Browse Comp Plus, DeepSearchQA, xBench-DS — daily work assistants, long-horizon planning including shopping-cart and travel-constraint tasks, and workspace productivity suites OfficeBench and OdysseyBench. Across all eighteen matched backbone-benchmark pairs with GLM 5.2 and DeepSeek V4 Flash, adding the trained meta-agent improves every single one: GLM average climbs seven point seven points to eighty-one point eight; DeepSeek-Flash climbs eight point eight to seventy-five point five. The largest single lifts are in planning: DeepPlanning-Shopping jumps twenty-four point eight points, travel-constraint satisfaction jumps twenty point two. And there is a genuinely quotable cell: DeepSeek-V4-Flash equipped with a generated harness reaches eighty-five point one F1 on DeepSearchQA, surpassing GPT-5.6 by nine point one points — a mid-tier open-weights model outranking a frontier flagship because it runs inside a better-fitted container.

The controlled-cost story may matter even more than raw accuracy, because Table Four holds the backbone constant and varies only the harness, letting them isolate what synthesis contributes economically. In four of six settings the generated harness posts best-in-class accuracy; where it trails fixed scaffolds, deficits are small and paid back in tokens — it loses to Claude Code on one benchmark by three point one points while spending fewer of them. On cost, it is cheapest in all six settings: thirty-six percent cheaper than the cheapest fixed harness on average, ranging up to fifty-four percent savings. The flagship cell turns five hundred twenty-seven thousand tokens into two hundred twelve thousand on XBench deep search, cutting seventy-five thousandths of dollars to thirty-nine, while score actually rises four points. Scaling checks across DeepSeek, Qwen, and Mimo checkpoints confirm the pattern survives model family changes, with the largest single lift — twenty-two points on DeepSearchQA — landing on a Mimo Pro checkpoint that started weakest.

Skeptical assessment. First, the seed bank is authored by the same lab that evaluates, and thirteen reimplemented scaffolds carry implementation-quality risk — though publishing the bank and naming baselines honestly, Hermes included, mitigates the usual fear. Second, benchmark fit: nine benchmarks were also the training signal's source domains via teacher synthesis, so generalization beyond these families remains demonstrated mainly through the scale grid, not unseen task types. Third, static versus streaming asymmetry: the headline numbers come from configurations where selection happens after candidate generation, which spends extra inference compute before the first environment step; the paper counts harness-synthesis tokens in costs, which is to their credit, but static-mode gains partly purchase themselves with that overhead. Fourth, all evaluation benchmarks here are text-and-tool tasks; whether per-task compilation helps embodied or latency-critical agents stays open. None of this dims the central result: treat the harness not as craft but as a learned function of the task, optimize it with the same preference machinery you would apply to the model, and mid-tier models start beating flagships — not by getting smarter, but by getting dressed properly. For anyone maintaining an agent pipeline by hand, the uncomfortable implication is that your scaffolding is now, officially, a trainable artifact.
