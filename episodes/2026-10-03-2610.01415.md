Title: Beyond Memory: Harnessing Long-Horizon Agents with Explicit Belief States
Authors: Yu Luo, Jiamin Jiang, Yimin Zuo
Labs: Nankai University; Alibaba Group; Tsinghua University
Arxiv: 2610.01415
Day: 2026-10-03
Upvotes: 71
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01415
---Beyond Memory is from Yu Luo, Jiamin Jiang, and Yimin Zuo, with the working team spread across Nankai University, Alibaba Group, and Tsinghua University, an inference-time framework for long-horizon agents.

The premise is that the way LLM agents handle history is broken in a specific way. The standard practice is context management: you keep a trajectory, or you compress it, or you retrieve relevant parts of it, and you feed that to the model as memory. But memory is not understanding. An agent that has the full history does not necessarily have a coherent picture of what is true right now: which objects exist, what state they are in, what has been done, and what is still required. This paper introduces PoS, which stands for progression of states, an inference-time framework that constructs and continually maintains an explicit belief state as the agent's decision context. A belief is not a summary of what happened. Each belief combines an estimate of the current world state, entities with their states and relations, each with a confidence, alongside the unresolved task requirements, making explicit what the agent still needs to learn and what it still needs to accomplish.

The framework has a loop, and the interesting part is the failure handling. Concretely, the belief at any step is a structured, task-conditioned object with three pieces: the world state, a set of entities with their states and the relations between them, each carrying a confidence; the goal, which is fixed for the task; and the deltas, the gaps between the current state and what the goal requires, split into epistemic gaps, things the agent does not yet know, and achievement gaps, things it still has to do. Every action is conditioned on the current belief plus the active gap, the single most important unresolved requirement. After the action, a component they call the belief sentinel does two jobs at once. It runs consistency validation, checking for conflicting states, unsupported relations, and updates that contradict the trajectory evidence, and it records progress, labeling whether the last step reduced a gap, gathered useful evidence, or opened a plausible path. That progress stream feeds into the belief health score, which is computed over a sliding window of recent validated transitions. The score is high when a persistent unresolved gap is not accompanied by progress stagnation or by belief recurrence, the same state configuration reappearing after a lag. Trapping is declared the moment the health score drops below a threshold, and a single step is never enough to declare it, which is deliberate, because one uninformative action is not a trap.

That is where the concept of belief trapping does its work, and the diagnosis is factorized along two dimensions. The first dimension is the agent dynamics pattern, classified in a fixed priority order. Static means the world state has not changed locally across the window, the agent is doing the same thing and nothing moves. Cycle means a periodic recurrence, the same transition reappearing at a dominant lag, like examining the same cabinet over and over. Drift means the world state is changing, but the changes do not advance the active gap, so the agent is moving without making progress. The second dimension is the blocked gap type: they compare the persistence of the epistemic gaps against the achievement gaps, and whichever is more persistently blocked decides the type. An epistemic block means the recovery has to acquire new discriminative evidence; an achievement block means it has to induce a task-relevant state change. The recovery then composes two constraints: a pattern-specific escape, which for static suppresses the ineffective state-action transition, for cycle cuts the recurrent edge, and for drift re-anchors action selection back to the active gap, plus the gap-specific progress constraint. The active gap itself stays fixed during recovery, the constraints are applied on top of the normal action selection, and after the resulting transition is validated the health score is recomputed. If it is still below threshold, the diagnosis is redone and new constraints composed; once it recovers, the constraints are released.

The paper documents this with a real ALFWorld case, goal is to put a hot mug in the cabinet. An implicit-belief agent fails after 50 steps, and when you look at the trajectory it examined the cabinet thirty-six times, while the belief health score sat near zero point one, a static stagnation. The same task with PoS succeeds in 17 steps, because the belief catches that the mug was never heated, the microwave step was missed, and the recovery targets exactly that gap.

The experiments are on four benchmarks spanning two kinds of tasks, execution and diagnosis, with three LLM backbones. The execution benchmarks are ALFWorld, a household task suite, and LOCA-Bench, and the diagnosis benchmarks are RCA-100 and ClinDiag, where the agent has to gather evidence and identify a root cause or a diagnosis. The baselines are a raw trajectory, four context-management methods, ACON, PACE, HiAgent, and LongHorizon-Harness, all sharing the same harness, ReAct, for a fair comparison. The headline results on the Qwen3.7-Plus backbone: on ALFWorld, raw trajectory is 62.69 percent task success, the best baseline, LongHorizon-Harness, is 72.39, and PoS is 88.81. On LOCA-Bench, raw is 43.62, the best baseline is 52.57, PoS is 56.38. On RCA-100, raw is 24.27, best baseline 28.16, PoS is 38.83, and on ClinDiag, raw is 38.91, best baseline 40.56, PoS is 45.03. The relative gains over the strongest baseline reach 22.68 percent on ALFWorld and 37.89 percent on RCA-100. The pattern holds on the other two backbones, Kimi-K3 and GLM-5.3, and PoS achieves the highest overall performance on every benchmark with all three.

One finding I would flag as important and somewhat uncomfortable: a large chunk of the existing context-management literature does not consistently beat just giving the model the raw trajectory. Table 1 shows PACE collapsing on LOCA-Bench to 17.33 against a raw trajectory of 43.62 on one backbone, and on ClinDiag with GLM-5.3, none of the context-management baselines beat the raw trajectory. So the baseline bar for this whole subfield is lower than the field assumes, and that is part of why the explicit-belief approach wins by such a wide margin. The ablations show where the win comes from. Removing consistency validation drops ALFWorld from 88.81 to 73.88, and removing the trapping diagnosis drops it to 73.13, so both validation and recovery are load-bearing. There are also context-scaling experiments showing the approach stays resilient as the raw context grows, rather than only helping when the context is short.

The skeptical read. This is an inference-time method, so every gain comes at the cost of extra LLM calls for belief maintenance, validation, and recovery, and the paper reports task success, not wall-clock time or token cost, so the efficiency trade is unquantified. The four benchmarks are all structured environments, household tasks and diagnosis with discrete states, which is where an explicit symbolic belief is most natural. Whether maintaining a belief state pays for itself in a more open-ended setting, where the world state is fuzzy and the task requirements are underspecified, is not tested. And the belief is maintained by LLM calls themselves, so a weak backbone degrades the belief quality, which the three-backbone comparison shows but does not fully resolve.

To sum up, this is the strongest case yet for a concrete position: the bottleneck in long-horizon agents is not memory access, it is the absence of an explicit, validated, maintained model of the current situation, and that bottleneck has a name now, belief trapping, with a measurable health score. The method is simple, the gains are large and consistent across backbones and task types, and the uncomfortable baseline analysis gives the field a more honest picture of what context management has actually been doing so far.
