VidUEU-Agent: A Data-Curation Agent for Multimodal Understanding, Editing, and Unified Tasks, by Tengju Ru, Weitong Lian, and Zecong Tang, from Zhejiang University, the University of Hong Kong, the Institute of Automation at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Huawei Technologies, is a pipeline for turning raw video into instruction data, and for using that data to improve a unified multimodal model. The motivation is the standard one for this area: raw video is abundant, but converting it into precise instruction data that a model can learn from is expensive, because it requires dense annotations that humans will not produce at scale. The gap is not that video is scarce, it is that the structured, verifiable instruction data a unified model needs to learn understanding, editing, and interleaved generation is hard to manufacture, and the existing curation pipelines are either too narrow, covering one paradigm, or too expensive to run at 100,000-sample scale. The paper's answer is an automated curation agent that goes from raw clips to a 100,000-sample instruction dataset, and that then feeds that dataset back into training and re-plans the data distribution based on what the downstream model actually gets wrong.

The pipeline has four stages. First, global perception, which does a coarse pass over a video to understand it and to filter out low-quality shots, so the later stages only spend compute on usable material. This stage is a hard gate, because raw video is full of dark frames, blurry motion, and shots that carry no signal, and letting those through would poison the metadata that the later stages build. Second, mining keyframes and multimodal signals, which selects the informative frames and pulls in auxiliary signals from a small tool pool that includes optical character recognition for on-screen text and speech recognition for audio, with a score-similarity greedy sampling step that prefers keyframes that are both informative and non-redundant rather than densely packed. Third, constructing metadata from the keyframe sequence and the filtered signals, so each surviving shot carries a structured description of what is in it, what is said over it, and what text is on screen. Fourth, routing that metadata to synthesize instruction samples, with the routing deciding whether a given sample targets the understanding paradigm, the editing paradigm, or the unified paradigm, where the model must handle both in a single model. There are explicit quality gates between the stages, and a metadata filtering gate in particular, because the density of the metadata is what lets a small annotation budget fan out into a large sample set. The scaling result is VidUEU-100K, a dataset of roughly 100,000 instruction samples, built from about 20,000 dense metadata annotations, and split into about 40,000 understanding samples, 30,000 editing samples, and 30,000 unified samples, spanning nine task paradigms and five scenario categories.

The part that makes this more than a dataset paper is the autonomous iteration loop. After training a baseline model on the current data mix, the agent examines the downstream results and adjusts the data distribution, targeting quality, balance, and training effectiveness, and re-synthesizes. The iteration is downstream-driven: the system looks at where the fine-tuned model underperforms, and re-weights which paradigms and which scenario categories get more samples in the next round. In their runs, iteration two moves the numbers in the intended direction on some benchmarks and slightly backward on others, which is the honest shape of a data-distribution search rather than a monotonic climb. The practical effect of the loop is that the dataset is not a one-shot artifact but a controlled experiment over what data mix a unified model actually benefits from, and it is the part that separates an agent that curates data from a script that just runs a fixed pipeline.

The training results are where the claims land. They fine-tune baseline models on VidUEU-100K and evaluate on a spread of benchmarks, including MMBench, MMStar, MMDU for understanding, GEdit for editing, and the WEAVE and MMT benchmarks for unified capability. Fine-tuning improves all the reported understanding scores, and the fine-tuned model reaches the best MMT-Bench score in their comparison at 0.651. On the editing side, the dataset lifts the overall score of the Bagel model on the WEAVE benchmark from 0.446 to 0.501, and the fine-tuned model outperforms both Bagel and a Bagel-Zebra variant, reaching 0.480 on the logic subset. Both models in the pipeline are evaluated on a single H20 GPU, which keeps the compute story realistic, and they note a quality-versus-throughput tradeoff: a higher-quality configuration scores in the 4.16 to 4.21 range on their internal quality metric but drops throughput from 12.4 to 4.1 samples per minute per GPU, which is the kind of knob a production curation pipeline has to tune deliberately. They also run a controlled comparison of the metadata-driven design against a direct question-answering generation by a vision-language model, generating 200 samples each and scoring them on a five-point scale, and the metadata-driven route wins on both quality dimensions. And scaling the vision-language model in the pipeline from 9B to 27B improves quality at the cost of throughput, so the pipeline has a clean cost-quality dial.

The ablations isolate which gates matter. Removing the metadata filtering gate hurts every benchmark, and the editing and unified tasks fall the most, which supports the claim that the gate is what keeps low-quality raw-video frames out of the training data. Removing the score-similarity greedy keyframe sampling hurts editing in particular, consistent with editing data needing keyframes that are informative and non-redundant rather than densely sampled. And replacing the whole structured pipeline with direct generation by a vision-language model, no metadata, no tools, no routing, produces data that trains worse, which is the ablation that justifies the entire four-stage design.

The skeptical read. This is fundamentally a data-engineering paper, and the strongest claim is the one that is hardest to reproduce: that an autonomous loop over the data distribution is better than a well-chosen static mix. The iteration results are mixed across benchmarks, which is realistic but means the loop's benefit is not uniform, and the paper does not isolate how much of the gain comes from the loop versus from the 100,000-sample scale itself. A static mix of the same size would be a natural baseline, and the comparison to it is the experiment that would settle the core claim. There is also the usual curation caveat that the tool pool and the perception stage set a quality ceiling, so the instruction data inherits any systematic errors in the coarse perception pass, and the dense metadata, while 20,000 annotations, is still a small fraction of the 100,000 samples, meaning many samples are synthesized from thin metadata. And the benchmark spread is wide, so a model can improve on the unified benchmarks while the editing gains are modest. That said, the four-stage pipeline is clearly specified, the dataset is large and public-facing, the single-GPU evaluation is honest, and the explicit routing across understanding, editing, and unified tasks is a clean way to structure a unified-model data recipe that other groups can lift.

Bottom line: VidUEU-Agent is a competent, well-specified video-to-instruction data pipeline that produces a 100,000-sample dataset and shows consistent, if mixed, improvements across unified multimodal benchmarks. The four-stage design with explicit quality gates is clearly documented, the dataset is large and public-facing, and the single-GPU evaluation is honest about the compute involved, with the cost-quality dial laid out explicitly. The autonomous data-distribution loop is the interesting idea but is not yet decisively proven over a strong static baseline, so treat the dataset and the gate design as the solid contributions and the loop as a promising, still-unsettled one. For anyone building unified multimodal models, the routing across understanding, editing, and unified tasks, plus the metadata-fan-out trick that turns 20,000 annotations into 100,000 samples, is the part worth adopting directly.
