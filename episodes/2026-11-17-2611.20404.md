Today's paper is Latent Feedback Is a Compute and Data Amplifier, from Benhao Huang, Chaitanya Dwivedi, and Xuezhe Ma, at the Institute of Foundation Models, with affiliations at the University of Southern California and Carnegie Mellon University. The question is a precise one that most of us hand-wave: when your high-quality data runs out, everyone trains for a few more epochs, and everyone knows each repeat is worth less than the last. Latent feedback — an architectural change where each token's top-layer state is fed into the next token's input through a linear map — is another way to spend that extra compute, but it has not been tested head-to-head against a vanilla transformer that spends the same compute on more epochs. This paper runs that test across a full grid of model sizes, data amounts, and epoch budgets, and the verdict is that latent feedback is an amplifier: at most it saves 48 percent of the compute and 47 percent of the unique data to reach a given loss, and the savings grow with how much unique data you have per parameter.

The setup. Latent feedback modifies the transformer's input pathway: for each token, the hidden state from the top layer of the previous token is mapped by a matrix W and added to the current token's embedding input. The extra compute is small and measurable — the paper reports the overhead cost c at roughly 1.20 to 1.27 across their four model sizes, with the feedback strength p fixed at 0.15 throughout. So this is not a doubling of FLOPs; it is a modest overhead that the paper claims buys you a real amplification effect.

The experimental design is the part to respect. The authors run a grid: model sizes from 60 million to 271 million parameters, unique data pools from 12.5 times N to 400 times N tokens — that is, from 12.5 to 400 unique tokens per parameter — and a budget of epochs for each. The data is a deduplicated sample of the Dolma 3 corpus that they process themselves, with documents sharing paragraphs with the Paloma benchmark removed, so Paloma stays a clean held-out evaluation set. Documents are ordered so every prefix has the full mixture of sources and topics at the same ratios, and each run's pool is the first U tokens of that order, so the pools are nested and differ only in size. For each cell of the grid, they tune the learning rate and the weight-decay timescale on a main run, branch off decay runs, and take the minimum of a quadratic fit — a proper tuning procedure at every single point, not a single learning rate across the board. Validation is on a held-out set of 47,274 documents, about 100 million tokens, from the same stratified order with training documents excluded. For downstream evaluation they score a 109-task suite in 37 groups, weighted by the inverse of each suite's seed variance so noisy suites do not dominate.

The headline results. With unique data fixed, latent feedback reaches a vanilla transformer's loss with less compute; with epochs fixed, it reaches that loss with less unique data. And both savings grow with unique data per parameter and with repetition. The numbers at the extremes: at 400 unique tokens per parameter, the compute savings are in the upper 40 percent range, and the data savings reach 47 percent. The regime where it does not win is when unique data is scarce relative to model size — the paper states that today's models are far from that regime, which is doing some quiet work, because it means the claim is about the data-rich scaling regime, not about small-data regimes where repeating data is your only option.

The scaling law is the second contribution, and it is the one I would actually use. The authors fit one scaling law to both models — the vanilla transformer and the latent-feedback transformer — in which latent feedback enters the law as a transformer with more parameters. That is an elegant way to say it: the amplification is equivalent to getting extra effective parameters for free. The law is fitted on models from 60 million to 271 million parameters, and it predicts the losses at a held-out 778 million parameter size to within 0.3 percent, and the gap between the two models to within 0.05 percent. That is a strong extrapolation result: a law fit on sub-300-million models predicts a 778 million model that way. The fitted law predicts that both savings keep growing with unique data and repetition, which is the extrapolation claim the title is resting on.

The mechanism behind the regime boundary is worth spelling out, because it is the part I would implement as a pre-flight check. When unique data is scarce relative to the model — the small-data corner — every additional epoch re-encounters the same examples, and the latent-feedback pass over the same tokens does not introduce any new signal; it only re-weights what the model has already extracted, so the extra FLOPs buy nothing and the two curves sit on top of each other. When unique data is abundant, every extra epoch meets new examples, and the latent-feedback pass now has fresh structure to fit, so the extra FLOPs buy real loss reduction. That is the whole argument in one sentence, and it is why the practical advice is not "use latent feedback" but "check your data-to-compute ratio first." For a lab planning a pretraining run, the workflow follows: fix the model size, count the unique tokens in the corpus, and if the pool is below the ratio at which the paper's curves separate, spend the budget on more data or a smaller model rather than on the feedback loop. The paper does not give a closed-form threshold — the boundary is read off the curves — which is a limitation worth fixing with a fitted formula, because right now the advice is qualitative even though the underlying effect is quantitative.

Now the skeptical assessment, and there are real limits here that the paper itself surfaces. First, the size range. The law is fitted on 60 million to 271 million parameter models, and the held-out check is 778 million. Frontier pre-training happens at 100 times that scale and up. The claim that the savings keep growing is an extrapolation over two orders of magnitude, and the 0.05 percent gap prediction at 778 million is encouraging but is one data point of evidence, not a proof of the growth trend. Second, the data is one corpus family: processed Dolma 3 with Paloma held out. The amplification claim is about the interaction of data repetition and the architecture, and I would want to see it hold on a differently distributed corpus before treating it as a law rather than a property of this data regime. Third, the overhead is not zero — the 1.20 to 1.27 cost factor means latent feedback is not free compute, and the savings percentages are net of that; the paper is honest about it, but a 20 percent overhead that saves 15 percent of the data is a wash for some budget constraints. Fourth, the downstream evaluation, while properly weighted, is a per-byte continuation metric on a fixed suite; the paper's claim is about pre-training efficiency, and the downstream agreement is supporting evidence, not the primary result. Fifth, the decoding cost: decoding latent feedback token by token is slow because each token depends on the previous top-layer state, and the paper evaluates with four parallel passes whose states nearly match the true decoding states, leaving the loss almost unchanged. That approximation is fine for evaluation, but if you deploy a latent-feedback model, you inherit a sequential dependency in decoding that a vanilla transformer does not have, and the inference-time cost of that dependency is a real engineering consideration the pre-training paper does not close out.

Engineering notes if you take this seriously. The nested-pool data ordering — every prefix has the full mixture at the same ratios — is a design pattern worth stealing for any scaling study, because it removes the confound of different data mixtures at different pool sizes. The per-cell tuning with quadratic fits is expensive but it is what makes the scaling law honest; a single learning rate across the grid would have produced a messier curve and a weaker law. And the NorMuon optimizer for the matrices with AdamW for embeddings and the head, at p equal 0.15, is the specific recipe to start from if you replicate.

Bottom line: this is the cleanest controlled test yet of latent feedback against the obvious alternative of spending the same compute on more data repetitions, and the fitted scaling law is a genuinely useful artifact that predicts held-out sizes tightly. I would trust the grid results within the tested regime and the law's interpolation, and I would treat the keep-growing-with-scale extrapolation as a well-motivated hypothesis that needs a 10-billion-scale confirmation before it should drive a pre-training decision.
