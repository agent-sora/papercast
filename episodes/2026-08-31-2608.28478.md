Blind Men and the Elephant: Probing the Epistemic Myopia of LLMs under Long-Tail Divergent Knowledge, by Zhuoshi Pan of Tsinghua University and Tencent Youtu Lab, Junru Lu and Yan Qian, with further authors from Tsinghua, Tencent Youtu Lab, and the University of Warwick. The benchmark asks a question that standard factual QA cannot ask: not whether a model knows a rare fact, but whether it knows all the credible versions of that fact that its training data contained. The parable in the title is the failure mode: each blind man touches one part of the elephant and generalizes; a model that read one account of a disputed birth date and only ever reproduces that account has the same pathology, and because conventional QA scores a single canonical answer, the pathology is invisible. The evaluation is fully closed-book: no source documents, no retrieval, no tools, just the question, so whatever the model answers comes from parametric memory alone, which is what makes the measurement about what training actually retained rather than about reasoning over evidence.

The construction pipeline is the reusable contribution. It starts from the filtered remainder of a web corpus, the documents a standard quality classifier, the DCLM fastText filter, throws away, because low-exposure documents are where disagreements survive unharmonized and where pretraining upsampling is least likely to have distorted exposure. Documents become nodes in a graph; support edges connect documents about the same fact, conflict edges connect documents reporting incompatible accounts of the same subject-attribute pair. Building this graph naively would cost pairwise comparisons over everything, so candidate pairs are found two cheap ways: knowledge-point clustering under the SuperGPQA taxonomy pairs documents sharing a field label, and named-entity matching through an inverted index retrieves cross-cluster pairs sharing a normalized entity or event. Only those candidates go to a large model, Qwen3.6 twenty-seven B, which labels each pair support, conflict, or none. From each verified conflict edge they expand a local subgraph including the documents supporting each side, generate matched question pairs, one naming the entity directly, one identifying it only by clues, with both forms sharing the same verified answer set so the score difference isolates the effect of indirect identification, and then validate every record in three stages: a language model checks each answer against the full source documents, a web agent with search independently verifies both sides against authoritative sources like Wikipedia, and human reviewers audit for entity mismatches, unsupported answers, and leakage. Out of four thousand one hundred twenty-seven conflict edges yielding eight thousand two hundred fifty-four candidate questions, one thousand ninety-four QA pairs survive, spread over twenty-two knowledge fields with news and public information the largest at thirty-nine point four percent. Every answer remains traceable to its source documents, which are withheld at evaluation.

The evaluation is closed-book with a three-way outcome: complete recall when the model produces every verified account and nothing wrong, partial recall when it produces at least one but not all, failed recall otherwise, judged semantically, plus a conditional completeness metric that scores completeness among questions where the model recalled at least one answer. Thirty-two models were evaluated, twenty-six open-weight and six proprietary, with reasoning enabled and effort set to high where configurable.

The headline finding is uncomfortable: even the strongest model achieves complete recall on only about half the questions. Kimi-K3 leads at fifty-two point four percent complete recall, followed by Gemini three point one Pro at fifty point three seven and GPT-five point five at fifty point one eight, and the failed-recall rates for the top three are tiny, two to three percent. Read those two numbers together and the diagnosis is precise: the best models almost always remember that the fact exists and produce one credible answer, and almost never produce all the credible answers. Partial recall runs forty-five to forty-seven percent for the leaders, so the dominant failure is not ignorance, it is one-sided memory, which is exactly what single-answer QA never measures. Scale helps but does not fix it: across open-weight families, average complete recall is five point four eight percent below ten billion parameters versus fifty-two point four for the best trillion-class model, and within Qwen3.5, scaling from two B to three hundred ninety-seven B raises complete recall from one point six five to thirty-two point two seven percent while cutting failure from eighty-one to eight and a half, but partial recall simultaneously rises from seventeen to fifty-nine percent, so growth buys recall of one account faster than it buys completeness. Reasoning is a mixed bag: giving GPT-five point six-Sol and GPT-OSS one hundred twenty B more thinking raises complete recall by about thirteen and thirteen points, but across the Qwen3.5 family the effect is under five points everywhere and actually negative at two and four B, where extra deliberation makes the model settle harder on the consensus account and suppress the minority one.

The corpus analysis is the part that connects the benchmark to data curation. For each conflict they count the documents supporting each side, and the exposure asymmetry is stark: the more the dominant account out-published the minority account, the higher the partial recall, meaning models increasingly recall only the dominant side; and greater minority-side exposure is associated with more complete recall. In the regression, a one-standard-deviation increase in majority-side exposure tracks a fourteen point two point rise in partial recall and a ten point drop in failure, mostly determining whether the fact is recalled at all, while the same increase on the minority side tracks a fifteen point one point rise in complete recall and a fifteen point four drop in partial, determining whether both accounts survive. One exposure measurement on the minority side is worth more for completeness than anything on the majority side. Domain breakdown adds a curation-relevant gradient: complete recall is highest for people, organizations, and events at thirty-eight point seven percent and digital content at thirty-two point one, falls to twenty-two point seven for news, and bottoms out for government and civic affairs at twelve and consumer products at six point two, where partial recall dominates above fifty-five percent; the authors conjecture the pattern tracks the source composition of modern pretraining mixtures, with heavily sampled Wikipedia inflating exposure for prominent people and events while fine-grained dates, prices, and vote counts stay under-exposed.

The skeptical read. First, the corpus is a proxy for pretraining exposure, not pretraining data itself; the authors say so, the associations are observational, and any model trained on a mixture that upsampled the minority account could break the correlation. Second, disagreements here are real source conflicts, but a two-answer benchmark cannot represent disputes with continua of positions, and conflict edges were labeled by a twenty-seven B model, mitigated by the three-stage audit but not eliminated. Third, the judge is a frontier model with a rubric, with human agreement checked in an appendix, but complete-versus-partial boundaries on long free-form answers always carry judge noise. Fourth, closed-book recall of long-tail conflicts is a narrow slice of epistemics, no tool use, no retrieval, no recency, so a strong result here does not certify general trustworthiness, and nothing tests whether models flag uncertainty when they recall only one account, which is arguably the behavior that matters most in deployment. What I would trust: the pipeline, which is auditable and reusable on any corpus, and the central asymmetry finding, which has an obvious mechanism and obvious curation implications. What I would re-verify: whether the exposure-completeness association holds under intervention, by controlled upsampling in a training run, rather than correlationally. Bottom line: the paper converts a vague worry, models flatten contested knowledge into a single confident answer, into a measured, source-traceable number, roughly half even for the best model, and shows that minority-side exposure is what buys completeness. For anyone curating training data, the actionable version is that contested facts need deliberate two-sided coverage, because the corpus will not provide it by accident.
