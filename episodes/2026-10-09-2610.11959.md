"MiMo-V2.6: scaling reinforcement learning towards self-improvement," a technical report from Xiaomi's LLM-Core team, with the first three named authors Zongming Qiao, Ziyue Hua, and Zirui Ou. This is a foundation-model release, so it is in scope on the standing rule regardless of upvotes, and it is a substantial one. The series has two models: MiMo-V2.6-Pro, a Mixture-of-Experts model with 1.02 trillion total parameters and 42 billion active, and MiMo-V2.6-Flash, a 310-billion Mixture-of-Experts model with 15 billion active. The central claim is that the main axis of progress for these models is not more pre-training but scaling the reinforcement-learning compute, and the report is largely a record of how they did that.

Let me set up the architecture, because it frames everything. The text backbone is a hybrid sparse Mixture-of-Experts Transformer that interleaves local sliding-window attention with global attention. A hybrid block is a run of consecutive sliding-window-attention blocks followed by a single global-attention block, and the sliding window size is 128. The first block is global attention with a dense feed-forward network to stabilize early representation learning. Both block types use a sparse Mixture-of-Experts feed-forward with no shared experts. The configuration table is concrete: the Flash main block has 48 layers, 39 of them sliding-window and 9 global, hidden size 4096, and 256 experts with 8 activated; the Pro main block has 70 layers, 60 sliding-window and 10 global, hidden size 6144, 384 experts with 8 activated. The motivation for the hybrid attention is stated plainly: preserve global context at low computational cost, which matters for the million-token contexts they are targeting. They add a lightweight MiMo Vision Transformer, a hybrid-attention visual encoder with sink-augmented sliding-window attention, audio encoders, and a speculative decoder built from a five-layer multi-token-prediction module that drafts seven tokens in a single pass for parallel verification.

Pre-training is a two-stage recipe. The first stage trains the language backbone on text only, and the second integrates the in-house vision and audio encoders and jointly trains the full omni-modal model. They start at a 32,000-token context and extend it to 256,000 partway through. Flash is trained on 48 trillion tokens, 26 trillion in the text stage and 22 trillion in the omni stage, while Pro is trained on 30 trillion tokens, 27 trillion and 3 trillion respectively. The interesting addition for this report is the mid-training phase, which sits between pre-training and reinforcement learning. It is agent-centric: a diverse data mixture combining realistic agent trajectories across coding, general, visual, and research tasks with high-quality text, repository-level code, and multimodal data, trained first at a 256,000 context and then extended to a one-million-token context. The stated purpose is to expand the exploration space so the model can find better task-solving trajectories during post-training. They also note a specific optimization finding: their base model was pre-trained with AdamW, but in mixed-task reinforcement learning they saw diminishing optimization efficiency as the batch size increased, so they adapt the optimizer for large-batch training. This mid-training step matters for the whole argument, because a model that has only seen static corpora gives the reinforcement learner a narrow set of starting behaviors to refine, whereas a model that has already practiced agent trajectories has more room to explore during post-training.

Now the actual contribution, scaling the reinforcement-learning compute along three dimensions. The first is larger batches and higher throughput. They run a fully asynchronous training setup that consumes 1,568 samples and between 2.7 and 3.7 billion tokens per step, at context lengths up to one million tokens, processing thousands of long-horizon rollouts per step. The second is more diverse and complex environments, spanning code, general, visual, and cyber domains under a mixture of agent harnesses, with the claim that varying both the harness and the task improves generalization. The third is more grader compute. This is the methodologically interesting part. Instead of assigning the same reward to every solution that passes the test cases, they compare solutions within each group to distinguish the quality of the problem-solving, which they call groupwise agentic grading. Two named mechanisms, Groupwise Reward Synthesis and Groupwise Advantage Redistribution, convert these fine-grained distinctions into more informative learning signals, steering the model toward shorter, more token-efficient solutions.

To keep this stable at scale, they freeze the Mixture-of-Experts router and establish a multi-layer defense against reward hacking. The router freezing is a notable stability choice: in a Mixture-of-Experts model the routing itself is a learned, high-variance component, and leaving it trainable during a large asynchronous reinforcement-learning run is a natural source of instability, so pinning it down is a pragmatic way to keep the expert selection from drifting while the policy updates. There is a whole infrastructure section for mixed-task agentic reinforcement learning: a unified trajectory representation, high-concurrency multi-framework rollout across a pool of harnesses, a decoupled control plane and data plane to buffer and transfer massive trajectories carrying routing and multimodal payloads, and a training-inference consistency alignment on Mixture-of-Experts routing and top-p sampling candidate sets. The training dynamics are reported across the benchmarks in the opening figure: both Pro and Flash improve steadily as training steps increase, across coding on DeepSWE, SWE-Bench Pro, and their own MiMo Code Bench, general workflows on AutomationBench, visual tasks on a MiMo Visual Coding benchmark, and cybersecurity on a MiMo Cyber Bench. The point they make is that the gains are not confined to a single verifiable domain but hold across coding, general workflows, visual design, and cyber, which they take as evidence of more general agentic intelligence.

Here is my assessment. What I would trust is the architectural and infrastructure story, because it is the kind of detailed, co-designed system that is hard to fake: the hybrid sliding-window-plus-global Mixture-of-Experts backbone, the one-million-token asynchronous rollout, the decoupled control and data planes, the training-inference consistency alignment, and the router freezing for stability are all specific, checkable engineering claims. The groupwise agentic grading idea is the most interesting methodological contribution, because moving from a binary pass or fail reward to within-group quality comparisons is a real, principled way to get a denser learning signal on long-horizon tasks, and it is a direction the community is clearly converging on. What I would be skeptical of, or at least reserve judgment on, is the magnitude of the reported benchmark gains, and that applies to any foundation-model report. These benchmarks, including several that are their own, are reported by the team without an independent replication, and a lot of the "scaling" here is infrastructure scaling, which is genuinely hard but is also the kind of thing where the reported numbers are only as good as their grading and their environment fidelity. The one thing I would want to see before treating this as a step toward the self-improvement framing in the title is an analysis of how much of the gain is attributable to the groupwise grading versus to raw compute and environment diversity, and whether the improvements transfer to environments outside the ones they trained on. They also open-source the RL stack, environments, and training dynamics, and release a smaller distillation model, which is a useful community baseline and lowers the barrier to reproducing the grading and infrastructure claims even if you cannot run the full Pro model.

Here is the bottom line. MiMo-V2.6 is a serious foundation-model release and a useful, unusually detailed public record of how you scale agentic reinforcement learning: a hybrid sparse Mixture-of-Experts backbone with a million-token context, fully asynchronous multi-billion-token rollouts, groupwise agentic grading for denser rewards, and router freezing plus reward-hacking defenses for stability. If you are building at this scale, the infrastructure and grading sections are worth studying closely, but treat the headline benchmark numbers as the team's own report and verify the transfer and the grading attribution before you build your expectations on them.
