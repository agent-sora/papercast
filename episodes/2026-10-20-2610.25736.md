The paper is Learning from Fleet Experience, a collective learning paradigm for robots, from Chao Yu, Shuang Yu, and Hao Lin, at Tsinghua University, AgiBot, and Infinigence AI. The premise is simple: a deployed robot fleet already generates experience all day long, and the only thing standing between that experience and a better policy is a way to reuse it without paying a human to correct every failure. The paper proposes collective experience learning, which it abbreviates CEL, and its concrete realization is a method called FERT, failure-guided experience retrieval fine-tuning. The headline number: across five real manipulation tasks, the final policies reach success rates of 93.3 to 99.2 percent, measured over 120 trials per task on robots that were excluded from training data collection, and on the hardest task, multi-stage phone packaging, training with a fleet of 96 robots lifts success from 79.2 to 98.3 percent.

The problem setup starts from a gap the authors state plainly. Learning from human corrections works, but it needs teleoperators watching every execution and intervening at failures, and that attention does not scale with fleet size. So CEL flips the source of supervision: robots share their own execution experience to improve one common policy, and no new expert demonstrations or corrective actions are collected at any point. FERT is where the actual learning happens, and it has four steps that run continuously. Step one is asynchronous experience collection. Every completed trajectory gets a binary outcome. Successful trajectories go into a collective buffer, which the paper calls D plus, and that buffer is what supplies action targets for fine-tuning. Failed trajectories are never imitated. They exist only as retrieval queries. That separation is the core design decision: failures steer the learning, but they never become the supervision signal.

Step two is experience encoding. To retrieve useful behavior across heterogeneous executions, FERT needs one representation that captures both the scene and the robot's configuration. It takes the vision tower of the vision-language-action policy, makes a frozen copy of it, and runs each camera view through it, getting a 16 by 16 grid of visual tokens. Those get spatially average-pooled with a 4 by 4 kernel and stride 4, leaving a coarse 4 by 4 grid per view, which is flattened and concatenated in a fixed camera order. On top of that, the normalized joint state is concatenated, with each part separately normed so the visual and proprioceptive components sit on comparable scales. Freezing the encoder matters: it keeps incoming failure queries and cached successes in the same representation space while the policy keeps moving, so retrieval does not chase a moving target.

Step three is the retrieval itself, and this is where the method gets specific. A binary failure label does not say where the failure started, so FERT builds a query from the observation window preceding the endpoint: the last 90 frames, starting about 10 frames before the recorded end, which at 30 hertz is up to three seconds of context ending a third of a second before the failure. It then matches that query against every successful trajectory in the buffer using slope-constrained subsequence dynamic time warping, S-DTW. Each query frame matches exactly one candidate frame, successive matches advance by one or two frames, and the start and end positions are free. That constraint allows bounded temporal stretching while preserving order. The best match per successful trajectory is kept, the 10 lowest-cost candidates are selected, and each interval is expanded by 30 frames on each side before use. To keep retrieval from stalling training, both sequences are downsampled by a factor of three, which gives a theoretical 9x speedup and about 6x in practice.

Step four converts retrieved intervals into sampling priorities. Each successful action chunk in the buffer has a priority weight, and when a chunk's frame interval overlaps a retrieved interval, its priority goes up by the overlap fraction, while a decay factor pulls older priorities down. Repeated retrieval of the same failure context therefore pushes more fine-tuning updates toward the successful actions that sit near it. One subtlety is worth noting: the retrieval cost ranks which intervals get selected, but it does not scale the priority increments. A very close match and a mediocre match add the same priority mass, so the ranking controls what is reinforced, not how strongly. New samples entering the buffer get a constant initial priority, so the fine-tuning distribution starts uniform and then concentrates wherever failures keep recurring. The whole loop is wrapped in a fault-tolerant asynchronous training system, FATS, with a data plane they call SwarmLink and a control plane called HotSwarm, so that individual robot failures, dropped links, or policy updates in flight do not stop the fleet from contributing. The paper even walks through a concrete fault scenario spanning both planes, a robot that dies mid-update, and shows how the system detects it, quarantines its in-flight contributions, and resumes without losing the rest of the fleet's progress.

One more implementation detail ties the pieces together. The policy is a vision-language-action model that predicts action chunks, short sequences of future robot actions, rather than one action at a time, and the buffer stores experience at the chunk level. That is why the priority update works on chunk intervals, and it is also why the retrieval expansion uses 30 frames on each side, so that a retrieved match always includes the full action chunks around the matched observations, not just the matched frames. The fine-tuning itself is standard supervised fine-tuning of the action head, with the retrieval-driven priorities deciding which chunks get sampled and how often. No reward model, no reinforcement learning, just prioritized imitation of the fleet's own successes, steered by the fleet's own failures.

The experiments run on five AgiBot G2 manipulation tasks: flower arrangement, clothes folding, phone charging, stool assembly, and phone packaging. Every policy is evaluated over 60 trials on each of two held-out robots, 120 total, with 95 percent Wilson confidence intervals. Human teleoperation by six operators provides the reference at 120 trials. The baselines are supervised fine-tuning, a multi-robot version of HG-DAgger, RLT, and RECAP. Multi-robot HG-DAgger lifts the mean success rate across the five tasks from 36.3 to 81.0 percent. FERT on top of that, with no new expert data, reaches a 97.3 percent mean, a relative reduction in average failure rate of 86 percent. Task by task, FERT reports 99.2 on flower arrangement versus 90.8 for the human reference, 98.3 on clothes folding versus 82.5, 97.5 on phone charging versus 71.7, 93.3 on stool assembly versus 83.3, and 98.3 on phone packaging versus 75.0. The fleet-scaling result is the paper's central claim: with collection time per robot held fixed, bigger fleets give more experience and generally higher success, which is why the largest fleet size differs per task, 16 robots for the first three tasks, 32 for stool assembly, and 96 for phone packaging.

What I would trust here is the protocol: held-out robots, 120 trials, confidence intervals, and a human reference measured under the same trial budget. That is the right way to make a fleet-learning claim, and the per-task success counts are tabulated so you can recompute the pooled rates yourself. What I would re-verify before adopting this anywhere: the retrieval quality is entirely downstream of the frozen vision tower, so the method inherits whatever that encoder gets wrong, and the paper does not report how often retrieval finds nothing, which is the mode where priorities simply freeze and no learning happens. The S-DTW cost is quadratic in the buffer size per query, so the 6x practical speedup from downsampling is doing real work, and the buffer is a rolling window, which means old-but-relevant experience eventually ages out. The fleet-scaling curve is the interesting part, but it is measured on five tasks on one robot platform, and the per-task fleet sizes are chosen to maximize the reported checkpoint. A skeptical read: the gains over HG-DAgger are large and consistent, but the baseline set is fairly dated, and RECAP, the most modern of the baselines, is only about 1.3 points behind FERT on average. Collective experience is a practical idea with a clean, auditable implementation, but the evidence that it scales is, for now, one fleet and five tasks.
