TRACE, Rollout-Guided Quantization-Aware Training for FP4 Reinforcement Learning of Mixture-of-Experts Language Models, is from Xin Wang, Hao Yu, and Zhengyang Zhuge, with the full team of twelve authors across Alibaba Token Hub, Alibaba Group, and Ohio State University, where one of the authors did the work as an intern. The paper is squarely in the efficiency-of-RL space, and it attacks a specific, well-defined problem: when you run the rollout generation for reinforcement learning in four-bit floating point to save compute, the rollout path and the training path diverge numerically, and that divergence destabilizes training, especially for Mixture-of-Experts models where tiny numerical differences can flip which experts the router activates.

The setup is this. In RL post-training, the bulk of the cost is generating long rollouts. Quantizing the policy to four-bit during generation, what the paper calls FP4 W-A for weights and activations, plus FP4 for the key-value cache, is a big win on both compute and memory. But a four-bit quantized model on the rollout path and a higher-precision or differently-quantized model on the training path are not the same model, and the policy the agent is exploring is not the policy being updated. The paper's core observation is that the existing FP4-RL methods get this wrong in a subtle way: they minimize the quantization error on each path independently, relative to a high-precision reference, but minimizing each path's error to the reference does not minimize the gap between the two quantized paths. Two values can each be close to the reference and still be far from each other, and in a four-bit grid that happens a lot.

TRACE's fix is to make the training path's rounding decisions directly follow the rollout path's actual quantization outcomes, rather than both aiming at the reference. Concretely, during rollout generation it records the quantization results of the FP4 routed-expert activations and the FP4 key-value states. Then in the quantization-aware training step, it uses those recorded rollout-side values to steer the corresponding training-side FP4 rounding. They formalize the thing they are minimizing as a Frobenius norm of the difference between the training-path quantized activation and the rollout-path quantized activation, measured at matched trajectory, token, and quantization sites. So the objective is the discrepancy between the two quantized paths, not the error of either one against the reference, which is the whole point and the part that differs from the prior methods like QUADS, Rollout-ResQ, and QaRL, which correct errors or match kernels but still leave the rounding outcomes free to diverge.

There is a second, very practical component, the quantization-information caching scheme, and it is what makes the method deployable. Recording rollout-side quantization information for every layer and every position would add real storage and communication overhead on top of the RL loop, which would eat the savings you got from quantizing in the first place. TRACE only retains the mantissa and scale information from selected deeper layers, not all of them, and dumps and reloads it between the rollout and training phases. That is the engineering that turns a clean idea into something you can actually run.

The training dynamics tell the story. On Qwen3.8-Flash-Next under aggressive FP4 rollout, vanilla FP4 quantization-aware training shows an increasing train-rollout log-probability difference as training proceeds, and the test score on Terminal Bench 2.1 degrades sharply. TRACE keeps the log-probability difference bounded and the score stable. There is also a second, more interesting effect: once the discrepancy is controlled, the policy starts to adapt to the FP4 rollout itself. TRACE initially suffers some quantization-induced degradation, but the gap relative to BF16 rollout shrinks over training and can be closed or even reversed, so the model is not just being dragged back to BF16 behavior, it is learning to be a good FP4 policy.

The evaluation spans four Mixture-of-Experts models, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next at 125B total with 6B activated, and Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, across reasoning, coding, and long-horizon RL tasks. On the 35B reasoning setup with joint FP4 weights, activations, and key-value rollout, the baselines fall well short of BF16: plain FP4 quantization-aware training averages 59.6 across the four math and code benchmarks, QaRL 58.6, QUADS 68.8, while BF16 is 74.9. TRACE lands at 75.3, which is actually slightly above the BF16 reference, and it beats QUADS by 6.5 points. The headline efficiency claim is a rollout speedup of up to 5.4 times over BF16 rollout, with limited algorithm overhead, and a final FP4 result that beats the pipeline of training in BF16 and then quantizing post-hoc.

Skeptically, the results that I trust most are the training-dynamics plots and the fact that the discrepancy metric is bounded rather than just the final score, because those are hard to fake with a better prompt. What I would re-verify: the 5.4 times speedup is a best-case number and depends on the FP4 kernels and the caching scheme being efficient on your hardware, so I would measure it on my own stack rather than assume it transfers. I would also want to see the caching layer selection made explicit, because retaining mantissa and scale from a subset of deeper layers is a heuristic, and I would want to know how sensitive the result is to which layers you keep. The MoE-routing sensitivity is the whole justification for the method, so a reader should want a direct measurement of expert-activation agreement between the two paths under TRACE, which the paper motivates but does not, in the parts I read, isolate as a number.

Bottom line: TRACE is a clean, well-motivated fix for a real failure mode in low-precision RL, and the reframe from per-path error to cross-path discrepancy is the contribution that generalizes beyond FP4. I would trust the quality result and the speedup direction, and re-verify the exact speedup and the layer-caching sensitivity on my own hardware before betting a training run on it.

For anyone implementing a version of this, the design has three separable pieces, and the first two are the ones worth stealing. The first is the objective swap: instead of a quantization-aware training loss that penalizes distance to a high-precision reference, you build a loss that penalizes distance between the two quantized paths at matched sites. That is a small change to the loss and a large change to what the optimizer is actually doing, and it is the piece that removes the specific failure where both paths look individually fine. The second is the information-caching contract: during rollout you emit a compact record of the rounding-relevant quantities, you store only what the training step needs, and you discard the rest. Getting that contract right is mostly an engineering problem, but it is the difference between a method that saves 5 times and a method that saves 5 times on compute and loses 2 times on communication. The third piece, the policy-adaptation effect, is not something you design for, it is something you get for free once the discrepancy is controlled, and I would treat it as a bonus rather than a target, because it depends on the reward landscape being stable enough that the FP4-adapted policy keeps improving instead of settling into a lower-precision plateau.

The paper also deserves credit for the honesty of its comparison set. It does not just compare against BF16, it compares against the full family of recent FP4-RL methods, plain QAT, QaRL, QUADS, Score Centering, and the post-hoc NVFP4 quantizers, and it reports the settings where TRACE wins and the settings where the gap to BF16 remains. The fact that the average on the 35B reasoning setup comes out above the BF16 reference rather than merely matching it is the kind of result that makes the method credible, but it is also the kind of result where I would want to confirm the evaluation protocol, the pass-at-1 over multiple samples, is applied identically to the BF16 run and the FP4 run, because a mismatch there is the easiest way for a number like that to be an artifact.
