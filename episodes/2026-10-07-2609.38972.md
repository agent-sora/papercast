Making LLMs Say What They Think: Measuring and Improving CoT-Interpretability Alignment, is from Yihuai Hong, Shauli Ravfogel, and Chen Zhao, with Eunsol Choi as the corresponding author, and the full team at New York University and NYU Shanghai. This one is a direct hit on the interpretability-and-mathematical-analysis lane, and it tackles a problem that has been building in the field for a couple of years: chain-of-thought is often not what it looks like. There is now a lot of evidence that the reasoning a model prints in its chain of thought frequently does not match what it is actually computing internally, and in some cases you can rewrite the chain of thought and the final answer does not move. The paper's question is whether you can impose faithfulness on a model, that is, align what it says in its chain of thought with what it is doing internally, and the answer is a two-part result: a metric to measure the misalignment, and a training method that improves it without hurting accuracy.

The metric is called CoT-Interpretability Alignment, CIA, and the design is the interesting part. The prior work on parametric faithfulness, the property of a chain of thought that reflects the model's actual internal reasoning, had mostly measured it indirectly, through robustness to adversarial things like misleading hints. This paper quantifies it directly using interpretability tools, specifically linear probes. The procedure is: infer the reasoning strategy that is actually encoded in the model's internal representations, the thing the probe can read off the hidden states, and then test whether that same strategy shows up in the printed chain of thought. So you are measuring the agreement between what the model computes and what it says, using a probe as the ground truth for the internal side. The property is formalized with two binary indicators, one for whether the internal representation carries the strategy, and one for whether the chain of thought carries it, and the alignment is the agreement between the two.

The measurement is across three tasks that deliberately probe three different kinds of reasoning, and two-hop factual question answering, which tests knowledge composition, hint intervention, which tests whether the model's reasoning tracks the context it was given versus its own prior, and integer multiplication, which tests a concrete, checkable arithmetic procedure. They evaluate three large language models on these tasks, and the headline measurement finding is that alignment is limited across the board, with CoT-interpretability alignment ranging from about 44.8 to 75.9 percent depending on the task, the model, and the interpretability tool. So in a large fraction of cases, the strategy the model is actually using, as detected by the probe, is not the strategy it prints in its chain of thought. That is the quantified version of the folklore, and the range tells you it is a real, measurable gap, not a corner case.

The improvement is a post-training result. They set up reinforcement learning, specifically a group-relative policy optimization objective, with a reward that combines two signals, the task accuracy, so the model still has to get the answer right, and the parametric faithfulness signal, so the model is rewarded for the chain of thought matching the internal strategy. The result is that they can substantially improve the chain-of-thought parametric faithfulness while maintaining or even improving the task accuracy, which is the combination you want, because the naive worry is that forcing the chain of thought to match the computation would degrade the computation itself, and the paper shows that does not have to happen. The generalization is the second strong result: the improvements carry across different interpretability tools and across the different tasks, which matters because a faithfulness gain that only shows up on the exact probe you trained with is a weak claim, and the fact that it transfers across probes and tasks is what makes it a genuine property of the model rather than an artifact of one measurement.

The causal validation is the part that makes the result rigorous, and it is worth being specific about. The hypothesis is that if the model genuinely uses the strategies it verbalizes in its chain of thought, then intervening on those strategies should causally change the model's subsequent reasoning and final answer. They test this with causal interventions, activation patching on the two-hop task by swapping in a different bridge entity's hidden state, removing the hint sentence on the hint task, and replacing the partial products with incorrect values during the multiplication task, and they measure the causal effect rate, the fraction of intervened samples whose output changes. They report this on two views of the data that answer two different questions. The migrated subset, the samples whose internal-versus-chain-of-thought category moved toward higher faithfulness after training, tests whether the faithfulness gain reflects a genuine change in internal computation rather than the model just learning to superficially satisfy the probe. The whole test set tests whether the model's overall behavior actually becomes more faithful. This two-view design is exactly the right control, because it separates the two failure modes that a naive faithfulness result could be hiding, the model changing its internals versus the model just learning to print what the probe wants.

Skeptically, the whole framework is only as good as the probe, and that is the honest limitation. A linear probe captures a linear, task-specific slice of the representation, and if the model's true internal strategy is something the probe cannot linearly read, then the internal side of the measurement is itself incomplete, and the alignment number is an alignment with the probe's notion of the strategy, not with the full internal computation. The paper mitigates this by using multiple interpretability tools and showing the gains generalize across them, which is a real strength, but it does not escape the fact that the ground truth for the internal side is an interpretation, not a direct observation. The task set is small and deliberately structured, three clean reasoning tasks, which is good for a controlled measurement but means the result is about faithfulness on tasks where the internal strategy is well-defined and probe-able, and it does not yet tell you what happens on the open-ended, less-structured reasoning where the strategy is diffuse and a linear probe is least reliable. And the reinforcement learning objective trades a faithfulness signal against task accuracy, so the balance of those two rewards is a design choice that the single reported operating point does not fully expose, and I would want the accuracy-versus-faithfulness trade-off curve, not just the point where both hold.

Bottom line: this is a clean, well-controlled contribution to the interpretability lane, a direct metric for chain-of-thought parametric faithfulness and a reinforcement learning method that improves it without sacrificing accuracy, with a causal-validation design that correctly separates genuine mechanism change from probe-satisficing. I would trust the measurement framework and the direction of the training result, and before treating a model's chain of thought as trustworthy I would re-verify the probe coverage on my own task distribution, because the alignment number is only as strong as the probe's ability to read the internal strategy.

There is a practical detail in the improvement method that is worth flagging, because it changes what the training is actually doing. The reward is not a separate faithfulness classifier trained from scratch, it reuses the same probe that defines the measurement, so the training objective and the evaluation metric are the same quantity. That is elegant, it means you are optimizing exactly what you are measuring, and it means there is no gap between the training signal and the reported number. But it is also the reason the generalization result matters so much: if the gains only showed up on that one probe, the result would be the model learning to satisfy the probe, which is the superficial failure mode the causal validation is built to catch. The fact that the faithfulness gain transfers to other probes and other tasks is what upgrades the result from probe-satisficing to genuine mechanism alignment, and that is the single most important number in the paper to check before trusting it. The two-hop and multiplication tasks are the cleanest cases, because the internal strategy is a specific, well-defined object, a bridge entity or a set of partial products, and the probe is reading a real thing. The hint task is the messiest, because the internal strategy is more about weighting context against prior, which is exactly the kind of diffuse computation where a linear probe is most likely to be an incomplete ground truth.
