Hunyuan-A13B Technical Report. This is a release from Tencent's Hunyuan team, and the author list is a long roster of seventy-five people, the first three named being Ao Liu, Botong Zhou, and Can Xu, so the honest byline is the team rather than any individual.

This is a new foundation model, so it is covered under the standing rule that any paper introducing a new foundation model gets an episode, and it is a real one, not a control layer or a fine-tune. Hunyuan-A13B is an open-source large language model built on a mixture-of-experts architecture. The number in the name is the active count: it has eighty billion total parameters but activates only thirteen billion during inference, which is the classic MoE trade, spend the parameters on capacity but pay only for the active ones at serving time. The design goal stated up front is to balance capability, compute efficiency, and deployment cost, and the whole report is organized around whether a thirteen-billion-active model can punch up into the range of much larger dense models.

The pre-training is the part worth reading for a technical audience. They trained on a rigorously filtered corpus of more than twenty trillion tokens, and they put particular emphasis on science, technology, engineering, and math data, building a refined labeling system and a multi-dimensional difficulty-grading framework to select harder, higher-quality STEM content. The stated motivation is that the quality of the STEM data sets the upper bound on reasoning ability, which is a fair claim given that competition math and science reasoning are where the frontier models actually differ. After the main pre-training they run a fast annealing stage to consolidate performance and a long-context stage that scales the window to two hundred fifty six thousand tokens. The architecture is a fine-grained MoE with one shared expert and sixty-four fine-grained non-shared experts, all at the same intermediate dimension, and the report notes from their own scaling experiments that having at least one shared expert matters, models without a shared expert underperform, while adding more than one shared expert gives diminishing returns.

The headline capability story is the dual-mode chain-of-thought. The model ships with two reasoning modes, a fast-thinking mode that produces concise short chains of thought for routine queries, and a slow-thinking mode that produces detailed long chains for complex multi-step problems, and the user or application can choose between them based on the complexity and resource constraints of the task. That is a meaningful design choice, because it decouples latency from depth: you do not pay the cost of a long reasoning chain when the question does not need one. The post-training is a structured multi-stage pipeline, reasoning-focused supervised fine-tuning, then targeted reinforcement learning on reasoning, then general supervised fine-tuning across domains, then a generalized RL phase for instruction following, with the dual-CoT schema layered on top.

The numbers, in the slow-thinking mode, are the interesting claim. Against models like DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3-A22B, and OpenAI's o1, Hunyuan-A13B is competitive on math, with AIME 2024 at eighty-seven point three, MATH at ninety-four point three, GPQA-Diamond at seventy-one point two, and OlympiadBench at eighty-two point seven, and it is notably strong on the agent benchmarks, where the report's own framing is that it significantly outperforms larger alternatives. On the agent table, BFCL v3 is at seventy-eight point three, C3-Bench at sixty-three point five, and ComplexFuncBench at sixty-one point two, which is where the claim of agent competence is strongest and where it edges out the comparators. The fast-thinking mode is a separate table, and it trades some depth for speed, as expected, with the math numbers lower but the function-calling and tool-use numbers still competitive, which is the whole point of offering the mode at all. On long context, using the RULER benchmark's question-answering task, it holds a strong score up through the sixty-four to one hundred twenty eight thousand token range, with performance decay the second best after one of the comparators, which is a reasonable result for a model with a two hundred fifty six thousand context window. The efficiency numbers are the selling point for a deployment audience: at sixteen batch with long input and output, throughput is in the thousands of tokens per second, and the report describes inference optimizations, including quantized KV caching and expert parallelism, that are aimed squarely at real-time, resource-constrained serving.

The skeptical read. This is a technical report from the team that built the model, so the benchmark table is self-reported, and the comparison set is chosen by the authors, which means the competitive framing, particularly the agent-benchmark claims, should be read with that in mind. The math numbers in slow-thinking mode are genuinely in the frontier-adjacent range, which is a real result for a thirteen-billion-active model, but the more interesting and less standard claim is the agent one, that a smaller active model can beat larger ones on agentic tasks, and that is the claim most worth independently reproducing, because it is the one that would actually change a deployment decision. The dual-mode design is the most practically useful contribution, because it gives you a knob for the latency-versus-depth tradeoff that most single-mode reasoning models do not offer, and the long-context and throughput results, while not record-breaking, are consistent with the stated goal of an efficient deployable model. The open question is whether the STEM-curation story is doing the work the report credits it with, because that is the part that would transfer to a from-scratch training run and that a smaller team could not easily replicate from the report alone. If you are evaluating open-weight MoE models for a cost-sensitive deployment, this is worth a hands-on test, particularly of the fast-thinking mode and the agent benchmarks, but treat the leaderboard edges as the authors' until you reproduce them. Verdict: a capable open-source MoE reasoning model whose dual-mode chain-of-thought is the most distinctive feature and whose agent-benchmark strength is the claim most worth verifying independently, with the caveat that it is a self-reported technical report against an author-chosen comparison set.

The pre-training data story has a specific mechanism behind it that is worth noting, because it is the part of the report that is hardest to reproduce and most likely to be doing the work. The STEM emphasis is not just a curation filter; they built a multi-dimensional difficulty-grading framework that labels training examples by difficulty across several axes, which lets them preferentially select the harder examples in the science and math domains rather than just de-noising the easy ones. That is a meaningfully different intervention from a quality filter, because a quality filter removes the bad examples while a difficulty grader up-weights the examples that actually stretch the model, and the report frames the whole STEM effort as raising the upper bound on reasoning rather than improving average data quality. The twenty-trillion-token corpus also runs a long-context stage that extends the window to two hundred fifty six thousand tokens, which is what the later RULER long-context numbers are measuring, and the fast annealing stage in between is the standard consolidation pass that stabilizes the model before the window is scaled. For a technical reader, the honest question is how much of the competitive math result comes from the difficulty grading versus the raw scale of the corpus, and the report does not ablate that separation, so the claim that STEM curation sets the upper bound is plausible but not isolated from the twenty-trillion-token budget that makes the curation affordable in the first place.

The post-training pipeline is the more conventional part, and it follows the current standard structure closely enough that a reader should not over-interpret any single stage. Reasoning-focused supervised fine-tuning on long chains of thought comes first, then a targeted reinforcement learning phase on reasoning tasks, then general supervised fine-tuning across domains, then a generalized reinforcement learning phase for instruction following, with the dual-mode chain-of-thought schema layered on top of the whole thing. That ordering, reasoning before general, is the current consensus for reasoning models, and the report does not claim any novelty there, which is fine, because the novelty is in the architecture and the dual-mode design rather than the recipe. The dual-mode design is worth a second look, because it is the feature that most distinguishes this model from a single-mode reasoning model. In practice it means the deployment side can route easy queries through the fast-thinking path, which produces short chains and returns quickly, and reserve the slow-thinking path, with its long chains, for the queries that actually need the depth, and the two modes are evaluated on separate tables in the report. The fast-thinking table shows the expected tradeoff, lower math scores than the slow mode but still competitive function-calling and tool-use numbers, which is exactly the profile you want from a latency-optimized path, and it is the reason the mode exists at all. The efficiency side is aimed at the same goal, with quantized key-value caching, tensor and expert parallelism, and fused mixture-of-experts kernels, and the reported throughput, in the thousands of tokens per second at batch sixteen with long sequences, is the number a deployment team would actually benchmark against their own hardware, because the paper's accelerator is not necessarily the one in your cluster.
