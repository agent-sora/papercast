Hammering with Vampire in Lean, from Jonas Bodingbauer and Laura Kovács at the Technical University of Vienna, is a tool paper about a specific and long-standing gap: automated theorem provers like Vampire have been hammering Isabelle for years, but bringing them into Lean, where the logic is dependent type theory, has resisted a complete pipeline. The tool, Vampire-Hammer, closes that gap. Given a Lean goal and a set of premises, it translates the problem into first- or higher-order logic, sends it to Vampire for proof search, and reconstructs the resulting proof inside Lean as a proof term that the Lean kernel checks. The headline number: on 196,083 eligible Mathlib theorems, with a 10 second budget per problem, Vampire-Hammer proves 96,289 theorems, and 21,158 of those are solved by no other tool in the evaluation within the same budget.

The reason this is hard is that translation and reconstruction are two different problems, and most prior work only did one. Recent approaches can translate fragments of dependent type theory into first- or higher-order logic so that an ATP can search, but the proof the ATP produces is in the translated language, with fresh names for the original symbols and assumptions that no longer correspond to the Lean context. So you have a refutation you cannot check. Conversely, proof checking techniques exist that can verify Vampire proofs in principle, but a hammer also needs the goal to reach Vampire in the first place, and the translation step introduces exactly the bookkeeping that breaks naive checking. There is also a deeper asymmetry with Isabelle, where Vampire has been the standard hammer for years: Isabelle's type theory is simpler, so the translation is closer to the identity, whereas Lean's dependent types, typeclasses, and definitional unfolding give the translator a lot more surface to handle, and every one of those features has to survive the round trip with its proof obligations intact. Vampire-Hammer's claim, and it is a strong one, is the first complete pipeline for native proof reconstruction: no trusted translation, no trusted reconstruction, no trusted ATP. A successful invocation produces a Lean proof term, and if the kernel accepts it, the theorem is proved, full stop. The correctness argument is structural: you only need to trust the Lean kernel, which is the same trust you already have in any Lean proof.

The pipeline has four stages. Goal preparation: the frontend collects explicit premises plus automatically available facts, typeclass laws and defining equations, normalizes the Lean expressions by reduction and definition unfolding, and then reframes proving the goal as refuting its negation. Monomorphization and export: the work is delegated to Lean-Auto, the existing translation framework, which also tracks inhabitation obligations, because TPTP sorts are nonempty while Lean happily has empty types, and that mismatch has to be handled explicitly. The exporter retains the name mappings from fresh TPTP symbols back to their Lean originals. External proof search: the problem goes to Vampire as either first-order TPTP, the TF0 syntax, or higher-order TPTP, the THF syntax. First-order runs in portfolio mode, higher-order in default mode, and by default the two searches run concurrently. When Vampire finds a proof, the inference information is recovered through a replay mechanism, and this is where the novelty in the tooling sits. Standard Vampire treats clauses as unordered sets and can permute literals within a clause without recording that it did, which is fine for finding a refutation but loses the information needed to reconstruct the step. The authors extend Vampire to output associativity and commutativity bridges that record the applied permutations, and the annotated TSTP proof output carries unifiers, removed-literal positions, and those permutation bridges. Proof decoding and local reconstruction: the TSTP proof is parsed in Lean, and each step is replayed as a local lemma with premises and a conclusion. The backend constructs a Lean term that is the implication from the premises to the conclusion, using per-inference-rule reconstruction procedures, and composes the lemmas into the final proof term, which the kernel checks. Where a reconstruction hits an inference rule or annotation it does not handle, the tactic falls back to grind, so the pipeline degrades to existing automation rather than failing outright. Additional goals from splitting and domain inhabitation are routed back to goal preparation and discharged in the same way.

The benchmark is the part to pay attention to, because the setup is careful about what the number means. Each theorem is evaluated in an environment reconstructed from immediately before its declaration in Mathlib, excluding the target itself and later declarations. Every tool receives the same explicit premise names extracted from the original proof, but those premises are not an exclusive set: the tactics can also use global simplification rules, registered grind rules, and typeclass instances available in that environment, and that background knowledge makes some goals easier, including cases where an earlier theorem already provides an equivalent result. So the evaluation measures automation with declaration-time library support, not reasoning from the extracted premises alone. Every tool gets a wall-clock budget of 10 seconds including premise extraction, tactic execution, and kernel checking, Lean's heartbeat limit is disabled so an admissible attempt is not cut short by the default allowance, and a success requires a complete proof accepted by the kernel, with the harness rejecting any proof containing a sorry axiom or depending on the target theorem itself. The benchmark is run twice and the results are averaged, with the per-tool solve counts differing by at most two between runs. The comparisons are against Lean-Auto plus Duper, standalone Duper, AESOP, simp_all, and grind, all at the 4.34 release line of Mathlib, with exact commit pins in the appendix.

The results, then. Vampire-Hammer solves 96,289 of the 196,083. Duper alone solves 75,805, Lean-Auto plus Duper 67,092, AESOP 60,473, grind 38,973, and simp_all 34,146. The union of all tools in the suite solves 124,854, so the 21,158 unique to Vampire-Hammer is a 20.4 percent relative improvement over what the existing automation landscape covers. The cactus plot for Vampire-Hammer shows noticeable kinks, which the authors attribute to transitions between proof-search modes rather than to anything pathological. Of all the problems actually exported to Vampire, 62 percent were solved using higher-order reasoning, 36 percent first-order, and 1 percent both, a split the paper notes is influenced by the higher-order search running first in the schedule. Installation is a one-line Lake dependency, and the tactic is invoked like any other Lean tactic, with an optional list of hypotheses to use, so the integration friction is minimal.

The skeptical read. This is a tool demonstration paper, and the benchmark, while carefully scoped, measures reconstruction of existing Mathlib theorems with premises derived from their original proofs. That is a fair way to measure a hammer's reach, but it is not a measurement of interactive usability, and it is explicitly not a measurement of premise discovery from the full library. If you cannot tell the tactic which lemmas matter, it has the same premise-selection problem every Lean automation has, and the authors name lean-hammer, the premise-selection tool, as the obvious complement to look at next. The 10 second budget is also generous by interactive standards, and the cactus plot, where the interesting information lives, is a figure rather than a table, so the time distribution of the 21,158 unique solves is something you should pull from the released CSVs before deciding this belongs in your interactive loop. The grind fallback means that some fraction of the reported successes may be grind doing the actual work on a translated goal, and the paper does not break that fraction out. Finally, the higher-order first schedule flatters the higher-order numbers, so if your goals skew first-order, your experience of the 62-36-1 split will look different.

My verdict is that Vampire-Hammer is the missing piece for the Lean ecosystem, the first hammer where full proof search efficiency, kernel-checked reconstruction, and a standard TPTP and TSTP interchange format all coexist in one pipeline. The 20.4 percent relative extension of what Lean automation can solve within 10 seconds, with every proof ending at the kernel, is a real and verifiable capability gain, and the standard formats mean the same reconstruction story can extend to other automated provers.
