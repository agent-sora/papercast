The paper is RoTA-Bench: Evaluating 3D Spatial Reasoning through Interactive Projection Alignment, from Qingchuan Ma, Yuexiao Ma, and Xiang Meng, from Xiamen University and Nanyang Technological University. It is a benchmark for a specific and hard skill, the ability to reason in three dimensions by repeatedly taking a rotated view of an object and choosing which rotation to apply next in order to line up that view with a target projection. The reason to build it is that current vision-language models are very good at static spatial questions, where you show them an image and ask which way something faces, and very bad at the interactive version, where the answer has to be produced through a sequence of decisions that build on each other. The headline finding is that the gap is large. GPT-5.6-Sol, their strongest model, succeeds on only 17 percent of tasks, while all of the human task runs succeed.

The task is concrete. You are shown a 3D object from some unknown orientation, plus a target image of what that object should look like from the reference view. At each step you can request one of four legal rotations of the object, and after each rotation you get a new 2D projection. Your goal is to steer the object back to the reference orientation. The measure of success is the intersection-over-union between your current projection and the target, and a task is a success when you reach a high overlap. This is different from a one-shot spatial question in two ways that matter. First, the model has to maintain a running hypothesis about the object's current pose, because no single view tells you the full orientation. A single 2D projection is a many-to-one map, so several distinct 3D poses can produce nearly identical silhouettes, and the model has to use the sequence of views it has gathered to disambiguate them. Second, each rotation is a decision that changes what you can see next, so the model is effectively doing a short planning horizon, deciding which rotation gives it the most useful information or the most progress. The authors build an automated pipeline that generates the tasks from 3D assets, yielding 674 generated tasks and a stratified 100-task test set, and the stratification is by how far the object starts from the reference view, so the test set contains both near-trivial alignments and ones that require a full sequence of rotations. That construction matters, because it means the difficulty is controlled and comparable across models, not left to whatever the asset library happened to produce.

The evaluation covers nine vision-language models, the GPT-5.6 family, Gemini 3.6 Flash, Claude Opus 5, Claude Sonnet 5, Qwen 3.7 Plus, GPT-6 Astra, and a specialized spatial model, SpatioLM. The result is that every frontier model struggles. GPT-5.6-Sol achieves the highest success rate at 17.0 percent, followed by Gemini 3.6 Flash at 10.0 percent, and ReAct attains the highest final IoU of 0.628 with a 16.0 percent success rate. Three of the models, Claude Sonnet 5, Claude Opus 5, and Qwen 3.7 Plus, fail entirely, with a 0 percent success rate, and their trajectories plateau early. The human runs, by contrast, complete all of them, with overall IoU gains that cross the 0.90 threshold, reaching an average final IoU of 0.923 in 16.22 steps. So the human-model gap on interactive projection alignment is one of the largest I have seen on a spatial benchmark.

Two greedy baselines sharpen the picture. Each enumerates all 24 legal rotation successors using the ground-truth simulator, then selects the action that maximizes the successor projection IoU, or that minimizes the true 3D pose error. The IoU-greedy baseline succeeds on only 39.0 percent of tasks with a final IoU of 0.725, which shows that greedily maximizing immediate projection overlap is not enough on many tasks, while the pose-greedy baseline, which optimizes the actual 3D pose rather than the 2D silhouette, is what completes them. That distinction is the paper's clearest argument that the models are failing at the projection-to-pose inference, not at the rotation execution.

The paper does a useful decomposition of why the models fail, and this is the part worth reading. They split the task into directional choice and angular precision. For directional choice, GPT-5.6-Sol selects an effective rotation direction far less often than humans, 58.75 percent versus 83.91 percent. For angular precision, conditioned on choosing the right direction, the model's fine-grained control is also weak, with effective rates in the low fifties to low sixties. So the failure is not a single thing, it is both choosing the wrong kind of rotation and then not executing the right one precisely enough. They also show that the models are not failing for lack of exploration. GPT-5.6-Sol and GPT-6 Astra achieve comparable total view coverage, around 79 percent, but they look at the wrong regions, and the regions they view and the order in which they view them are what separate a successful run from a plateauing one. That is an important distinction, because it rules out the easy explanation that the model is not looking at enough of the object.

There is one more ablation that I find telling. They remove an auxiliary view that GPT-5.6-Sol can request, and task success drops from 16.67 percent in the full input setting to 3.33 percent, with final IoU falling from 0.5656 to 0.5490. That says the model does use the extra observation when it is available, and that the bottleneck is not simply the visual input but the reasoning over it. They also find the model's fine-grained control is sensitive to its own interaction history, and that conditioning on choosing an effective direction, the model's mean angular deviation from the best legal angle is 6.49 degrees versus 2.99 degrees for humans. The paper frames all of this as evidence that the models can perceive the object but cannot plan the rotation sequence, which is a different and arguably more fundamental gap than a perception gap.

What I would trust is the benchmark design and the negative result. The task is well-specified, the automatic generation pipeline makes it reproducible, and the 100-task stratified test set with confidence intervals is the right scale to claim a gap. The human baseline succeeding everywhere is a clean anchor, and the decomposition into direction and angle is exactly the analysis you need to know where the failure lives. I also like that they rule out the exploration explanation, because that is the first thing a skeptic would check, and they check it.

What I would re-verify is the generality of the 17 percent number. This is a narrow task, rotating objects to match a reference view, and it is the kind of task where the exact set of legal rotations, the number of steps allowed, and the IoU success threshold all matter a lot. A 17 percent success rate is striking, but I would want to see it under a looser threshold and a longer horizon before concluding that interactive 3D reasoning is broadly broken, because the benchmark may be harder than the underlying skill. The fact that three models score zero is also worth a grain of salt, because a zero can mean the model does not know the action format as much as it means it cannot solve the task, and the paper should be clearer about how much of that is a format issue. And the human baseline is 400 runs, which is fine, but it is a small number for a success-rate claim.

Two-sentence verdict: RoTA-Bench is a clean, well-anchored benchmark that puts a precise number on a real weakness, frontier vision-language models are dramatically worse than humans at interactive 3D projection alignment, and the failure decomposes into both choosing the wrong rotation and imprecise execution rather than a lack of visual exploration. The pose-greedy baseline completing tasks that the IoU-greedy one cannot is the cleanest single piece of evidence that the gap is in the projection-to-pose inference. It is a narrow task, so treat the 17 percent as a strong lower bound on the difficulty of interactive 3D reasoning rather than a full measure of the capability.
