The paper is called World In-Context Learning for Agentic Robot Manipulation. The first three authors are Heng Fang, Zehui Guo, and Yuehan Zhang, working at Huazhong University of Science and Technology, with co-authors from ArcheOne AI.

The motivating observation is a mismatch that most robot-learning people recognize but do not fix. The dominant way to teach a robot to manipulate things is to train an action policy, usually a vision-language-action model or a world action model, and write the manipulation skill into the network weights. That works, and it has produced real progress. But the skill is bound to the distribution it was trained on. The paper gives a concrete number that shows how badly: a widely used world action model trained on clean demonstrations of the RoboTwin two-point-zero benchmark reaches seventy seven point eight percent mean success on clean scenes, but drops to one point nine percent under domain randomization. Every new task or new distribution means new data and new training. The alternative, in-context learning, avoids that coupling because a frozen model adapts to a new task from what its context shows, with no gradient update at all. Recent models can already perform a new manipulation task from a single demonstration given in their context. But in-context learning has not matched trained policies, and the reason the authors pin down precisely is that the context carries no feedback from the world. A demonstration recorded in another scene cannot tell the robot what will happen on the table in front of it.

Their key insight is that what an agent learns in context is semantic. It records task steps, object roles, and what went wrong, not the visual appearance of the world. So experience gathered in one world transfers to another, as long as the two worlds agree on the semantics of the task. A cheap world that is faithful to the task, such as a simulator, a learned world model, or even a latent model of the scene, is enough to learn in. Rehearsal in that world is also safe in a way acting directly on real hardware is not, where unsafe actions have damaged equipment.

They formalize this as World In-Context Learning, or WICL. A WICL learner keeps its weights frozen, starts from a demonstration, rehearses the task in a world, and writes the feedback into its context rather than its weights. After rehearsal, the context is frozen and evaluated on instances that were never rehearsed, so the measured result is generalization, not memorization. The context is readable text, which means the agent can review its own evaluations and rewrite what it has learned, enabling what they call recursive self-improvement without any gradient updates. The world is what makes every round of that improvement safe. They draw an explicit analogy to coding agents, which learn a new repository by running code in a terminal and reading what comes back. The feedback loop that coding agents rely on has had no counterpart in robot manipulation until now, and the argument is that a simulator can finally play the same role the terminal does for software.

They build the infrastructure as RoboScript, which is modular. Every component runs as an independent service and can be swapped out, and the whole thing is organized so that the agent plane reaches the world only through a deployable interface while a separate control plane holds the privileged simulator data. That separation is doing real work: every acting agent, whether in rehearsal or in deployment, observes only camera images with depth and proprioception, the same inputs available on a real robot. Privileged simulator information is used only as supervision during learning, and a memory linter rejects coordinates, ground-truth terms, and seed identifiers. The point of the linter is that what is learned in simulation is directly deployable on physical hardware, and they enforce that by construction rather than as an afterthought.

The main result is the one you want to hear first. On all fifty tasks of RoboTwin two-point-zero, with one clean demonstration per task and no gradient updates, RoboScript reaches eighty three point four percent mean success across the clean and randomized settings. The strongest trained action policy, trained with fifty demonstrations, reaches sixty one point seven percent. So one demonstration plus rehearsal in a world beats a policy trained on fifty demonstrations by more than twenty points, with no weights updated. That is the central claim, and it is backed by a table that lists a dozen other baselines. The next-best in-context-style entries like four-dimensional WAM and LingBot reach the high seventies to eighty one percent on clean scenes but fall to the low thirties and forties under randomization, whereas RoboScript stays at eighty three point six on clean and eighty three point two randomized. The gap under randomization is where the method clearly earns its keep.

Five findings come out of the experiments. First, one demonstration with rehearsal is enough, as I just described. Second, what is learned in context generalizes across distributions: rehearsing only on clean scenes, RoboScript keeps comparable success under domain randomization, whereas the trained action policies retain at most sixty five point one percent of their clean-scene success. Third, reflection improves the frozen memory: each round of the recursive self-improvement rescues more episodes than it breaks on the difficult tasks. Fourth, the framework improves with the foundation model, and this one is striking. With no change to code, prompts, or protocol, one foundation model reaches eighty three point six and eighty three point two on clean and randomized scenes, a second reaches seventy nine point eight and seventy six point zero, and even a lightweight model reaches seventy seven point eight and seventy eight point four, which still surpasses the strongest trained action policy. So the ceiling of the method is set by the model you put behind it, and it scales with that. Fifth, the learned task memories transfer across embodiments: with a lightweight adapter that rewrites an existing memory for a new robot, they deploy on a physical dual-arm robot without retraining or re-rehearsing.

Now the skeptical pass. The headline numbers are impressive, but I want to flag three things before you treat this as settled. One, the entire result leans on the simulator being faithful to the semantics of the task. The method is only as good as the rehearsal world, and the paper is careful to say a cheap faithful world is enough, but faithfulness is exactly the thing that is hard to guarantee and easy to take for granted. If the simulator gets the contact dynamics or the object interactions wrong, the semantic memory you distill is quietly wrong too, and the linter will not catch that because it only checks for leaked privileged data, not for physics errors. Two, the comparison to trained policies is partly a comparison to their weakest failure mode. The most damning number in their own table is the one point nine percent under randomization for the strong world action model, which is a real result but also a setup where the baseline is failing hard. The fairest read is that in-context learning wins precisely because the trained policy cannot transfer, and the interesting open question is whether a better-trained, better-regularized policy closes that gap. Three, recursive self-improvement is the most ambitious claim and the least bounded. Each reflection round rescues more episodes than it breaks on difficult tasks, which is the right kind of ablation, but "improves without gradient updates" is a strong phrase and the paper would be stronger with a clear stopping criterion and a look at whether the gains saturate or can regress on the easy tasks.

There is also a practical engineering note that actually favors this approach. Because the memory is readable text and the deployment interface sees only real-robot-available sensors, the system is auditable in a way a trained policy is not. You can read the task memory, you can see exactly what the agent thinks it learned, and you can lint it. For a safety-sensitive deployment, that inspectability is not a minor feature.

Here is my bottom line. This is a genuinely clean result: one demonstration plus rehearsal in a simulator, with no gradient updates, beats the best trained action policy by more than twenty points on a fifty-task benchmark and holds under domain randomization where the trained policy collapses. The method is modular, deployable by construction, and its ceiling scales with the underlying foundation model. The caveats are that it inherits the faithfulness of its rehearsal world, that its edge over trained policies is largest exactly where those policies fail to transfer, and that the recursive self-improvement loop is promising but not yet bounded by a clear stopping criterion.
