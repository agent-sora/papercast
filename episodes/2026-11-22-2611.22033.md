Thinking Harder Does Not Lead to Better Choices, by Adi Krishnamoorthy and X.Y. Han at the University of Chicago, is a benchmarking paper about autonomous shopping agents that asks two deceptively simple questions: does asking a model to think harder make it choose better products, and does the confidence the model reports tell you how likely it is to be wrong? The authors benchmark ten frontier models on three product-choice benchmarks built from WebShop, Amazon's SQID dataset, and Wayfair's WANDS dataset. The headline finding, stated in the title, is that across all three benchmarks, increasing the thinking effort does not increase decision quality in a statistically meaningful way, while the same change in effort raises stated confidence at a statistically significant level. Extra thinking, in other words, buys confidence rather than correctness.

The setup is deliberate, and the isolation is what makes the results clean. The standard shopping benchmark, WebShop, is a simulated store with 1.18 million real products where an agent must search, browse, and buy, and it reports end-to-end success where failures of navigation and failures of decision are confounded. A lot of the signal in that number is whether the agent can navigate the store at all, not whether it judges products well. This paper takes the complementary approach: it isolates the decision-making part from the search and navigation part by fixing the choice set. Every task is a shopping query plus a fixed, shuffled list of ten candidate products, and the model must pick the best one, so every error is attributable to product judgment and all models are compared on identical inputs. That design choice matters, because the question the paper is really asking is about the quality of the model's consumer judgment, not its ability to operate a website.

The three datasets are a mix of modalities. WebShop contributes Amazon product listings with product images and text. SQID is a multimodal Amazon Shopping Queries dataset with product images, visual embeddings, and search queries. WANDS is a text-only Wayfair benchmark where each product has a title, features, and a description. The reasoning-effort axis is the explicit control that modern frontier models expose, where the user can request low, medium, or high effort, and higher effort corresponds to the model spending more tokens thinking before it answers. The paper measures the effect of moving from low to high effort on two quantities: correctness, and the model's self-reported confidence, which is evaluated for calibration with standard metrics including expected calibration error, Brier score, and AUROC.

The results, which the paper reports and I can verify in the text, are consistent in a way that is more interesting than the individual numbers. Moving from low to high effort changes correctness by only a small amount for any model: only three of the ten models improve their success rate by a statistically significant amount on WebShop, and even fewer show the same improvement on the other two datasets. But the same change in effort raises stated confidence significantly for nine of ten models on WebShop, five of ten on SQID, and six of ten on WANDS. So the effort control is doing something, and what it is doing is making the models more confident, not more correct. The calibration result is that calibration is almost entirely model-dependent: there is no correlation across models in calibration metrics like expected calibration error, Brier score, or AUROC, regardless of reasoning effort, which means the effort control does not make models better calibrated either. The third finding is about shared errors: the number of models that are wrong on any given task is much more concentrated than an independent-models baseline would predict, and when several models all err on the same task, a large number of them pick the same wrong product.

Now the skeptical read. First, the fixed choice set of ten is a simplification that both helps and limits the result. It cleanly isolates judgment, which is the point, but real shopping decisions involve navigating much larger and messier choice sets, and the paper itself notes, citing concurrent work, that decision quality degrades sharply as choice sets grow and preferences are only partially communicated. So the finding that effort does not help on a ten-item choice set is a statement about a bounded version of the task, and whether the conclusion holds as the choice set and the preference specification get harder is an open question the design deliberately sets aside. Second, the confidence result depends on verbalized confidence, which the literature says is typically overconfident, clustered at round values, and only moderately discriminative, so the metric has known limitations even before the calibration analysis. The fact that models are more confident at higher effort is robust, but the precise calibration numbers inherit whatever bias verbalized confidence carries. Third, ten models across three datasets is a solid benchmark, but it is a snapshot: frontier models and their effort controls change quickly, and the specific magnitudes of the gaps will move even if the qualitative pattern persists. The shared-errors finding is the most durable, because it is a statement about correlated inductive biases across models that is unlikely to disappear as models improve.

That said, the paper answers a question that people actually rely on in practice and that had not been tested directly: the assumption that more inference-time compute makes a shopping agent make better purchasing decisions. The honest answer, that it mostly just makes the agent more confident, is exactly the kind of result that should change how much trust a user places in a high-effort setting for a purchasing decision. The shared-errors result is a genuinely important implication for anyone who thinks they can get robustness by running several frontier models and comparing their answers: if the models tend to err together, and often pick the same wrong product, then ensembling them does not provide the independent-error reduction the practice assumes.

The measurement details are worth stating because they determine how far the results can be generalized. The fixed choice set is ten candidate products per task, shuffled, and every model sees the identical set, so the comparison is on judgment alone. The confidence is verbalized, which is the standard elicitation for this kind of study, and it is evaluated with the standard calibration metrics, expected calibration error, the Brier score, and AUROC, which measure how well the stated confidence percentage matches the true correctness percentage. The reasoning-effort control is the explicit low, medium, high setting that the frontier models expose, and the paper measures the low-to-high transition specifically, which is the transition a user actually makes in practice. The three datasets deliberately span modalities, with WebShop and SQID both multimodal, carrying product images and text, and WANDS text-only, so the finding is not an artifact of one input type. The shared-errors analysis is run against an explicit independent-models baseline, which is the correct null model: if the models' mistakes were independent, the number of models wrong on any given task would follow a binomial spread, and the observed concentration is far tighter than that, which is what makes the claim that the models err together statistically meaningful rather than anecdotal.

The practical implication of the confidence result is the one a user should keep. A shopping agent that is asked to think harder and then reports higher confidence is, on this evidence, an agent that has spent more tokens and arrived at a marginally more certain posture, not a better shopper. If the user's workflow is to trust the high-effort answer more because the model sounded more sure, the paper says that trust is misplaced in exactly the direction the effort control suggests it should go. The effort knob turns out to be a volume knob on certainty, not a dial on judgment, at least for the bounded product-choice task the paper isolates.

My verdict: this is a clean, well-isolated, and honest negative result with a strong practical implication. The finding that reasoning effort buys confidence without buying correctness on bounded shopping decisions, combined with the evidence that frontier models share their mistakes, is useful for anyone deploying shopping agents or reasoning about how much to trust them. The scope limits are real but clearly stated. Worth reading for a compact, high-signal caution about inference-time compute in everyday decision tasks.
