From Evidence to Action: How Tool-Using Agents Fail, is from Hongzhan Lin, Shidong Cao, and Ziyang Luo, with the full team of six authors spanning the National University of Singapore, Hong Kong Baptist University, Amazon Web Services, and Princeton University, led by Wynne Hsu. The paper studies a specific and consequential failure mode: the agent gets the right answer, but not because it was supposed to. It changes external state, and the change happens to be correct, but the change was not actually supported by evidence the agent had established beforehand. The paper's question is where that evidence-to-action chain breaks, as the agent moves from deciding whether to act, to executing a single action, to executing a dependent sequence of actions.

This matters because tool-using agents are increasingly the thing we point at real systems, and the evaluation practice in the field rewards outcomes. If your benchmark scores you on did-the-final-state-end-up-correct, then an agent that takes an action on a whim, happens to get the right outcome, and never actually checks its work, will score as well as one that carefully gathers evidence first. The paper's position is that outcome correctness is a necessary but not sufficient condition for the kind of agent you can trust with real-world side effects, and it builds a methodology to tell the two apart.

To make this measurable, the team introduces SafeActBench, a suite of 656 cases across six operational domains: customer and policy operations, engineering and infrastructure, legal and financial operations, research assistance, smart-home control, and healthcare operations. The design follows three principles. First, the difficulty is staged. There is a static protocol where the agent just makes an allow, block, or defer judgment over a fixed candidate action, and then four interactive regimes that progress from investigated non-action, where the agent must finish the investigation that justifies not acting and then stop without producing the side effect, to single-action execution, to a linear multi-action workflow, to a dependency-constrained workflow where the dependencies form a directed acyclic graph. Second, a provenance-bound Evidence Ledger tracks, for each consequential action, whether the required evidence was actually established for the correct entity and state before execution. A fact can be true in the underlying environment and still not count, if the agent never observed it. Third, a deterministic evaluator replays the trajectory and checks the evidence, the exact tools, targets, and arguments, the execution receipts, the dependencies, and the resulting state changes, without looking at the model's hidden reasoning and without an LLM judge. That last property is a real strength, because the scores are reproducible and not at the mercy of a judge model's mood.

The headline finding is that strong static action assessment can coexist with much weaker interactive execution. Ten model-harness configurations are evaluated, five model families, Claude-5, GPT-5.6, DeepSeek-V4, Qwen3.8, and GLM-5.2, each paired with its family-associated harness and with a shared ReAct harness. Two patterns stand out. First, the static judge scores are high across the board, roughly 78 to 98 percent on the legacy decision protocol, but the interactive exact-case-success scores are far lower, ranging from about 38 to 67 percent overall, and the spread between configurations is enormous. The starkest example is GLM under its own ZCode harness, which exceeds 97 percent on the static decision protocol but collapses to between 12 and 34 percent on the interactive protocols, while DeepSeek under its family harness stays around 60 percent on those same protocols. The paper holds the case identity fixed for a subset and re-evaluates: static accuracy stays at least 95 percent while interactive success drops to at most 52 percent. So the gap is not about the tasks, it is about whether the agent establishes the evidence before it acts.

The failure diagnostics localize where the chain breaks, and the story is more interesting than the aggregate numbers suggest. Failures mostly happen before execution, not during it. In the non-action protocol, stopping before completing the required investigation ranges from about 22 to 63 percent of episodes. In the single-action protocol, acting before the evidence is complete ranges from 37 to 67 percent of action-attempt episodes. But conditional on the evidence being established and an action being attempted, single-action execution is highly reliable, 93 to 100 percent for nine of the ten configurations. The multi-action protocols add two more failure signatures: unresolved prerequisites at an action checkpoint, and workflows that are started but left incomplete, and which of those dominates varies by configuration.

The controlled evidence interventions are the part I found most useful and most alarming. On 43 single-action cases, they make one decisive record unavailable, and the agents still act in roughly 47 to 54 percent of the completed withheld episodes, even though withholding reduces the action probability by 37 to 45 points. When they instead contradict a prerequisite field, the action probability drops by 54 to 70 points on the eligible subset, but that reduction does not necessarily mean the refusal was correct. In one configuration, of the 66 withheld episodes that still included an action, 65 called the affected tool, so the agent queried the missing record and then acted anyway. The takeaway the authors draw is that retrieving relevant information is not the same as acting only when the decisive support is present, and the presentation of the missing evidence, whether you show the tool responses or have the requester claim the record was checked, measurably changes whether the action is submitted.

Skeptically, this is the kind of paper I would trust most of all, because the evaluation is deterministic, the cases are hand-authored with all 656 reference solutions passing the evaluator, and the claims are modest and well-localized. The main things I would push on: 656 cases is a modest sample, and six domains means the findings are about business-operational tool use, not about the open-ended, long-horizon, multi-environment agent tasks people actually deploy, so I would not extrapolate the exact percentages. The interventions are on a fixed set of 43 cases, which is fine for a mechanism probe but too small for a robust effect size. And the model-harness pairing means the harness effects are entangled with each model's own tooling, so the harness result is a property of the specific harness implementations used, not a universal fact about a given model.

Bottom line: SafeActBench is a clean, deterministic, and genuinely useful benchmark, and the central result, that agents can be strong static judges of actions but weak at establishing the evidence before acting, is a finding the field should take seriously. If you are deploying tool-using agents against real state, the lesson is that outcome-based evaluation is letting through a class of agents that would not survive contact with a production system, and the fix is to score the evidence-to-action chain, not just the endpoint.

There is a design choice in the benchmark that is worth calling out as a strength and a limitation at once. By making the evidence requirements and expected actions private, hidden from the agent, the benchmark guarantees that the agent cannot game the evaluation by reading the rubric, and it guarantees that the evidence the agent uses is the evidence it actually gathered. The cost is that the agent never gets to see the bar it is being held to, which is realistic for a real deployment, where the policy is implicit, but it also means the benchmark is testing a specific notion of evidence that the authors have encoded into the case specifications, and a reasonable reader might disagree with which facts count as decisive for a given action. That is a defensible design, it just means the numbers are a property of this particular encoding of consequential action, and a different encoding would shift them. The deterministic evaluator, by contrast, is a contribution you can reuse wholesale, the pattern of binding facts to their source interactions and their target entity and state, and then replaying the trajectory against that ledger, is a general tool for auditing any state-changing agent, and I would expect to see it adopted in other evaluation setups.

The practical implication for anyone shipping these agents is not that the models are bad, it is that the evaluation was wrong. The strongest model-harness configurations are all quite good at the static decision, which is the part everyone has been benchmarking, and they are all middling at the part that determines whether the agent is safe to point at a real refund button. The interventions show that the failure is not purely a lack of information, because withholding information does reduce action, it is a failure of the decision rule under incomplete evidence, the agent treats retrieved-but-not-decisive information as sufficient. That is a tractable failure, and it is the kind of failure that targeted training on investigated non-action and on evidence-gated execution should attack, which is the natural follow-up I would expect from this line of work.
