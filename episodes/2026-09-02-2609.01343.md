SMELT: Scaling Laws for Compute-Matched MoE Looped Transformers, by Shaowen Wang, Ge Zhang, and Kairong Luo of Tsinghua University, ByteDance Seed, M-A-P, and TokenWave AI, with further authors from those labs. Looped transformers repeat a shared block of layers instead of stacking new ones, and the pitch is seductive: reasoning depth without extra parameters, with looped models matching or beating unlooped models several times their size on arithmetic and math. But there is a catch the literature mostly waves away: looping a twelve-layer model to twenty-four executed layers stores half the weights yet spends roughly a twenty-four-layer model's compute per token and needs its full key-value cache. Most prior evaluations hold parameter count fixed and let compute float, so the reported gains conflate an architectural advantage with uncontrolled extra computation. This paper is, by its own accounting, the first multi-scale study to hold all three budgets fixed at once, per-token FLOPs, total non-embedding parameters, and KV cache, and it still finds looping wins, which makes it the most convincing evidence yet that depth reuse is real.

Mixture-of-experts architectures are what make honest matching feasible. When looping costs extra FLOPs, the recipe narrows the hidden dimension; because MoE decouples total parameters from per-token compute, it then recovers the lost capacity by raising the expert count rather than giving it up; and KV cache parity is restored separately through head size and grouped-query-attention ratio, with every matched pair within four percent on FLOPs, one percent on parameters, and four percent on cache. Three ablations at the two-hundred-million scale then lock the recipe, and each ablation answers a question practitioners actually argue about. Which layers to loop: sweeping the looped span from zero to the full stack, validation loss bottoms out near the middle half of layers at both sparsity levels tested, consistent with a recurring finding across the field that first and last layers serve specialized roles, grounding input and shaping output, that benefit from unique weights. How deep: the looped model's optimum sits at a larger effective depth-to-width ratio than the baseline's, and the authors hypothesize this is because shared layers receive gradient contributions from multiple visits, including later occurrences with shorter paths to the output, which may make extra serial computation easier to optimize than an equally deep stack of independent layers. How many times: two passes beat three and four outright, because under matched FLOPs every extra visit forces a thinner model. The resulting recipe, SMELT, sparse MoE transformer, middle layers loop twice, also scales each looped residual update by one over the loop count to keep weight-tied writes from inflating the residual stream.

The scaling study is the paper's spine: four sizes from one hundred million to one point six billion active parameters, fifty-four billion non-embedding total, crossed with sparsity levels near eighty-five, ninety-five, and ninety-seven percent, thirty-two runs, ninety-six matched pairs, one hundred ninety-two evaluation endpoints, all on identical token sequences, roughly two hundred fifteen billion tokens, no repeats. At every scale and every sparsity, SMELT reaches lower validation loss than its matched baseline. Fitting a separate Chinchilla-style surface to each architecture, the frontier exponent comes out zero point two five zero for SMELT against zero point two three seven for the baseline, and a steeper exponent compounds: at ten to the twenty-first FLOPs of training compute, SMELT reaches the same loss with fourteen point seven to eighteen percent less compute depending on sparsity, and the projected saving widens with budget. The compute-optimal token-per-parameter split barely moves between architectures, so the win comes from lower loss at the same allocation, not from reallocating the budget. Downstream, the effect amplifies rather than washes out: SMELT wins the free-form completion metric in ninety-six of ninety-six matched pairs, DCLM Core in eighty-three of ninety-six, and MMLU in twenty-nine of the thirty pairs above the chance floor, and a residual analysis against a sigmoid calibrated on all ninety-six baseline endpoints shows the benchmark gains exceed what the validation-loss gap alone predicts, with the excess growing monotonically with scale. By domain, the compute saving is largest on code at twenty point four percent, then finance and math, smallest on web text, and the authors read that ranking as tracking internal structure, strict syntax and long-range dependencies rewarding a second pass. Two more signatures: the gain concentrates on long samples, one point five two times larger on the longest quartile than the shortest, where adding parameters or experts shows no such tilt, and it widens with in-context examples, from under one point at zero-shot to nearly two points with demonstrations, with a bracket-matching case study where both models score near zero without examples but SMELT reaches twenty-nine point eight percent at thirty-two shots against twenty-six point four.

The mechanistic section is what elevates this beyond a benchmark paper. Probing the second visit directly: the router reuses a core of its experts across visits, nearly all eight in small pools, two to three out of eight at high sparsity, still far above chance, so the second pass deliberately recomputes with a familiar core and diversifies the rest. Residual writes grow on the second visit, one point two to three and a half times the first visit's norm, and point in a similar direction, so the second pass amplifies the first rather than overwriting it. And the attention analysis is the most striking: queries and keys stay nearly identical across visits, cosine around zero point nine, while values diverge, and the two visits attend to substantially the same tokens, top-eight overlap around fifty-six to sixty-six percent against a twenty-eight to thirty-four percent control. In other words, the model preserves where it looks and changes what it reads. Within that, the attention sink, the pathological concentration of mass on early tokens regardless of content, shrinks on the second visit, reversing the ordinary depth trend where sinks strengthen in later layers, freeing attention mass toward content-relevant tokens, which the authors connect to the in-context-learning advantage.

The skeptical read. First, the ablations that select the recipe ran only at the two-hundred-million scale, and the authors concede the optimal span or count may differ at larger scales; the scaling grid validates the chosen recipe but never re-runs the search at the top. Second, budget matching equates FLOPs, parameters, and cache size, not wall-clock time; looping is serial, so two passes over a block cost more wall-clock latency than one pass over a wider layer, and for latency-bound serving the honest comparison is harder than this paper's. Third, everything is pretraining-side; no finetuning, long-context, or post-training behavior is tested, and the serial-recurrence family, single-layer recurrence run sixteen to thirty-two times, is explicitly treated as out of scope, so conclusions may not generalize to implicit-depth models. Fourth, the compute-saving brackets at the largest budget are wide, from zero to fifty-one percent at the extrapolated end, so the headline should be read as the fitted range with real uncertainty, and the irreducible-loss floors differ by less than the fit noise. Fifth, the training corpus is internal, so domain-breakdown numbers, especially the code result, are corpus-specific until replicated. What I would trust: the three-budget matching protocol, which is the methodological contribution and the thing prior work skipped, the loop-count finding that two passes beat more under matched compute, and the mechanism-level evidence that the second visit is a refinement pass, preserving retrieval coordinates while rewriting values. What I would re-verify: wall-clock comparisons at serving latency targets, and whether the recipe's edge survives the relaxed variants, per-visit adapters or learned halting, which the authors leave open. Bottom line: this is the cleanest answer yet to whether looping is an architectural win or an accounting artifact, and the answer is a real one, worth roughly seven to eighteen percent of training compute at scale, strongest on structured data and in-context learning, with a mechanistic story, refine the read, not the lookup, that finally makes the second pass legible. Depth is becoming a design axis you can spend compute on rather than parameters, and scaling laws for that axis now exist.
