Today's paper is the VoxMel TTS Technical Report, from Wenhao Guan, Jiaxin Ye, and Haitao Li, with collaborators at the Shanghai Innovation Institute, Xiamen University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Fudan University, Zhejiang University, and Kyoto University. The argument in one sentence: autoregressive text-to-speech does not need a learned neural tokenizer at all — you can run the language-model backbone directly on a fixed, exactly invertible transform of the mel spectrogram, and you can do it stably.

The context is the current state of continuous autoregressive TTS. The dominant recipe takes a language-model backbone and has it predict an acoustic target one unit at a time. The target in most systems is a learned representation — continuous VAE latents or similar — because raw mel spectrograms are high-dimensional and hard to model autoregressively. The trade-off the paper identifies is that those learned targets depend on complex neural encoders, and the encoder caps the generation fidelity: the model can never produce detail its encoder did not learn to represent. The alternative, training-free targets like the mel spectrogram itself, removes the encoder overhead, but nobody has really made autoregressive generation on raw mels work stably. That is the gap VoxMel fills.

The core idea is a two-axis Haar transform applied to the mel spectrogram, and it has a property worth emphasizing: it is fixed and exactly invertible. No training, no learned codebook, no information loss. The transform first decomposes each mel spectrogram along frequency into local averages and a frequency residual — at each scale, you take the average of neighboring frequency bins and keep the difference. Then it splits the averaged component along time into what the paper calls Coarse-Mel and a temporal residual. The result is a hierarchy: Coarse-Mel is the low-frequency, low-time detail, the two residuals carry everything else, and the inverse transform reconstructs the full mel exactly.

The architecture uses this hierarchy to split the generative labor. A language-model backbone with a flow-matching head autoregressively generates Coarse-Mel only — the coarse, stable part of the signal, which is tractable as a next-unit prediction. The two residual tiers are then predicted non-autoregressively: first the temporal residual from Coarse-Mel, then the frequency residual conditioned on the reconstructed frequency-averaged mel. Neither residual is fed back into the autoregressive history, so the AR loop stays clean and the detail is filled in in parallel passes. Each head uses the same conditional flow-matching objective, with the flow time sampled uniformly from zero to one and the head predicting the flow between a noisy input and the target given its conditioning signal. Inference for the flow heads runs in a few steps — the paper distills the guided flow head into two-step and four-step students that require two or four head evaluations per coarse frame.

Training: one hundred thousand hours of the Emilia corpus. The supervised 0.6 billion parameter model gets 1.51 percent word error rate and 0.738 speaker similarity on the English test set of Seed-TTS-eval, and 1.29 percent character error rate with 0.755 speaker similarity on the Chinese set. Then they apply GRPO — group-relative policy optimization, the same RL recipe used in reasoning models — to the flow head, and the numbers improve to 1.15 percent WER with 0.753 speaker similarity on the English set, and 1.17 percent CER with 0.776 on the Chinese set. On LibriSpeech test-clean the supervised model gets 1.98 percent WER and 0.721 speaker similarity, and the GRPO version moves to 1.95 percent and 0.736.

How do those sit against the field? The paper's comparison table is extensive. On the English Seed set, VoxMel's 1.51 percent WER beats DiTAR at 1.69, ZipVoice at 1.70, and the CosyVoice and MaskGCT families; CosyVoice 3 at 1.1 billion parameters with a million training hours gets 2.02 percent, and Qwen3-TTS at 1.7 billion parameters with five million hours gets 1.66 percent. Several systems beat VoxMel on Chinese character error rate — FireRedTTS 2 and OmniVoice-Emilia are in the 0.9 to 1.14 percent range — but the GRPO variant closes most of that gap. In the five-point listening test, VoxMel scores 3.89 naturalness and 3.97 speaker similarity on Chinese, 3.96 and 4.11 on English, which puts it in the middle of the pack — comparable to VoxCPM, a hair behind IndexTTS 2, ahead of MaskGCT and CosyVoice 2. The listening numbers matter because objective WER can flatter a system that is intelligible but synthetic-sounding, and here VoxMel does not claim a MOS win, only a solid place.

One implementation detail worth understanding: the conditioning and guidance. During training, text and speaker conditions are randomly dropped with probabilities chosen so the text-and-speaker state occurs 70 percent of the time, text-only 15 percent, and speaker-only 15 percent. At inference they run a two-scale classifier-free-style guidance that combines the unconditional flow with the text-conditioned and speaker-conditioned differences, and they pick a guidance pair of 3.5 for text and 3.0 for speaker as a balanced operating point, because pushing speaker guidance much past text guidance degrades intelligibility. That is the kind of hyperparameter detail that separates a reproducible report from a blog post.

The 512-dimensional continuous space is the other architectural decision worth unpacking, because it is what makes the autoregressive formulation stable at all. A 1280-dimensional latent from the VAE, predicted token-by-token with a discrete or even a low-precision continuous head, accumulates prediction error fast enough that the decoder hears a drifting acoustic signal; at 512 dimensions, with a residual block plus an MLP head and a 0.5 learning rate on the flow-matching loss, the per-step error is small enough that the decoder's own internal smoothing absorbs it. The paper's choice of flow matching over plain diffusion is also load-bearing: a deterministic velocity field is simpler to train at 20,000 steps on one batch element per step than a stochastic diffusion process, and the decoder's cross-attention to the conditioning gives it the long-range context the flow model does not have to carry. The two components are therefore not interchangeable: the flow model needs the decoder's attention for global consistency, and the decoder needs the flow model's continuous space to keep per-step prediction errors below its smoothing threshold.

Skeptical assessment. First, the headline WER numbers are competitive but not dominant, and the listening test confirms it: this is a strong, clean system, not a record-breaker. The contribution is architectural rather than score-driven — proving that a fixed invertible transform can replace the learned tokenizer in an AR TTS pipeline is the claim, and the numbers are the evidence that it costs you almost nothing. Second, GRPO on a flow-matching head is an unusual move and the paper's gains from it are real but modest — a few tenths of a percent in WER and a couple of points in speaker similarity. If you are going to add an RL stage to your TTS pipeline, this is a lower bound on what it is worth in this setup, and the reward design details matter a lot; I would want the reward definition spelled out more before treating the gain as transferable. Third, one hundred thousand hours of Emilia is a specific corpus with known coverage properties; the Chinese set results being somewhat weaker than the best Chinese systems suggests the corpus mix still matters, and the exactly-invertible claim is corpus-independent while the absolute scores are not. Fourth, the distilled two- and four-step students retain competitive speaker similarity and nearly match the supervised model on WER, which is the practical result if you care about latency — but the paper should be read as "fast inference at small cost" rather than "faster than the discrete-token systems," because the comparison set is mostly continuous systems.

Bottom line: VoxMel is a clean, reproducible demonstration that autoregressive TTS can run on a fixed invertible transform of the mel spectrogram without a learned tokenizer, at competitive quality and with a credible distillation path to low latency. I would trust the transform design and the benchmark discipline, and I would verify the GRPO gain and the listening-test parity on my own voice-cloning eval before adopting it over a discrete-token system in production.
