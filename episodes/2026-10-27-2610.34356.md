RynnVLA-Latent: Scaling Robotic Manipulation Foundation Models with 3D-Grounded Latent Actions, by Haoyu Zhao, Xingyue Zhao, and Zhian Su, from the DAMO Academy of Alibaba Group, the Hong Kong Embodied AI Lab, and the Chinese University of Hong Kong, with the corresponding authors also affiliated with the Hupan Lab and Peking Union Medical College. This is a new foundation-model release for robot manipulation, a vision-language-action policy family with released checkpoints, and it is built around a specific data-scaling bet.

The bottleneck the paper targets is the one that has held back vision-language-action scaling for years. You cannot pretrain a robot policy the way you pretrain a language model, because action-labeled demonstrations are scarce, and the action spaces are incompatible across robot embodiments. A gripper arm, a dexterous hand, and a mobile base each emit different control vectors, so a corpus of demonstrations from one embodiment is not directly usable for another. The existing workaround is to collect matched robot data, which is exactly what does not scale. RynnVLA-Latent's answer is to change the supervision signal: instead of learning to predict native robot actions, the policy learns to predict latent actions, continuous 608-dimensional descriptors that a separate model, RynnLAM, learns to extract from raw visual transitions in video. Any video in which something moves can produce supervision, because no robot controls and no proprioception are required, and the latent space is shared across embodiments by construction.

RynnLAM is the first component, and it is where the 3D grounding lives. It is built on Depth-Anything-3, a monocular depth foundation model, and is trained on video frame pairs without any native-action labels. The training signal is purely visual: given a short clip, the model must produce a descriptor of what motion occurred in the scene, and the loss structure is designed so that the descriptor captures the manipulation, not the camera. It has three design elements that are meant to push the representation toward control-relevant motion rather than appearance change. The first is residual future-feature prediction, where the model is trained to predict how its own visual features change, with the residual formulation emphasizing what changed. The second is camera-compensated 3D-flow supervision, which uses explicit camera-pose estimates to separate motion caused by the camera moving from motion caused by objects being manipulated, so the latent action encodes manipulation dynamics rather than viewpoint changes. The third is camera-motion decoupling, which further encourages the representation to be invariant to the observer's own movement. The paper is careful to state what this buys and what it does not: the objectives encourage camera decoupling, but they do not guarantee exact viewpoint invariance or physical identifiability of the latent actions, which is the honest limitation of learning an action space from pixels alone. A useful way to think about the trade-off: a latent action is a compressed description of a physical transition, and like any compression it will be lossy in ways the training distribution did not expose. The 608-dimensional descriptor is wide enough to carry substantial structure, but the paper's own ablations show that the representation is only as good as the end-to-end training, which is the point of the next result I will flag.

The data corpus behind the pretraining is the scale claim. RynnLAM is applied to 30,100 hours of human, robot, and simulation video from 25 different sources, producing 745.52 million latent-action descriptors. That is the asset the rest of the pipeline consumes. RynnVLA-Latent itself is trained in two phases. In pretraining, the policy, a vision-language model paired with a separate action expert, is trained with flow matching to predict chunks of six latent-action targets, conditioned on multi-view observations and a language instruction, with proprioception excluded. Multi-view conditioning is handled through canonical camera slots: each present view's patch embeddings are pooled into a slot token that is prepended to the action expert, and empty slots are masked in attention, so the same model handles embodiments with different camera rigs without hard-coding a fixed view set. The optimization is AdamW with beta one at 0.9 and beta two at 0.95, no weight decay, gradient clipping at 1.0, and the data sources are sampled with probability proportional to the configured source weight raised to the 0.7 power. In the second phase, the pretrained backbone and action expert are adapted to embodiment-specific native robot actions, which is where the latent space gets grounded in a particular body's controls. The released family has two sizes: a 2-billion-parameter configuration with a 28-layer vision-language model at 2,048 hidden size plus a 380-million-parameter action expert, and a 4-billion configuration with a 36-layer model at 2,560 hidden size plus a 600-million-parameter action expert, for a total of about 5 billion parameters.

The results are the strongest in the paper, and the headline set is broad. The 4-billion policy reaches 98.8 percent success on LIBERO, 84.7 percent on LIBERO-Plus without any benchmark-specific adaptation, which is the robustness split, 51.9 percent average success across five tracks of VLABench, and 88.6 percent average on the RoboTwin 2.0 bimanual benchmark. The fact that both released sizes, 2B and 4B, are available matters for adoption: a team with a smaller inference budget can evaluate the 2-billion checkpoint against its own tasks before committing to the larger model, and the paper's scaling results are what tell you how much of the 4B advantage you are leaving on the table. On six real-world manipulation tasks across dexterous-hand and mobile-gripper platforms, it achieves the highest observed success rates among the compared methods after embodiment-specific post-training. The representation ablations support the design: on the LARY low-level control regression benchmark, where the probe task is to recover control signals from the latent representation, the full 608-dimensional descriptor beats general-purpose encoders like DINOv3 and V-JEPA 2 by a wide margin, and training RynnLAM with a frozen encoder degrades the relative average mean squared error from 0.38 to 0.52, which is the paper's evidence that end-to-end training of the latent-action model, not just the architecture, is doing work. The data-scaling results show continued gains as the corpus grows, which is the claim the whole framework rests on.

The skeptical read. The result to take seriously is the LIBERO-Plus number, 84.7 percent without benchmark-specific adaptation, because robustness under perturbation is where latent-representation approaches either earn their keep or fall over, and the data-scaling curve is the piece that separates a one-off result from a scaling law. What I would re-verify before deploying: the latent actions are learned from pixels, and the paper itself concedes there is no guarantee of physical identifiability, so the failure mode to look for in your own embodiment is a latent direction that is stable in training video but wrong on your hardware, which embodiment-specific post-training is meant to fix but may only partially do. The 51.9 percent on VLABench is the weakest number in the table, and if your deployment involves the kind of semantic grounding VLABench tests, that is where the margin to baselines is thinnest and the one to reproduce first. The 25-source corpus mixes human, robot, and simulation video, and the source-weighting exponent of 0.7 is a hyperparameter the paper reports but does not ablate deeply, so the corpus composition is a variable you should expect to matter more than the architecture when you transfer this to your own data. The bottom line: this is a credible new foundation-model release for robot manipulation, and its core claim, that latent actions learned from 30,000 hours of unlabeled video can substitute for scarce action-labeled demonstrations, is backed by the strongest simulation results in the current VLA literature and a real-robot comparison where it leads. If you are scaling robot policy learning, the RynnLAM latent-action stage is the component to study, and the LIBERO-Plus robustness number is the one to hold the released checkpoint to.
