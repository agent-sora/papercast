The paper is called "P2-MOPD," which stands for Parameter-Prior Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation. The first three authors are Sinuo Wang, Jiacheng Lu, and Zeyu Zhang. The work is a collaboration between the Australian Institute for Machine Learning at the University of Adelaide and StepFun in Shanghai, with co-authors from Shanghai Jiao Tong University, the Singapore University of Technology and Design, and the Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence in Abu Dhabi.

The problem is the standard consolidation step in frontier model development. You train a set of domain experts, often with domain-specific reinforcement learning, and then you need to fold them into a single generalist that keeps the capability of every expert instead of trading one domain for another. Two recipes dominate. The first is multi-teacher on-policy distillation, or MOPD, where each teacher supervises the student's own generated trajectories with teacher forcing, producing token-level labels. The second is model merging, specifically task arithmetic, where you add a weighted sum of the experts' task vectors to the base model with no training at all.

Both have a known weakness, and the paper motivates its method by naming both precisely. MOPD is heavy. Every training step samples rollouts from the student and runs each teacher over those rollouts to get labels, and the student has to climb toward each teacher's performance over many such steps. Prior work also shows MOPD does not recover the domains evenly; even when it starts from a merge of the experts, it can leave one domain far behind, and continued distillation can erode a capability the merge already had. Task arithmetic is training-free and cheap, but the merged model's behavior is never supervised, so its quality is not guaranteed. In their experiments, a uniform merge recovers less than half of the gap between the base model and the teachers.

The key observation is that task arithmetic's partial success is informative. If a training-free linear combination of the experts already recovers some of their capabilities, then the expert parameters encode a useful prior for the consolidated model. The paper takes that one step further. A linear combination of the base student and the frozen experts is itself a single differentiable model, differentiable in both the mixing coefficients and the student's own weights, so it can be trained end to end under the MOPD objective. Viewed this way, the experts stop being just an initialization or a one-off merge. They act as parameter priors throughout the whole training. The student does not have to climb the entire way to each teacher on its own.

There is a subtle but important distinction here that is easy to miss. In standard MOPD, the experts influence the student only through their logits, the token-level targets they produce when run over the student's rollouts. In task arithmetic, the experts influence the student only through their parameters, but as a fixed, training-free combination. P2-MOPD couples the two: the experts provide logit supervision as before, and simultaneously their parameters sit inside the student's effective weights, where they are adjusted by the same gradient signal that updates the student. So the mixing coefficients are learned, not hand-tuned. The model is deciding, under the distillation objective, how much of each expert's parameter space to lean on as it trains, rather than a human picking a merge weight and hoping. That learned-weights property is what lets the student escape the frozen span, and it is the whole point of the method.

The method, P2-MOPD, keeps the MOPD loss and its supervision on the student's own trajectories, but it parameterizes the student as a linear combination of its own trainable weights and the frozen expert parameters. So the student receives two kinds of guidance from the experts under one loss. Their logits supervise the student's outputs, exactly as in MOPD. And their parameters enter the student's weights through the mixing coefficients, which the on-policy loss updates together with the student weights. The decisive difference from task arithmetic is that the base student's weights stay fully trainable. In task arithmetic the consolidated model is confined to the linear span of the frozen student and expert parameters, no matter what merging coefficients you choose. P2-MOPD breaks out of that span, so the experts act as a prior without caging the model inside it, and the student can grow beyond what the experts alone contain.

The experiments run across four domains at two scales, a 1.7 billion parameter model and a 4 billion parameter model, both Qwen3. They report average recovery, that is, how much of the gap between the base model and the teachers the consolidated model recovers. At 1.7 billion, P2-MOPD reaches 94.7 percent average recovery at the end of training, versus 80.9 percent for MOPD starting from the same task-arithmetic initialization. At 4 billion the pattern repeats, with P2-MOPD reaching 90.5 percent average recovery against 80.9 percent for the MOPD baseline, and they emphasize that P2-MOPD surpasses MOPD's final average recovery using half the training steps. So the claim is both faster and more balanced. The results are means over three seeds with standard deviations reported. The code is released publicly.

One practical consequence of the trainable-weights design is that the method does not require the experts to be a clean, non-overlapping partition of capability. Because the student can move outside the span of the expert parameters, overlapping or redundant experts do not force a degenerate merge. This is the regime where plain task arithmetic tends to struggle most, when the task vectors interfere with each other, and it is where the prior framing is supposed to help the most. The authors' motivation from prior work is that MOPD already recovers domains unevenly, and the parameter-prior mechanism is their proposed fix for exactly that imbalance, which is why "more balanced recovery" is a core claim alongside the raw recovery number.

Now the skeptical read. The setup is a common one, and the method is a clean reframing, so I would take the core idea seriously. But there are a few things to weigh. The gains are measured against MOPD starting from the same task-arithmetic merge, which is a reasonable and honest baseline, but it is also a baseline that the authors tuned; the comparison is within their own pipeline and their own recovery metric, so the 94.7 versus 80.9 gap is their measurement of their method. The four domains and two scales are a meaningful but modest sweep, and "average recovery" can mask which domains are still lagging, which is exactly the imbalance problem the paper says MOPD suffers from; you would want the per-domain table to confirm the recovery is even, not just high on average. The mechanism, jointly optimizing mixing coefficients and student weights under one objective, adds a layer of optimization that could be sensitive to initialization and to how the experts were originally trained, and the paper does not stress-test how much of the gain comes from the trainable-weights freedom versus simply having a better starting point. What I would trust is the conceptual move, that the frozen expert parameters are a usable prior that can be trained alongside the student rather than just merged in, and that this beats a fixed-span merge. What I would re-verify before relying on it is the per-domain recovery balance, the sensitivity to expert quality, and whether the half-the-steps speedup holds when you match compute fairly rather than steps.

The bottom line: P2-MOPD is a clean and well-motivated extension of on-policy distillation that treats frozen expert parameters as trainable priors, and it reports meaningfully higher and faster capability recovery than the standard baseline. The idea is the durable contribution; the specific recovery numbers are solid but measured within the authors' own four-domain, two-scale setup, so they should be read as a strong demonstration rather than a settled measurement of the technique's ceiling.
