The paper is Beyond Compilation, from Ke Zhang, Patricio Gallardo Candela, and Sudhir Murthy, at UC Riverside, with collaborators at the University of Arizona and UC San Diego. It is a measurement paper about a gap that most people in the formal-math-with-LLMs space quietly accept but nobody had quantified: that compiling a generated Lean declaration is a far weaker signal of correctness than the field pretends. In theorem proving, the target statement is fixed, so if your proof compiles in Lean, that is a strong success signal. But in statement autoformalization, where the model writes the theorem itself, Lean can only certify that the declaration you wrote is well typed. It cannot check whether it is the theorem the user meant. The whole paper is about measuring how large that gap is, and how much of it is a real translation error versus a judge making a mistake.

The setup is clean. They build a benchmark of two hundred and twenty seven university-level statements, pulled from four open-source LaTeX textbooks in real analysis, complex analysis, topology, and abstract algebra. The key property is that the sources contain no formal code, so there is no canonical Lean target. A single statement can have several faithful encodings, which is exactly the situation that breaks the standard evaluation tools. Exact match is off the table, and reference-based scoring needs a reference you do not have. So they need a reference-free criterion. Theirs is strict: an output is accepted only if it compiles in Lean with Mathlib, and two large-model judges from different families, GPT-5.2 and Gemini-2.5-Pro, both grade it at least nine out of ten for faithfulness against the natural-language source. The gap for a system is then its compile rate minus its acceptance rate, which counts every output that compiles but one or both judges reject.

The first question they answer is how well this criterion tracks human judgment, and on which side it errs. On an independent random audit they agree with the human majority on ninety-one and a half percent of cases, with a confidence interval from eighty-one and a half to ninety-six and a half. The asymmetry is the important finding. Humans confirm ninety-five and a seven percent of the automatic positives, but only about seventy-seven to eighty percent of the automatic negatives. In other words, the criterion rarely accepts an unfaithful output, but it does reject some faithful ones. They walk through a concrete case: an output stating that every contractible space is connected and has trivial homotopy groups, expressed through Mathlib's ContractibleSpace and HomotopyGroup. Every human reviewer accepts it, but Gemini grades it a five. The point is that the conservative operating point is safe in the direction that matters for a benchmark, it does not pass garbage, but it inflates the measured gap by rejecting correct work.

The second question is how big the gap is across system families. They run the same criterion on one-shot general large models, specialized formalizers, prover-oriented Lean models, and a tool-augmented GPT-5.2 agent that has drafting, search, and a Lean REPL feedback tool. Every system has a gap, ranging from one and a three percentage points for one-shot GPT-5.2 to as high as twenty-one points for the prover-oriented models, and twenty-nine and a five points for their own agent. The agent is the striking one because it is the best at compiling: it compiles eighty-seven and two percent of the statements, but only fifty-seven and a seven percent pass the semantic criterion. That means roughly thirty out of every hundred declarations it produces compile cleanly and still do not state the intended theorem. Human review of all the agent's rejections confirms that twenty-three and a eight of the twenty-nine and a five points are real translation errors, and they show two concrete examples. One drops the invertibility assumption that makes a matrix group a group, relying on Mathlib's convention that the inverse of a singular matrix is zero, so the set containing only zero satisfies every hypothesis without containing the identity. The other rewrites a loop condition into something that is trivially satisfied. These are exactly the failures that compilation can never catch.

There is a sharp twist in the third question, which is how much of each gap is translation errors versus judge errors versus defective statements. For seven of the eight translation systems, a two-stage review finds that seventy-eight to one hundred percent of the gap is real translation errors. But for two models released after GPT-5.2, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5, the story flips. When GPT-5.2, one of the two judges, grades their translations, it accepts eighty-three percent of the statements for each, against only twenty-one percent for one-shot GPT-5.2. The same review classifies only four of their fifty-four rejections as actual translation errors. So for these stronger models, most of the measured gap is the judge getting it wrong, not the model. They frame it as a moving relationship: as the system makes fewer real errors, a growing share of its rejections are the judge's error rather than the model's. That is an important caution for anyone building an autoformalization leaderboard, because the very metric you use to rank systems quietly changes what it is measuring as the systems get better.

The tool ablation of the agent is the final piece. A twenty-three-configuration factorial design shows that Lean REPL feedback drives most of the agent's compilation gain, ahead of search and translation tools. But nearly half of that compilation gain fails the semantic criterion, which is the paper's punchline stated plainly: compiler-guided repair makes the code valid, and valid Lean that states the wrong theorem is precisely the thing you cannot tell apart from valid Lean that states the right theorem.

There is a methodological detail that underpins the whole gap decomposition, and it is where the paper is strongest. They do not just assert that the two-stage review works; they validate it on the subset of rejections that humans actually labeled. On sixty-nine compiling outputs that GPT-5.2 rejects and that a human majority scored, the two-stage review, Claude Opus 5.5 agents with access to the Lean REPL followed by a GPT-6.1 Sol check, classifies about eighty-two and a half percent of the truly unfaithful ones as translation errors and leaves two-thirds of the truly faithful ones unflagged. They report the confidence intervals, they show that where two human reviewers agree the review matches them as often or more often than a third human does, and they release every prompt, every review, every discussion round, and every Lean check. That level of auditability is rare in this literature, and it is what lets them make the strong claim that the gap is mostly real for current models while also conceding that for the two newest models it is mostly the judge's error. The honesty of that flip, reporting that their own criterion gets the newest models wrong, is the most defensible thing in the paper.

I would push back on a couple of points. The whole measurement rests on two large-model judges from the same generation, and even with a second family and a third-family robustness check, the errors of modern large models are correlated, so the faithfulness grade is only as good as those judges. And the two-stage review used to decompose the gap is itself a large model with Lean access, so you are using one kind of system to measure the errors of another. That is the best available method, and they validate it against human labels, but it is still a model in the loop. This is one of the most useful measurement papers in the LLM-for-mathematics space right now, because it tells the field that compilation, the number everyone reports, overstates faithfulness by as much as twenty-nine points, and that the gap is mostly real for current models but increasingly a judge artifact as the models improve.
