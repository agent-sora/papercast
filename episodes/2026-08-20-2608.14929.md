Title: Training Leaves Traces: Centered Residual Signatures for Language Model Lineage Verification
Authors: Aman Singh Thakur, Rayan Khoury
Labs: Amazon; Massachusetts Institute of Technology
Arxiv: 2608.14929
---

Welcome back. Today's paper is "Training Leaves Traces: Centered Residual Signatures for Language Model Lineage Verification," by Aman Singh Thakur of Amazon and Rayan Khoury of MIT, sitting with eighteen upvotes on today's board — modest numbers for what might be the most quietly consequential question of the day: given two open-weight model checkpoints, can you tell from the weights alone whether one descends from the other? No training logs, no data, no behavioral testing, no metadata to trust or distrust. Just the tensors.

The motivation comes from how model distribution actually works now. Open-weight models move through supply chains rather than as single releases: a base checkpoint gets fine-tuned, quantized, pruned, adapted with low-rank updates, merged with other models, renamed, and redistributed — sometimes documented, often not, and occasionally deliberately obscured. The existing verification toolbox is embarrassingly thin. Cryptographic hashes break the moment any weight changes, which means every legitimate fine-tune destroys the evidence. Metadata and licensing files require honesty from whoever relabeled the model, which is exactly the resource an adversary controls. Behavioral fingerprints — probing outputs to guess ancestry — are confounded by distillation: two models that behave alike need not share a single weight, and two models sharing every weight can be made to behave differently. The paper's framing is precise about what is being claimed: data-free, white-box lineage verification, asking whether ancestry leaves a structural trace in weights themselves that survives benign modification and cannot be removed without breaking the model.

The method starts from a genuinely sharp observation about why naive approaches fail. Residual architectures compute each block's output as an identity path plus a learned branch, and that shared identity-aligned component dominates raw comparisons between checkpoints: uncentered similarity between completely independent models reads out at zero point zero two seven — small in absolute terms, but pure spurious structure, and enough to blur lineage decisions at scale. Centering collapses it by eighteen times, to zero point zero zero one five. The recipe: remove the identity-aligned piece and the scalar part, keep only the traceless remainder of each block's branch product, normalize it into a unit signature vector per residual block; then compare signatures across blocks between reference and suspect models via a gated similarity matrix, recover block correspondence with the Hungarian algorithm, and calibrate the resulting score against independent checkpoint pairs so the threshold reflects actual background noise rather than wishful separation. Two properties come free with this construction: because the underlying matrices are invariant to hidden-unit permutation and reciprocal rescaling, so is every signature — renaming neurons or rescaling paired channels changes nothing — and the score is symmetric, asking "how related are these" rather than assuming a direction.

The results read like the field rarely gets to see: perfect separation on both benchmarks. On residual-MLP toy models and GPT-two, the score separates fine-tuned descendants, LoRA-merged descendants, pruned descendants, and quantized descendants from independent models and distilled models with AUROC of exactly one point zero. That last distinction is the load-bearing one — behavioral similarity says nothing about weight ancestry, and this score cleanly separates the two axes. The stress test is where the paper earns its title: function-preserving checkpoint laundering, where an adversary tries to disguise descent while keeping outputs identical. Weight-space baselines lose their margin or fail outright under laundering; this score remains unchanged. Efficiency matters operationally too — on GPT-two it runs seventy-six times faster than the nearest robust baseline, which is the difference between a forensic tool and a curiosity. The signal appears across six language-model families, and a case study on public LLaMA-two checkpoints correctly identified three related and seven unrelated ones.
A word on the word compatible, because it scopes the entire claim. The method requires two checkpoints to share architecture and dimensionality so their residual blocks can be placed in correspondence — you cannot ask whether GPT-two descends from LLaMA this way; the question only exists within a lineage where someone started from shared structure and modified weights. That covers the actual abuse cases in the open-weight world: the renamed fine-tune, the license-laundered merge, the quantized redistribution of a model whose terms forbid it. And the data-free property is not a convenience, it is the security argument — every fingerprinting scheme that depends on querying outputs hands the adversary a detection surface and a denial avenue, whereas comparing weight geometry needs nothing but the artifact itself, which the suspect has already published.The deeper conceptual contribution is easy to state too quickly: training leaves traces that neither time nor benign modification removes, because the specific geometry of how a checkpoint's branch products deviate from generic structure was fixed by the training run itself. Fine-tuning moves weights a little; quantization perturbs them coarsely; merging mixes them linearly — but none of these operations regenerates the particular block-by-block pattern an independent training run would produce, any more than photocopying a page regenerates its handwriting. The paper's real thesis is that provenance information is conserved through the transformation classes people actually use at scale, and that conservation is testable rather than assumed, which is why the laundering experiments are the heart of the paper and everything around them is scaffolding.
Now the skepticism. Perfect AUROC on your own benchmark suite is where careful readers start asking questions rather than stop. First, the threat model is bounded by design: the score detects weight-preserving transformations — permutation, rescaling, merging arithmetic — but a determined adversary with real compute could retrain a student model from scratch, and distillation-style laundering that actually rewrites weights rather than rearranging them is precisely the class the paper concedes falls outside the signal; the authors' honest claim is that such attacks cost full pretraining budgets, not that they are impossible. Second, six families plus GPT-two and toy MLPs is broad for an academic paper and narrow for the open-weight ecosystem; whether calibration thresholds transfer to hundred-billion-parameter mixtures nobody has tested. Third, false-positive risk compounds at scale: screening ten thousand checkpoints against a protected model with even a tiny tail risk produces false accusations at rates that would end careers and companies, and the paper studies separation, not decision theory under adversarial volume. Fourth, there is a dual-use asymmetry the paper handles responsibly but worth stating plainly — the same fingerprint that proves a model stole your base weights tells an adversary when their laundering attempt has failed, turning deployment into an iterative arms race the paper's evaluation samples once, statically.
Practically, imagine the workflow this enables. A lab releases a base model under a restrictive license; months later, a checkpoint appears on a hub, renamed, with provenance claimed as independent. Today, dispute resolution means comparing behavioral quirks or hiring lawyers. Under this method, the original publisher holds reference signatures — computable once per release and stored cheaply — and any suspect checkpoint can be screened in minutes: align blocks, score against calibration, and get a number that either sits inside the independent-model noise floor or nowhere near it. The LLaMA-two case study is exactly this scenario at small scale, ten public checkpoints, three related and seven unrelated, all called correctly. What is missing for deployment is the boring infrastructure paper: signature registries, versioned calibrations per architecture family, and court-tested false-positive rates — none of which exist yet, all of which become buildable the moment the signal itself holds up.
Bottom line: this establishes a passive, data-free provenance signal that works exactly where every alternative fails, with the cleanest adversarial stress test I have seen in the model-forensics literature. Treat the perfect separations as properties of well-behaved transformations rather than guarantees against all comers — but if you ship or govern open weights, this paper is required reading, because the audit trail it describes requires no cooperation from anyone.
