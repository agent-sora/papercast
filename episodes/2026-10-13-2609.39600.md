The paper is "GroundAnything: Reconciling Parallel Decoding with Precise Visual Grounding at Flash Speed," by Qize Yu, Lianrui Fan, and Bowen Ping, from XPeng Inc., Peking University, and the University of Hong Kong, with the same Berkeley, Princeton, NUS, and HKUST collaborators as the sibling GroundingPI paper — and it is, in effect, the decoding half of the same research program. It has 16 Hugging Face upvotes, sixth on the day. If GroundingPI argued that perception deserves its own foundation model, GroundAnything argues that the autoregressive decoding inherited from language models is the wrong shape for spatial prediction.

The core claim is a reframing. Autoregressive grounding models serialize spatial predictions: labels and coordinates are emitted one token at a time, which introduces sequential latency and imposes a causal order on output tokens that does not reflect the underlying problem. The paper's observation is that you may notice objects in an image in any order and reach the same judgment about what is present and where. Objects, locations, and spatial relations are jointly constrained by the image and the query, but their dependencies do not imply an intrinsic left-to-right generation order. Grounding is therefore cast as structured visual evidence extraction, and bidirectional masked diffusion is proposed as the matching mechanism: spatial hypotheses emerge in parallel and are jointly refined through iterative denoising, using shared visual evidence and partially recovered structure. The framing has a practical consequence beyond speed: under a fixed latency budget, a denoising schedule has a bounded and predictable cost, whereas an AR sequence has variable length, so the diffusion mode gives a system designer a handle that the AR mode does not.

The model is a 4B-parameter grounding foundation model with two variants from a shared pretrained checkpoint. GroundAnything-VLM is the autoregressive version, and GroundAnything is the diffusion version, obtained by direct AR-to-diffusion conversion. The architecture is a MoonViT-V2 visual encoder — the Kimi K3 encoder — through a projector with 2-by-2 spatial aggregation and a two-layer MLP, feeding a Qwen3-4B language decoder. The output interface is a shared vocabulary of semantic labels, protocol markers, and 1,000 quantized coordinate tokens from 0 to 999, with a None payload for absent categories. The conversion introduces a mask token and jointly trains causal next-token prediction and blockwise masked denoising with a block size of 32; both modes share the decoder and the vocabulary head. That shared head is what enables the self-speculative decoding mode later: the model can draft with diffusion and verify with the autoregressive path. The diffusion objective factorizes over consecutive response blocks: bidirectional attention within each block for parallel recovery, completed blocks forming a causal prefix, with semantic ordering — including trajectory time order — preserved.

Training follows the staged recipe of the sibling paper: grounding pretraining on public datasets plus dedicated data engines, then supervised fine-tuning, then GRPO-based reinforcement post-training with causal GRPO for the diffusion variant and task-specific rewards for boxes, OCR, and pointing. The AR-to-diffusion conversion step is the novel piece: rather than pretraining a diffusion model from scratch, they take a checkpoint that already knows the coordinate vocabulary and the task distribution, add the mask token, and train both objectives jointly so the model keeps its autoregressive competence while learning blockwise denoising. The paper is 61 pages, and the operational details that matter for replication are the block size of 32, the joint AR-plus-diffusion objective with a shared head, and the fact that the conversion starts from the fully pretrained VLM rather than a diffusion pretrain — that last point is what makes this a conversion recipe you could apply to your own AR grounding model instead of a one-off artifact.

The numbers, across 30 grounding benchmarks. GroundAnything-VLM establishes a new overall state of the art among similarly sized models at 72.42 percent average, which the paper notes remains competitive with GPT-6 Astra at 71.35 percent. The diffusion variant, evaluated in entropy-guided decoding mode so the main tables reflect pure diffusion without autoregressive verification, averages 61.75 percent — which the paper frames as the first diffusion language model to surpass the prior overall autoregressive state of the art at this scale — against 53.32 percent for the fast MTP-based LocateAnything model, at more than 2 times the speed. The speed story has three layers. First, decoding strategy: entropy-guided decoding adapts how many denoising steps each position gets based on the model's uncertainty. Second, an optional self-speculative mode that combines diffusion drafting with autoregressive verification, which improves both speed and accuracy over the entropy-guided point and achieves a 4.51 times speedup over the AR counterpart with only a 0.74 percentage-point drop in F1 at mean IoU on COCO. Third, infrastructure: progressive optimizations with SGLang, CUDA graphs, and FP8 that translate the algorithmic parallelism into measured wall-clock speedups, which is the part that matters if you intend to serve this in a latency-sensitive system.

The ablation tables in the appendix are worth skimming even if you skip the rest. The architecture sweep compares coordinate representations, visual encoders, and the Stage III training configuration under a controlled protocol, and the headline result is that the full recipe — quantized coordinates, the Kimi encoder, and the full three-stage training — is what produces the 72.42, with each individual swap costing a measurable slice. The speed section asks four explicit questions about the speed-accuracy trade-off, and the block-size sweep on COCO shows tokens-per-forward and throughput rising with block size from 4 to 32, with the best throughput in the 8-to-16 range. The GRPO stage samples 8 responses per prompt at temperature 0.7 with a two-pass likelihood computation, and the diffusion variant improves over its supervised starting point, so the RL stage is doing work rather than merely reshaping the supervised output. For the speculative mode, the draft-acceptance analysis at candidate length 16 is the number to look at if you plan to serve this: the one-step diffusion drafter accepts 3.48 draft tokens per round against 2.69 for causal MTP at the same candidate length, which is where the wall-clock advantage actually comes from.

The skeptical read. First, the diffusion variant's 61.75 average sits about 11 points below its own AR twin's 72.42, and while the paper sells the speed-accuracy trade, anyone choosing between the two variants is choosing between the best accuracy at AR latency and faster inference at a real accuracy cost — the entropy-guided mode is pure diffusion by the paper's own description, and the speculative mode that recovers accuracy reintroduces the AR path. Second, the "first diffusion LM to surpass prior AR state of the art at this scale" claim is a specific comparison against LocateAnything's fast MTP mode, not against AR models at their own best accuracy, so the claim is narrower than the framing. Third, the benchmark battery is the team's own 30-benchmark suite shared with GroundingPI, and the GPT-6 Astra comparison is, as with its sibling, a proprietary general-purpose model on a battery designed around the authors' strengths. And the 4B Qwen3 decoder ceiling applies here too: this is a perception and localization model, and its semantic range is that of a small language model. If you adopt the recipe, the piece to validate is the AR-to-diffusion conversion on your own checkpoint — whether joint training preserves AR quality while denoising learns — because everything else in the pipeline is standard.

Bottom line: a well-executed demonstration that masked diffusion over a shared quantized-coordinate vocabulary can match or beat fast parallel-AR grounding at much better latency profiles, with the conversion-from-AR-checkpoint design being the transferable contribution. The 11-point gap between the diffusion and AR variants is the number to keep in mind: you are buying speed, the paper is honest about the price, and for the latency-critical deployment target this paper explicitly names, that trade is a reasonable one to make for the right workload.
