TailSteer, Trajectory-Tail Steering for Drift-Resistant Multi-turn Image Editing, is from Ruikang Li, Zixuan Chen, and Jiarui Wu, working at the MMLab at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, SenseTime Research, and the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory.

The problem is one that any team that has shipped an iterative image editor already knows from experience. A model can do a great single edit, but the moment you start chaining edits, where each output becomes the next input, the small unintended changes stop being small. Fine textures wash out, non-target objects and facial details drift, background structures distort, and after enough turns the image is not the thing it started as. Strong single-turn editing performance does not predict robust long-horizon editing. The paper's central claim is that this drift is fixable with a final-stage training procedure that does not relearn the model's existing capabilities, and the procedure is deliberately lightweight.

The three existing families of approaches each have a real limitation, and the paper walks through them to justify its design. The first adds auxiliary modules or constraints to a pretrained model, spatial guidance masks, specially trained LoRA adapters, or frequency corrections. These improve preservation, but they do it by constraining or sacrificing part of the large-scale generative prior, so they are limited to a narrow range of localized edit types. The second refines the representation space, working on the VAE latent to get a more suitable editing space, or bypassing the VAE for pixel diffusion. These help within a single turn but still degrade across repeated edits. The third keeps explicit memory across edits, caching latent states or previous visual context, or using reasoning models for agentic editing. These can work, but they are inference-time mechanisms that are hard to scale to foundation models.

TailSteer follows a simple principle, and the principle is the whole paper. Keep the early denoising dynamics close to those of the base model, and concentrate the preservation corrections near the trajectory tail, where the fine-grained deviations actually accumulate. In a diffusion editing process, the early steps of the denoising trajectory are where the global structure and the major content decisions are made, and that is exactly where you want the base model's learned priors to run unmodified. The drift, the loss of fine detail, the small unintended changes to things you did not ask to change, shows up in the later steps. So TailSteer learns to apply preservation corrections only in that tail region, leaving the bulk of the denoising trajectory as the base model already runs it.

The training signal is the elegant part. The corrections are learned from the editor's own on-policy denoising rollouts, using the unchanged source content as the preservation reference. That means you do not need curated ground-truth editing pairs, which is the expensive and brittle part of most editing training data. You take the source image, run the model's own editing rollouts, and the unchanged source tells the model what was supposed to stay put. The model learns to suppress the deviations between its own output and the source in the content regions that should not have moved. This is a self-supervised loop, and it is why the procedure can be applied on top of a strong base model without a large new annotated dataset.

The experiments run across three model families, FLUX, SenseNova, and Qwen, which is the point that this is a transferable final-stage procedure rather than a model-specific trick. The base model used in the main visual results is the FLUX.2 base 9B. Across fifteen to twenty editing turns, TailSteer substantially reduces the cumulative drift while largely preserving the base model's performance on extensive text-to-image and single-image editing benchmarks. That second clause is the one that matters for adoption: the whole risk of a final-stage training procedure is that it degrades the single-turn quality you started with, and the paper's claim is that it does not, that the text-to-image and single-edit scores stay close to the base model across all four backbones and the reported steering onsets.

On the quantitative side, the single-turn capability retention is evaluated on Qwen-Image-Bench and BizGenEval for text-to-image, and on GEdit-Bench, RISEBench, and ImgEdit-Bench for single-image editing. The finding is that text-to-image performance stays close to the base model, as do the GEdit and ImgEdit scores, while the competing inference-time preservation modules, FreqEdit and Consistency LoRA, show substantially larger declines in those same single-turn metrics. That is the direct cost comparison: the modules buy you some preservation but they measurably tax the base capability, whereas TailSteer, by steering only the tail, avoids that tax.

The qualitative comparisons are where the long-horizon claim gets its evidence. Against training-free methods like MTC, video-data-trained VIN-CIE, and the inference-time preservation modules, plus a strong proprietary generator, GPT-Image-2.5, accessed directly via API, TailSteer preserves the source-specific details that the others lose. MTC keeps scene coherence but drops the source-specific detail. VIN-CIE degrades severely across turns. The preservation modules ease some drift but alter the base model's behavior and degrade single-turn editing. In the headline example, TailSteer preserves the tabletop color and previously established details, the coffee cup, the latte art, the cookie, even after twenty turns, and the paper notes that it preserves the tabletop color more faithfully than the proprietary generator in that specific example, which is the claim that an open-weight model after TailSteer can rival or surpass a strong proprietary generator on long-horizon tasks.

A skeptical read has a few things I would verify. The single-turn retention is the number I would hold the paper to, because the entire adoption argument rests on not degrading what you already had, and I would want to see the retention across a broader edit distribution, not just the reported benchmarks, because the tail-steering correction is applied to content that should stay put and the risk is over-correction, where the model starts resisting edits it should have made. The self-supervised training signal is elegant but it is defined by the model's own rollouts, so if the base model has a systematic bias in which regions it drifts, the preservation reference is teaching it to hold exactly those regions, which is fine for preservation but could in principle make the model worse at legitimate changes to those same regions. The cross-family result is encouraging but the families differ in their denoising schedules and latent structures, so the steering onset, where along the trajectory the correction kicks in, is a per-model hyperparameter that the paper reports tuning, and that is a reproduction cost that is easy to underestimate.

One more point on the training loop that matters for reproduction. Because the preservation reference is the unchanged source, the method is effectively teaching the model a notion of which parts of the image are invariant under the edit it is performing, and that notion is learned from the model's own behavior. That is elegant, but it also means the correction is only as good as the model's ability to distinguish the regions it actually moved from the regions it accidentally drifted. If the base model has a habit of slightly redrawing a region it was not supposed to touch, the self-supervised signal treats that redraw as exactly the kind of deviation to suppress, which is the desired behavior, but it also means the method inherits and entrenches whatever the base model's drift pattern is. The paper's retention results argue that the entrenchment does not generalize to over-suppressing legitimate edits, which is the failure I would test hardest, but the retention benchmarks are single-turn, and the over-correction risk is a multi-turn phenomenon that the single-turn retention does not directly measure.

The engineering gotcha is the steering onset. The whole method is a function of where you start applying the tail correction, and get that onset wrong in either direction and you either leave the drift uncorrected or you start interfering with the structural denoising steps that should run on the base prior. It is a single scalar knob per model, but it is the knob that determines whether the method works at all, and it is the first thing to sweep if you are porting this to a new backbone.

Bottom line: TailSteer is a clean, cheap final-stage training procedure that attacks multi-turn drift by steering only the denoising trajectory tail, learned self-supervisedly from the model's own rollouts against the unchanged source, with the strong claim that it does not tax the base model's single-turn capability. The main caution is that the preservation signal is defined by the model's own drift, so validate against over-correction on legitimate edits and treat the per-model steering onset as a required sweep, not a free lunch.
