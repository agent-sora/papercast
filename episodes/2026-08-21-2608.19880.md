EnvHarness: Awakening Static Worlds for Agent Learning, by Chengsong Huang, Zifeng Wang, and Rujun Han, with fourteen more authors including Burak Gokturk, Tomas Pfister, and Chen-Yu Lee. Huang did this work while interning at Google Cloud AI Research, and the affiliation list covers Washington University in St. Louis, Google Cloud AI Research, Google Cloud, and the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

Here is the premise, and it is a reasonable one. As language models become agents, the scarce resource for training shifts from curated text data to interactive environments. Those environments are hand-built: someone hardcodes interaction logic and a verifier, and the result behaves identically whether it faces a novice or a competent policy. Two failure modes follow. A static environment cannot target the specific weaknesses of the policy you actually have, and once the policy learns to solve its existing tasks, the environment has nothing left to teach. Automated environment generation exists as an alternative, with systems like GenEnv simulating environments and their feedback inside a model, VeriEnv synthesizing executable environments programmatically, and SWE-smith generating new task instances within an existing benchmark. But those pipelines are domain-specific, each built separately for web navigation, programming, or tool use, and because both tasks and verifiers come out of a model, teams over-generate and filter heavily without ever guaranteeing correctness. EnvHarness takes a different position: stop authoring new environments, and reshape the ones you already trust.

The formal object is standard reinforcement-learning notation. An environment is a tuple of state space, action space, observation space, transition function, reward induced by the verifier, and initial state. An EnvHarness component is an environment-agnostic transformation that maps this tuple to a modified one strictly at the interface level, changing the initial state, filtering exposed actions and observations, or altering transition mechanics, without touching the simulator backend. Because nothing inside changes, every reshaped environment inherits the original human-built verifier. That is the part worth taking seriously; it sidesteps the unreliable-verifier problem entirely rather than mitigating it.

Three component types are defined. A Stage specifies a sequence of state-manipulation actions applied to the state returned by reset, so only the initial state changes. On their running ALFWorld mug-cleaning task, where the default instance leaves the mug in the open and ends immediately on placement, one Stage executes take mug, open drawer, put mug in drawer, close drawer, hiding the object to force search behavior; another pre-cleans the mug in advance, leaving only placement steps. A Contract specifies a triplet of transformation maps, each defaulting to identity, that rewrite the action space, transition dynamics, and observation space. Concrete instances truncate a room description after its first two sentences to force building a spatial representation across steps, block the cleaning action unless the agent is holding the mug, and delete high-level teleport navigation commands so movement must be stepwise. A Chain pairs the base environment with an extension environment plus a composition logic, exposing a composite through the same interface, with unioned action and observation spaces and a conjunctive composite verifier; the worked example appends heat a potato and put it on the countertop, which verifies only when both halves pass, teaching goal persistence past the point where the agent would normally stop. Components compose freely by nesting, Stage inside Contract inside Chain, and composition does not commute, so nesting order determines which constraints apply at initialization versus during interaction.

Automation comes from EnvRigger, a four-stage loop treating the policy as a black box throughout; no weights are inspected. Observe collects successful and failed trajectories on the base task; successes matter because they bound where the flaw starts. Diagnose extracts systemic causes such as repetitive action loops, failures parsing long observations, or misread tool constraints, and decides direction: scaffold missing steps for a struggling policy, or inject harder scenarios when success saturates. Write synthesizes candidate components against that diagnosis, often combining a Stage and a Contract when one flaw needs both. Validate wraps the current environment, runs fresh rollouts on the same task, and judges purely on trajectory metrics like success rate and failure distribution: accept, reject candidates that are unsolvable or non-challenging, or send poorly scaled ones back for revision until a budget expires. Accepted components join an accumulating harness. The loop presumes deterministic resets for validation, which already excludes live-service backends where a sent email cannot be undone.

Experiments cover five benchmarks in four domains: ALFWorld for embodied text tasks, WebArena for web interaction, SWE-bench Verified for software engineering, and OfficeQA plus SpreadsheetBench for office automation, evaluated under skill-based learning with skills extracted following ReasoningBank, plus a separate reinforcement-learning arm. Crucially, EnvRigger and the policy share the same model backbone on each benchmark, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite on ALFWorld and WebArena and Gemini 3.5 Flash elsewhere, so gains cannot be dismissed as distilling a stronger teacher. Chains were excluded from the automated pipeline because the designer cannot observe internal states of joined environments; they get their own analysis. Baselines are the frozen agent with no skills, skills from original environments, and the domain-specific generators, all sharing seed instances, environment count, and extraction pipeline, with each evaluation instance attempted once and results averaged over three runs.

The numbers are consistent rather than spectacular. Skills from customized environments beat skills from original ones everywhere: ALFWorld average rises from 62.4 to 68.3, with a 9-point jump in the held-out split from 61.4 to 70.4; WebArena average goes from 38.5 to 41.6. Against GenEnv specifically, EnvHarness skills win by 5.7 points on average and 8.5 points out of distribution, which matters because generic instance generation merely adds repetitive practice. On SWE-bench Verified the success rate moves from 49.88 to 52.58, ahead of SWE-smith's 50.12 by 2.46 points, while cutting average execution steps per episode from 55.01 to 49.61, the advertised 9.8 percent reduction; skills from untouched environments had pushed steps up to 55.01. OfficeQA exact match improves from 54.40 to 56.20. On SpreadsheetBench, skills extracted from unmodified environments fall below the no-skill baseline of 46.44, while EnvHarness lifts pass@1 to 49.15, evidence that practicing on static tasks can actively hurt. The RL arm uses GRPO on Qwen3-8B-base across eight H100s with vLLM rollouts, temperature 0.4 sampling, an invalid-action penalty of 0.1, maximum prompt length of 4096 tokens, response length 512, history and episode caps of 50 steps, and 150 training steps. Customized environments carry real optimization signal: ALFWorld in-distribution success climbs from 81.4 to 87.9, WebShop score from 75.6 to 79.2, with one honest negative, an out-of-distribution dip from 89.6 to 88.8. Under an identical 300-environment budget on SWE-bench Verified, each batch of 50 yielding one skill bank targeting the previously equipped policy, EnvHarness reaches 54.79 versus 52.13 for real environments and 50.37 for generated ones, still climbing where both baselines flatten. Across four backbones spanning Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, Qwen3.6 27B, Gemini 3.5 Flash, and Claude Sonnet 4.6, with skill-free success rates from 30.7 to 67.2, gains over real-environment skills hold between 2.7 and 3.7 points regardless of base strength. One more capability: the loop accepts explicit user constraints, such as rejecting code submissions unless tests ran first, compiled into a contract enforcing a pre-commit hook, from which the pipeline distills a general verification-driven development skill.

Limitations deserve plain statement. The design loop burns rollouts per candidate environment, weaker designers need more iterations, and building pools consumes substantial inference compute even if amortized once per environment rather than per episode. The reset requirement excludes non-resettable backends and physical robots. Chains concatenate but cannot express branching or shared intermediate state and have no notion of semantic relatedness between joined tasks. Everything operates over textual actions and observations only.

Where would I apply scrutiny before adopting this? The acceptance criterion in Validate is judged by the same model family that wrote the components, so a systematically wrong diagnosis could self-confirm; the paper reports aggregate wins, not per-component audits. The headline ALFWorld gain rides on recurring parametric task types, the friendliest setting for targeted wrapping. Verify step-count savings on your own traces, since shorter episodes could partly reflect conservative behavior. Bottom line: this is a clean engineering reframing, wrapping verified environments instead of generating new ones, and verifier inheritance alone makes it worth prototyping. Treat reported gains as plausible upper bounds for well-behaved benchmarks, and budget real compute for the design loop before committing.
