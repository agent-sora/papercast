Why Gated DeltaNet Survives 4-Bit Quantization, by Sergii Kozyrev and Davyd Maiboroda, from Minima AI. This is a short, focused systems paper rather than a model release, and its whole argument rests on one counterintuitive claim: the recurrent half of a hybrid large language model is the easier half to quantize, not the harder half. If you are running hybrid models like Qwen 3.8 27B and you have been hand-tuning which blocks to keep in high precision, the paper says the hand-tuning is backwards.

The setup. Hybrid large language models interleave ordinary softmax attention with linear-attention layers such as Gated DeltaNet. Gated DeltaNet keeps a recurrent state that summarizes the whole context in a fixed size, which is what makes it cheap to run over long sequences. The catch that has guided the community so far is that a recurrence is supposed to accumulate error. Every small mistake in the state gets carried forward and compounded over thousands of steps, so people assumed the Gated DeltaNet block, especially its decay gate and its write-strength gate, had to stay in 8-bit or 16-bit precision no matter what. Early community 4-bit quantizations of Qwen 3.8 27B, which has 48 Gated DeltaNet layers and 16 attention layers, all left the Gated DeltaNet block in 8 or 16 bits on exactly that intuition. The decay gate in particular was treated as sacred, on the reasoning that a small bias in the decay rate is a small change every single step, and over 32K steps a small per-step bias becomes a large state drift.

This paper tests that intuition directly. The authors build a checkpoint they call Minima, and Minima quantizes everything: NVFP4 weights and NVFP4 activations, that is 4-bit floating point with per-block scaling, on all 496 linear layers of the model, Gated DeltaNet gates included. No block gets a pass. Then they measure it against the full-precision BF16 baseline across a spread of tasks: perplexity at 4K and 32K context, MMLU-Pro, GSM8K, AIME 2025, GPQA Diamond, LiveCodeBench, and RULER retrieval at up to 64K tokens. For the 4-bit recipe to be fair they also compare against the two most common community starting points, the Unsloth 4-bit checkpoint and a RadixArk checkpoint, so the result is not just quantized-versus-not but quantized-recipe-versus-quantized-recipe.

The headline result. Minima matches BF16 within seed noise. On a five-task average it lands at minus 0.52, which the authors treat as inside the variance you get from different random seeds. The 32K perplexity on WikiText-2 sits right at about 2.02 to 2.03, essentially on top of the BF16 figure, and the gap between the quantized and full-precision perplexity actually shrinks as you move down the sequence rather than growing. The long-context retrieval numbers carry the same story: on RULER, long-needle retrieval out to 64K tokens holds at the BF16 level, which matters because long-range retrieval is precisely the regime where an accumulating state error would be most likely to show up. It is also the smallest and fastest recipe they compare: 17.5 GiB on disk, and 14 to 19 percent faster on prefill. On AIME 2025 they report the 95 percent confidence intervals explicitly, with the BF16 baseline running from 79.2 up to 94.2 across seeds, and the quantized checkpoints overlapping that band, which is exactly why they lean on the confidence intervals rather than the point estimates when they make the within-noise claim. The five-task average itself is defined as the mean over MMLU-Pro, GSM8K, AIME 2025, GPQA Diamond, and LiveCodeBench, so it is a deliberately broad spread rather than a single hard benchmark.

Now the interesting part, because a single benchmark table does not tell you why. The authors run a four-part mechanism study, and it is the part worth reproducing if you build quantized hybrids. First, NVFP4 uses block scaling over 16 elements, and that block scaling is what localizes the extreme outliers in the residual stream. Because the outliers are contained to their own block, the activation error gets spread out evenly across layer roles instead of piling up somewhere fragile, which is the exact opposite of what you get with a single global scale. Second, and this is the surprising one, the gate projections that everyone assumed were fragile turn out to be the least sensitive part of the whole block. The softplus, exponential, and sigmoid parameterizations compress roughly 11 percent GEMM-level error down to about 2 percent error at the gate output, so the nonlinearity is doing the work that the high precision was supposed to do. A direct-noise probe on the decay gate confirms the asymmetry: the recurrence is robust to a lot of injected error, and the paper notes that only a targeted 1 percent direct perturbation at a single position demonstrably harms the state, which is far less than the quantization noise you would expect to be dangerous. Third, the delta-rule recurrence holds injected noise at a flat plateau over 32K tokens, and it forgets a state impulse within a few hundred steps. That is because each write overwrites the state along the current key direction, so old noise gets overwritten rather than accumulated, which is the mechanism that defeats the compounding-error story. Fourth, the per-token quantization cost washes out as context grows instead of compounding.

There is one engineering gotcha that is genuinely useful and would waste you a day if you hit it blind. When per-module-calibrated NVFP4 checkpoints are served by kernels that fuse those modules into a single GEMM, a global-scale mismatch appears, and the fused path degrades in perplexity even though the per-module numbers look fine. The paper documents the repair, and it also shows that calibrated FP8 KV-cache scales are essentially performance-free, which is a nice free lunch if you are serving the model, because you get the KV-cache memory savings without paying for it on quality.

The skeptical read. This is a two-author paper on a single model family, one checkpoint, and one quantization format. The claim that the recurrent half is the easy half is well supported within Qwen 3.8 27B, but the authors do not sweep it across architectures, and the mechanism study, while convincing, is a handful of controlled probes rather than a broad ablation. The 5-task average being inside seed noise is reassuring, but the individual AIME and GPQA numbers do move by a point or two depending on the seed, so a single-run comparison is not the story; the seed confidence intervals are. The global-scale-mismatch repair is specific to a particular serving setup, so if your kernel fuses modules differently, you will need to re-derive it. And the throughput win is measured on a single RTX PRO 6000 at tensor-parallelism one with FP8 KV, so the 14 to 19 percent prefill number is the figure to trust for that exact configuration, not as a constant you can carry to any deployment. One more thing to re-verify before adopting: the paper measures the quantized model against its own BF16 sibling, so the within-noise claim is about this particular Qwen 3.8 27B lineage, and whether a different hybrid with a different attention-to-recurrent ratio or a different decay-gate parameterization reproduces the flat plateau is left as an open question.

The bottom line: this is a compact, well-argued systems result that overturns a widely held rule of thumb, and the four-part mechanism is the part to keep. The practical takeaway, quantize everything and ship calibrated FP8 KV scales, is directly actionable on Qwen 3.8 27B and should be on your list the next time you ship a hybrid. The limits of the claim are the single-architecture, single-checkpoint, single-format scope, so treat the "the recurrent half is the easy half" conclusion as robust within hybrid Gated DeltaNet models but not yet as a universal law you can apply to any recurrence.
