Random Is Almost Enough, Stable and Low-Cost Benchmark Compression via BestRand, is from George Ling, Shanshan Zhong, and Lijin Yang, working at Bosch Research and Carnegie Mellon University.

Benchmark compression is the practice of evaluating a model on a small subset of a benchmark and using that to estimate the full-benchmark score, because running every test example on every candidate model is prohibitively expensive. The paper starts from a question that most of the field has just assumed: how accurate and how reliable is that estimate, really? And the answer, which is the contribution, is a derived analytical approximation to the error distribution when you estimate a full-benchmark score from a subset. The shape of that distribution is the finding.

The distribution is long-tailed. Most subsets produce small errors, but a few produce much larger errors. That single fact explains two things that have looked contradictory in the literature. On one hand, random sampling alone achieves surprisingly strong performance with high probability, which is why a lot of teams just pick a random subset and move on. On the other hand, random sampling is unstable, because of those rare large-error subsets, and the instability is not something you fix by evaluating more models. The paper shows that the instability is inherent to modern foundation models and persists even as more models are evaluated, which is the counterintuitive part, because you would expect a bigger model population to average out the noise.

The explanation for both the high-probability success and the persistent instability is the similarity among large models. The models are correlated in how they score benchmark items, and that correlation is what creates the long tail. When models agree a lot, a subset that happens to be easy or hard for one model is easy or hard for all of them, so the subset-level error does not average out across the model population. The paper quantifies this with a behavioral correlation parameter, and it shows that higher correlation produces longer tails, and that this correlation together with a common-shift scale strongly predicts the empirical tail risk, the P95 of the error distribution. The fit is tight, with reported R-squared values near zero point nine nine, which is about as clean a theory-to-empirics match as you get in this area.

The method, BestRand, is deliberately modest, and the modesty is the point. It preserves the efficiency of random sampling while reducing the high-error tail. Concretely, it generates multiple random subsets and uses a small number of probe models to search for the best candidate subset. The probe models are cheap to run, and the theory identifies a mechanism by which probe-guided selection reduces the common-shift variability, which is what makes the selection meaningful rather than just picking the subset that happens to look good on one probe. So the construction cost is the cost of running a handful of probe models over a handful of candidate subsets, which is a fraction of a full evaluation.

The validation is broad, which is the part that gives the result weight. BestRand is validated on more than seventy text, multimodal, and agent benchmarks, and it is extended to vision-language-action evaluation, which is a harder setting because the benchmarks are closed-loop and expensive. Against competitive methods, BestRand reduces the high-error risk by fifty point two percent and reduces the average estimation error by sixteen point four percent, while requiring only fifty-one point three percent of the construction cost of the alternatives. That is the core efficiency-accuracy trade: better than the competition on both axes and roughly half the cost. The out-of-distribution results are the more interesting of the two, because they are where compression methods usually fall apart. Under three types of shift, score-range shift, model-family shift, and temporal shift, BestRand reduces the out-of-distribution estimation error by twenty-one point eight percent on average, and it achieves the lowest average mean absolute error in every single out-of-distribution setting in the reported table.

The cost framing is worth being precise about because it is how you would actually use this in a lab. The paper defines a construction-cost unit where one full evaluation of one model is cost one. Response-free methods, which pick subsets without looking at any model scores, sit at cost zero but have much larger errors. BestRand stays accurate at a cost below one, meaning it is cheaper than a single full model evaluation to build, whereas the learnable baselines require a cost of at least one, a full evaluation, just to construct the subset. At a fixed twenty percent retention, where you keep twenty percent of the benchmark, BestRand achieves lower average mean absolute error at the same construction cost across the text benchmarks, and requires less construction cost to reach the same accuracy. For a team that is evaluating dozens of model candidates, that is the difference between a subset you build once cheaply and a subset that costs you a full evaluation to construct.

The theory-empirics agreement is the part I would lean on most, because it is what lets you predict the behavior on a new benchmark before you run it. The paper shows that the behavioral correlation and the common-shift scale together predict the empirical P95 error with an R-squared near zero point nine nine, and that the mode of the theoretical error distribution matches the empirical mode within about one percent on the representative benchmarks. That is a strong enough fit to use as a planning tool: if you can estimate the correlation among your candidate models on a small held-out set, you can predict the shape of the error tail you are about to live with, and you can choose the retention rate to keep that tail within a bound you are willing to accept. The theory is doing real work here, not just explaining the result after the fact. A skeptical read has a few things I would verify: the theory is clean but it rests on the behavioral correlation being stable, and that correlation is a property of the current generation of models, which are highly correlated precisely because they share a lot of training data and architecture. If the model population diversifies, the correlation drops and the tail shortens, which means the instability that BestRand is fighting against partly goes away on its own, and the method's advantage over plain random sampling shrinks in that regime. That is a real limitation that the paper's own theory implies but that I would want them to state more explicitly. Second, the probe-based selection is a form of model selection over subsets, and the probe models need to be representative of the models you will actually evaluate, because the selection is optimizing for the probes. If the probes are not a good proxy for your candidate population, the selected subset is optimized for the wrong distribution. The out-of-distribution results are strong, but they are averaged over three shift types, and I would look at the per-type variance, because the temporal shift, past to future, is the one that matters most for a benchmark you are reusing over time, and that is the one most likely to be the tail of the tail.

The engineering gotcha is the probe choice and the retention rate. The retention rate determines your evaluation cost, and the probe choice determines whether the selected subset is actually good for your models. Both are inputs you control, and both interact: a low retention rate makes the probe selection more important because you have fewer items to get right, and a weak probe set makes the whole method no better than random at low retention. If you are adopting this, the first experiment is to run BestRand and plain random at your target retention on your own candidate models and look at the actual tail, because the fifty percent high-error-risk reduction is an average across seventy-plus benchmarks, and your specific benchmark may sit at either end of that distribution.

Bottom line: BestRand is a well-motivated, low-cost refinement of random benchmark compression, with a genuinely useful analytical account of why the error distribution is long-tailed and why the instability persists as you add models, and with broad validation showing it beats the alternatives on both error and cost across text, multimodal, agent, and vision-language-action benchmarks. The main caveat is that its advantage over plain random sampling is largest exactly when modern models are highly correlated, so on a more diverse model population, or on a benchmark at the tail of the seventy-plus it validates on, re-measure the tail on your own data before you commit.
