The paper is OR-License, an evaluation of large language model agents as real-world operations research practitioners, from Peisong Wang, Feixue Liu, and Yize Meng, at HKUST-GZ, the University of Hong Kong, and Qingzhi Optimization, with a large multi-institution author list that includes Tencent's Hunyuan team. Operations research is the discipline that turns an incomplete business problem, a logistics constraint, a staffing schedule, a production plan, into a validated optimization model and a solver, and it is high-skill work. The Gurobi state of optimization report the authors cite finds that 82 percent of practitioners hold a master's or doctoral degree. The paper asks whether an LLM agent can do that work end to end, starting from the original client materials, without a human reformulating the problem first. That is a harder question than the existing benchmarks ask, and it is the gap the paper is built to fill.

The paper identifies three gaps in how LLMs are currently evaluated on optimization work. Gap one is task complexity and practical relevance. Most benchmarks are built from academic, synthetic, or reconstructed problems, and they do not capture the operational complexity of a real client engagement. Gap two is information acquisition. Benchmarks that hand the model the full task context skip the work of establishing that context, and in a real engagement the practitioner has to notice what is missing, ask targeted questions, and integrate the answers as the work proceeds. Gap three is grounding in professional delivery. There is no reference tied to a solution a real expert actually delivered and a real client accepted, so there is nothing to measure professional quality against.

OR-License addresses all three, and the three pieces fit together in a specific order. First, the task set. It is an end-to-end benchmark of 31 instances across 10 industrial scenarios, drawn from real paid operations-research engagements with a total project value of 3.68 million dollars. The instances preserve the original requirements, the input data, the deliverable formats, and the information boundaries, and are de-identified but otherwise left intact, so the agent faces the same ambiguity, the same missing pieces, and the same formatting constraints a real client would impose. Second, the client simulator, ORSim, which lets the agent acquire client-side business information throughout the workflow, but only discloses business facts and never supplies operations-research expertise, so it preserves the boundary between what a client knows and what the practitioner is supposed to figure out. Third, the reference. The evaluation compares each agent's solution against the original expert solution that was accepted by the client before the benchmark was built, checking feasibility and comparing objective quality against a delivery that was actually used.

The central metric is what they call Deployment-Adjusted Quality, or DAQ. It averages expert-relative quality across the instances, but assigns zero credit to any infeasible delivery. That is a deliberate choice, and it matters. An agent can produce a solution whose objective looks good but violates a hard constraint, and a naive quality score would reward it. DAQ does not. Infeasibility is treated as a total failure, which is the right call for a deployment setting where an infeasible plan is worthless, and it is also what makes the expert reference meaningful, because the expert solutions by construction were feasible and were accepted.

The evaluation runs 11 frontier large language models, each wrapped in a coding-agent harness, so the model is doing the work through the same interface a practitioner would use. The harness matters here in a way it does not in a static benchmark, because the agent is expected to read the original client materials, decide what is missing, run the simulator to get answers, formulate the optimization model, implement the solver, and iterate. That is a long chain, and a weak harness can hide a strong model and a strong harness can partially paper over a weak one, which is why the paper reports the harness configuration alongside every result.

The headline result is a clear split. Several models exceed the expert reference on conditional objective quality, meaning that when they do produce a feasible solution, its objective value can be competitive with or better than the expert's. But none of them matches the expert's DAQ. The strongest primary configuration, a Claude Code setup, achieves 88.39 percent strict feasibility and a DAQ of 0.87, against the expert reference of 1.0. So even the best agent gets a feasible, near-expert solution most of the time, but it is not reliably there, and the gap to 1.0 is exactly the infeasible deliveries that DAQ zeroes out.

The paper then looks at where in the workflow the agents acquire information, and the finding is that it is not a one-time clarification step at the start. Information acquisition spreads across solution development and validation, and some later stages actually show higher proportions of confirmed issue resolution than the initial stage. That is a useful and somewhat counterintuitive result. It suggests the agents do learn things as they build the model, not just when they ask upfront. The trajectory analysis also measures research-chain completion, the rate at which an agent carries a complete loop of noticing a gap, investigating it, and revising the solution based on evidence, and that rate ranges from 24 to 45 percent across models on the evaluable tasks. That is the reliability gap in concrete terms. An agent may reason well when it does complete an investigation, but it only completes the investigation in a minority of the opportunities where one was needed.

The paper makes a point of separating high per-investigation quality from sustained reliability. One model, Qwen3.8-Max, has the highest conditional stage quality, the best reasoning within the investigations it does complete, but a lower DAQ of 77.97 percent, and it completes the full research chain in only 27 percent of opportunities. So the best reasoning and the best end-to-end reliability are not the same model. That is a clean illustration of the paper's central point: current agents can show strong evidence-based reasoning in isolated episodes but lack the reliability to carry investigations from uncertainty to a grounded revision across a long task. The paper's own summary of this is that the agents demonstrate strong evidence-based reasoning when they do complete an investigation, but the investigation itself only happens in a minority of the opportunities, and the DAQ gap to the expert is dominated by the opportunities where the agent never completed the loop at all.

What I would trust here is the reference, which is the strongest part of the paper. Tying the evaluation to client-accepted expert deliveries, and using a metric that zeroes infeasible outputs, is exactly the right way to ground a professional-quality benchmark, and the 31 real engagements with preserved data and information boundaries make it substantially more realistic than a synthetic set. The inter-annotator agreement numbers, Krippendorff's alpha above 0.83 on the labeling dimensions, are also reassuring. What I would re-verify: 31 instances across 10 scenarios is a small benchmark by the standards the field now expects, and the conclusions are only as strong as that sample, so the DAQ numbers carry wide uncertainty. The harnesses are coding-agent wrappers around frontier models, and the results are sensitive to the harness as much as the model, which the paper acknowledges but does not fully decompose. And the expert reference is a single accepted delivery per instance, so it is a point estimate of expert quality, not a distribution. A skeptical read: this is the most realistically grounded operations-research benchmark I have seen, and the finding that no agent matches expert DAQ despite near-expert conditional quality is a defensible, important result. But it is a small sample, a single expert reference per task, and a harness-sensitive measurement, so treat the specific DAQ values as directional rather than precise.
