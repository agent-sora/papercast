Trajectory2Gym: Decision-Grounded Compositional Interaction Synthesis for Tool-Use Agents, by Yihao Hu, Zhihao Wen, and Yitong Lin, from Westlake University and the Ant International research group in Hangzhou, with code and model weights released on the project page and Hugging Face.

The starting point of this paper is an uncomfortable observation about how tool-use agents are trained today. The default recipe is to collect trajectories that succeed, filter them, and do supervised fine-tuning on the whole thing. But a successful trajectory is not automatically a useful teaching example. A run can succeed because the agent happened to take a shortcut, or because the environment was forgiving, while the specific multi-step dependency the task actually required was never exercised. Conversely, a successful run can contain an action that was never justified by anything the agent could see at decision time: the model got away with it, the trajectory passed the success filter, and the model now has a demonstration of a move that would not generalize. The authors formalize two requirements that a training trajectory should meet. The first is compositional coverage: the important observation-to-action dependencies, the cases where an earlier observation or user update determines a later decision, must actually be represented in the data. The second is decision grounding: every action you supervise must be supported by the interaction history that is visible to the learner at the moment of the decision. Their argument is that these two requirements are coupled, and that most pipelines fail both at the same time, because outcome filtering answers a different question than the one training needs answered.

The framework has three stages, and the paper is explicit that they are coupled: synthesis without grounding teaches the collector's shortcuts, grounding without synthesis certifies a pool where recovery behavior stays rare, and selection without both just re-weights the pool. Stage one is compositional interaction synthesis. Instead of mining the environment for successful runs, they construct tasks around interaction paths that are under-represented in naturally occurring trajectories: the lookup that fails, the retry after a user correction, the confirmation that partially applies to a multi-part request. These tasks are built and then executed in the native environment, so the data carries authentic tool outputs, state transitions, and error messages rather than hand-written ones. Stage two is grounded supervision. Each candidate action in a realized execution is checked against the agent-visible history, the execution result, and the workflow order, and a decision-level supervision mask is produced: some actions in an otherwise successful run are kept as teaching targets, and others are excluded because they were not grounded in what the model could see. Notably, authentic errors are kept in the run as recovery context rather than discarded; the model sees the wrong move and the correction, which is exactly the pattern that matters in multi-turn support work. Stage three is structural-support learning. Trajectories are selected so that capability paths get a per-scale amount of repeated support, and the fine-tuning objective is parent-normalized supervised fine-tuning, where the semantic interaction, not the individual turn, is the unit of training weight.

The paper walks through a concrete failure mode from the Tau2 retail domain to make the distinction tangible. A user asks for a larger bottle of one product and a dimmer setting on a lamp, then, after the agent proposes both changes, confirms only the lamp. Executing the run with the native tools, both candidate actions replay successfully, so a success-based filter keeps both as teaching targets. But the catalog evidence and the user's latest confirmation say the bottle exchange was never accepted, and the grounding check rejects that action while keeping the lamp exchange. The same two candidate actions, two different verdicts, depending on which rule you apply. That is the class of error the supervision mask is designed to catch, and it is exactly the kind of partially-confirmed multi-part request that appears constantly in real support conversations.

The evaluation is where the claims get testable. They train five Qwen3 scales, from 1.7 billion parameters up to a 30 billion parameter mixture-of-experts model with 3 billion active parameters, and evaluate on three benchmarks: Tau1 and Tau2, the interactive tool-use suites, and BFCL, the function-calling leaderboard with parallel, multiple, and irrelevance categories plus live and non-live splits. The headline number is an equal-weight three-benchmark mean that goes up by 16.9 to 29.8 percentage points at every tested scale, with the paired gain on the two Tau benchmarks ranging from 20 to 41 points. The smallest paired gain is at 1.7 billion, about 20 points, and it grows to the 35-to-41 range from 4 billion upward, which the authors read as evidence that the gains concentrate in stateful, multi-turn interaction where later decisions depend on earlier observations, the exact region their synthesis targets. Two comparisons deserve emphasis. The post-trained 4 billion model beats the public 30 billion initialization on Tau2, 69.17 versus 28.33, so the data recipe outweighs a seven-fold size gap on that benchmark. And the post-trained 30 billion model reaches 84.17 on Tau2, within 1.2 points of the closed-source references the paper reports at 84.76 and 85.31. Against re-evaluated agent-training checkpoints from five public families, including 14 billion and 32 billion models trained with reinforcement learning, their 8 billion model reaches 74.17 on Tau2, at least 28 points above every one of them. On the BFCL side there is one consistent exception: Live Simple is down 6 to 13 points at four of the five scales, which the authors hypothesize reflects the fact that their synthesis over-represents complex multi-call structure relative to that category.

The ablations are matched, which is more than most data-centric papers give you. The largest isolated gain comes from parent normalization, 8.04 points on the three-benchmark mean at the 8 billion scale, with structural-support selection contributing a separate, smaller effect. A sensitivity test on the structural-support depth, which controls how much repeated support gets allocated to certified interaction structures, finds the shallowest setting performs best, 72.28 on the mean versus 70.55 and 69.44 for deeper settings, even though deeper settings produce longer trajectories. The paper is careful to flag that the headline schedule was fixed before those runs, so this is a sensitivity test rather than checkpoint selection. There is also a statistical caveat worth repeating: one Tau2 Airline task moves the three-benchmark mean by 0.56 points, so gains of a few points are directional rather than decisive, and the authors say so.

The skeptical read. What I would trust is the mechanism: the decision-level supervision mask is a concrete, checkable artifact, and the matched ablations showing each stage contributes under a fixed budget are the right kind of evidence for a data recipe. The scale sweep, showing the same recipe lifting five different model sizes, is a stronger claim than a single checkpoint comparison. What I would re-verify before adopting: the public agent-training checkpoints are used as references rather than matched controls, meaning they were not re-trained under identical compute and data budgets, so the 28-point margins on Tau2 should be read as upper bounds on the comparison. The BFCL Live Simple regression is a real cost, and anyone deploying on live multi-step function-calling traffic should reproduce it. The construction pipeline itself, building tasks around under-supported paths, is the part with the most moving pieces, and the paper's own framing is that the three stages are coupled, which is also a warning that porting one stage without the others will not reproduce the result. The bottom line: this is one of the more disciplined data-centric training papers in the tool-use space, with gains that scale across model sizes and ablations that isolate each component. If you are building tool-use agents and currently filter trajectories by success, the decision-grounding check is the part to steal first.
