The paper is called ProTEUS: A mutation-resolved world model for closed-loop protein evolution. The first three authors are Yikun Zhang, Minghao Guo, and Guangxue Xu, with the lead institution Foundry Bioscience and collaborators from MIT, Stanford, and a handful of other universities.

The problem is a combinatorial one that has haunted protein engineering for decades. You want to make a protein do something better, maybe catalyze a reaction faster, bind tighter, stay stable. The sequence space is enormous. You can mutate a few residues, you can mutate many, and the interactions between mutations, the epistasis, means that a combination that looks good on paper can fail in the lab and a combination that looks mediocre can be the winner. Experimental screening, the gold standard, can only sample a tiny fraction of that space. So the central challenge is to navigate the space of mutations in silico, to predict the effect of a mutation before you make it, and then to use the experimental results to sharpen the predictions for the next round. This is the closed loop that directed evolution runs, but doing it well has been hard.

The existing models, and this is the gap they are filling, fall into two camps. The sequence-based protein language models, the ESM family, learn from natural proteins and estimate the effect of a substitution from amino acid likelihoods. Then there are the family-specific and multimodal models, things like MSA Transformer, Tranception, PoET, and the ones that combine sequence and structure, and the inverse-folding models that check whether a sequence is compatible with a backbone. These are all useful, but the paper is blunt about the ceiling: they show only a moderate correlation with experimentally measured activity, especially for proteins that are underrepresented in evolution. And there is a deeper reason. Natural sequence distributions reflect the selection pressures of an organism, which can differ a lot from the specific activity you are trying to optimize. A favorable evolutionary or structural score does not guarantee higher activity for your target. So the models are well-calibrated to the wrong objective.

So ProTEUS is a world model for closed-loop protein evolution with high mutation resolution. The idea, and this is the framing worth sitting with, is to treat protein variants as states in a joint embedding space and mutations as actions whose functional consequences are predicted. That is the reinforcement-learning vocabulary, states and actions, applied to a biological problem. A variant is a state. You take a mutation, that is an action, and the model predicts what happens to the function. And then you use experimental feedback to refine the model and guide the next round of selection. That loop is the whole point.

The architecture is called MutationFormer, and it is a mutation-biased attention design. The key insight is a resolution problem. Most protein models pool the residue-level features into a single protein-level representation, and that obscures the local information that matters for predicting the effect of a specific mutation. A variant differs from its neighbors by just a few substituted residues, and you need to focus the model's attention on those few residues while preserving the surrounding context that determines their effects. So MutationFormer aligns the amino acid identity, the multiple sequence alignment, and the structural features residue by residue, marks the substitutions with an explicit mutation mask, and runs attention that is biased toward those substituted positions. A twelve-layer transformer does this. The bias is the point: the model focuses on the mutations that define a candidate while letting their predicted effects depend on the surrounding protein.

They pretrain this on one hundred eighty two deep mutational scanning assays from ProteinGym, spanning one hundred sixty one protein backgrounds, with measured stability, organismal fitness, activity, expression, and binding. The scale of that pretraining data is one of the interesting findings. They show a data-scaling behavior with respect to protein activity data, the same kind of scaling you see in language models and protein language models. Increasing the training data by a factor of eighty reduces the test mean squared error from zero point two six two to zero point one one five, a fifty six point three percent reduction, and it follows an approximate power law, about a twelve point one percent drop in error for each doubling of data. That is a potentially new axis of scaling for protein foundation models, namely, throw more measured activity data at it. That is a genuinely useful observation for the field.

The benchmarks are strong. On the held-out test set of the pretraining data, ProTEUS gets a Spearman correlation of zero point nine three three and a mean squared error of zero point one one five, while the strongest evaluated baseline, PoET-2, manages only zero point eight six one. When they ablate, the mutation bias is doing real work: removing it drops the Spearman correlation from zero point eight four zero to zero point six zero six. And the multimodal context matters: sequence representations alone get zero point eight zero seven, while the joint sequence, MSA, and structural representations get zero point nine three eight. In zero-shot, on nineteen held-out assays, it gets the highest macro-averaged correlation. In supervised fine-tuning, it gets a mean Spearman of zero point five four nine versus zero point three six seven for the next best method. And in a simulated directed-evolution loop, by round five, ProTEUS reaches a normalized best fitness of zero point seven four one versus zero point five five five for EVOLVEpro.

There is also a practical component that makes the closed loop actually fast. They built a fast, physics-constrained all-atom structural predictor that combines two denoising steps with differentiable Gauss-Seidel projection to enforce geometric and stereochemical constraints. This gives them over ten times the speed of AlphaFold three, with guaranteed physical validity, which is what you need when you are going to screen a large library of higher-order candidates in silico before sending them to the lab.

Now the headline experimental result, which is the one that matters. They ran two closed-loop, multi-objective engineering campaigns in the lab, one on aldehyde dehydrogenase and one on levansucrase, and in both they found variants with more than ten times higher activity than the wild type, within a budget of a hundred sequences. Ten-fold, within a hundred sequences, in the actual lab, not a simulation. That is the proof that the closed loop works end to end.

Now the skeptical pass. First, the comparison to EVOLVEpro and MULTI-evolve is fair on the benchmarks, but I would note that these are the two main baselines in this specific line of work, and the field is young. The ten-fold improvement in the lab is impressive, but it is on two enzymes, and the generalization of the closed-loop framework to a very different protein class or a binding objective rather than a catalytic one is not yet demonstrated here. Second, the data-scaling result is on pretraining data from ProteinGym, which is a curated set of deep mutational scanning assays. The power-law is encouraging, but it is measured within a limited range of data sizes, and whether it holds as you scale to orders of magnitude more data, or whether it saturates, is an open question they do not fully resolve. Third, the zero-shot and fine-tuning benchmarks are strong, but the most important number for a practitioner is the hit rate in a real campaign, and the paper reports the ten-fold result without a very detailed breakdown of how the nomination strategy allocated the budget across rounds, which is where a lot of the practical value or failure would hide.

Here is my bottom line. This is a well-constructed world model that brings the states-and-actions framing and the closed experimental loop to protein engineering, and the two ten-fold lab results within a hundred sequences are the strongest evidence that the approach works in the real world. The mutation-biased attention and the data-scaling finding are both genuinely useful contributions. The caveats are that the lab validation is on two enzymes, the scaling law is measured within a limited range, and the budget-allocation details that would matter for replication are thin. But as a framework for closed-loop protein evolution, it is a credible and promising step.
