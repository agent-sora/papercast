Paper six is RSIAgent: Autonomous Exploration for Recursive Self-Improvement in New Environments, a collaboration between Aether AI, UC San Diego, and the University of Illinois Chicago. This one is an agent framework, not a model release, and the claim is the most literal use of the word "recursive" on today's show. The problem it targets is that a frontier model dropped into an unfamiliar environment, a new operating system, a new exam-style task suite, a new API surface, starts from zero: it has no memory of the environment's quirks, no strategy library, no sense of which parts of the task space are easy and which are traps. The standard fix is to collect demonstrations or fine-tune, and the paper's argument is that both are the wrong answer for a new environment, because you do not have demonstrations for something that does not exist yet, and fine-tuning is too expensive to do per environment. Their answer is that the model should explore the environment autonomously, in a structured way, before it is asked to solve real tasks, and the exploration itself should be organized by a set of cooperating agents.

The architecture is a training-free multi-agent loop with three roles. The curriculum agent looks at the environment and generates a sequence of subtasks ordered from easy to hard, which is the "broad" phase: it is mapping the whole task space and building an initial library of what is possible. The actor agent executes individual subtasks, and here is the key mechanism, it does not just try and fail; it runs a broad-then-deep exploration policy, where "broad" means sampling many different approaches to many different subtasks in parallel to build coverage, and "deep" means taking the most promising approach and iterating on it until it either works or is clearly not going to, specifically hunting for hard cases, hidden constraints, boundary conditions, and previously unknown causal dependencies. The verifier agent scores the outcomes and gives feedback, and critically, the memory is frozen: the system writes what it learned into a persistent memory that is not fine-tuned into the weights, it is read back as context in subsequent runs, evolving from round t to round t plus one without a single gradient step.

What gets written into that memory is the most specific claim in the paper, and it is worth unpacking. It is not a log of successes, it is a library of environment-specific causal relationships: reusable links between an action, the condition under which it works, and the consequence that follows. The paper frames this as agentic causal discovery, which is a stronger claim than "the agent stores notes," because a stored note says what happened, while a stored causal relationship says what will happen if, and that is the form of knowledge that transfers to a new task in the same environment. The keyword list on the first page names all four ingredients: recursive self-improvement, digital agents, broad and deep self-exploration, and agentic causal discovery, and the frozen memory is the artifact that connects the last one to the first.

The results are on two benchmarks. On OSWorld 2.0, a computer-use benchmark with 82 tasks, offline: partial score goes from 71.97 to 78.98, and binary success from 37.80 to 42.68, after the exploration phase. The context for how big those moves are sits in the comparison table, where the same family of models without the RSI framework are dramatically lower: Kimi-K2.6, the predecessor of the Kimi-K3 they run on top of, sits at 22.10 partial and 4.60 binary on OSWorld 2.0, so the base-model gap is the dominant term in the comparison, and the RSI gain is layered on top of an already-strong open model, which is the setup that lets the 78.98 partial exceed the GPT-6 Astra numbers they copy from official reports. ALE, Agents' Last Exam, a long-horizon knowledge and reasoning benchmark, near-term setting, 67 tasks: partial from 83.75 to 84.82, binary from 49.25 to 50.75. And this is where the headline lives: because the gains compound on top of a strong open-source base, the RSI framework, with GLM-5.3 serving as the actor agent and Kimi-K3 serving as both verifier and curriculum agent, ends up outperforming the frontier closed-source models they compare against, including Claude Opus 5 and GPT-6, by 6.38 points on OSWorld 2.0 and 2.56 on ALE. Note the division of labor, which is a design choice and not an accident: the model that executes the exploration and the model that grades it and proposes the next subtasks are different models, so the verifier is not just re-reading the actor's own reasoning and rubber-stamping it. That is the paper's central claim, and it is worth taking seriously, because it is not the model that improved, it is the environment-specific memory that got read back in.

The ablations are the part worth staring at, and they are run on four specific OSWorld 2.0 tasks, which makes them more honest than a single aggregate number. The four are a WPS spreadsheet repair, a REAPER audio editing task, a browser-based presentation repair, and a 3D Slicer liver segmentation task, and on all four, combining broad and deep exploration yields the highest partial score. In mean, the full RSI loop gets 74.54 percent, broad-only exploration gets 65.52, deep-only gets 56.50, and the no-RSI baseline with empty memory sits below all of them. There is a detail that changes how you should read those three numbers, though: broad-only exploration improves over the baseline on every one of the four tasks, whereas deep-only exploration actually falls below the baseline on the REAPER and presentation tasks. That means the deep phase, run from an empty memory, is not just less useful than broad, it is sometimes actively harmful, because it commits the agent to iterating on a single approach before it has any sense of the environment, and the iteration then entrenches a wrong strategy. The deep phase only earns its keep once the broad phase has filled the memory with enough structure to tell it what is worth iterating on. That ordering, broad first, deep second, is the actual finding of the ablation, and it is the opposite of the instinct, which is to spend your compute on the deep, clever search.

My skeptical read: the binary improvements are real but modest, 4.9 points on OSWorld and 1.5 on ALE, and the OSWorld offline setting is a setup where the exploration budget is controlled by the authors, so the gains are partly an artifact of having more compute to spend on the environment before the test, which is a legitimate strategy but not the same as the model getting smarter. There is also a baseline problem in the comparison table worth naming: the GPT-6 and Claude Opus 5 numbers are copied from those labs' official reports rather than re-run under identical conditions, so the "open model beats frontier model" headline is only as strong as the comparability of those two columns, and a 6-point gap on an offline snapshot can shrink or grow once the settings are matched. The frozen-memory design is still the transferable idea, and it is a clean one: if the improvement is in the memory, it is inspectable, it is portable, and it does not require you to trust a weight update you cannot audit. The ALE result is the weaker one, and the paper does not fully explain why the binary gain there is so small, which I would want a table for. Bottom line: RSIAgent is a reasonable engineering answer to the cold-start problem in new environments, the broad-then-deep split is the finding to remember, and the recursive label is earned only in the sense that the memory compounds, not in any sense that touches the weights.
