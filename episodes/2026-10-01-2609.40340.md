EvoDuet. The first three authors are Young-Jun Lee, Jinheon Baek, and Soyeong Jeong, working between the University of Minnesota and KAIST, with Seoul National University and Hanyang University on the rest of the byline.

The problem is evolutionary search with language models on optimization tasks that require knowledge the model does not have. The standard fix is to add a web search tool to the loop, and the paper shows why that fails: as the candidate solutions change, the search keeps returning the same pages. In their measurement, the in-loop search with GPT-5.6-Luna retrieves only 85 distinct URLs, of which 88.1 percent have already appeared before, against 248 distinct pages for EvoDuet, and the naive process stops improving after about 50 iterations. The best program found that way reaches a normalized discovery gain of 27.7 percent, against 84.5 percent for EvoDuet.

EvoDuet is a bilevel method that co-evolves the solutions and the search queries, with the model parameters fixed throughout. The outer loop is the usual evolutionary loop: generate candidates in parallel from the currently retrieved documents, evaluate them, keep the evaluated outcomes for later searches. The inner loop evolves the queries themselves, ranking candidate documents by the solution scores they are predicted to yield. Connecting the two is a retrieval gate: at each iteration the model assesses its own knowledge gap and chooses to retrieve new documents, reuse stored ones, or proceed without any. The inner loop is what breaks the repetition, because queries are selected for predicted usefulness rather than issued from the current candidate's surface form.

The setup is concrete. The scaffold is OpenEvolve, one candidate per iteration as the base setting, with parallel generation up to 16 candidates per iteration in the scaling experiments. The model lineup is Qwen3.5-9B, GPT-5.6-Luna, and Gemini-3.8-Flash. There are 21 optimization tasks for the headline comparison, drawn from domains including quantum compilation and protein design, and the evaluation metric is normalized discovery gain, which measures how far the best found solution sits between a baseline and an oracle.

The headline numbers: EvoDuet raises OpenEvolve's normalized discovery gain from 74.1 percent to 78.0 percent with GPT-5.6-Luna, and from 61.3 percent to 82.3 percent with Gemini-3.8-Flash. Qwen3.5-9B does not benefit, a result the paper analyzes in detail rather than hiding. The best EvoDuet runs surpass the previously reported best scores on eight tasks, including Swap Reduction on Q20 and a Rosetta task, and match the prior best on three more. On Denoising, EvoDuet surpasses the prior SimpleTES result at roughly 7 times lower compute.

The preliminary study in the paper is the part that motivates the whole design, and it is worth going through. Oracle documents, meaning the ideal retrieved content for each task, raise the average normalized discovery gain by 10.3 percent for Qwen3.5-9B and only 4.0 percent for GPT-5.6-Luna, because the larger model already reaches at least 95 percent of the oracle on 16 of the 31 tasks, where the oracle changes the gain by only 0.1 percent on average. The tasks where retrieval helps most are the hard ones: on Swap Reduction, a quantum compilation task, oracle documents add 6.1 percent for Qwen and 41.5 percent for GPT-5.6-Luna. But retrieval is not universally helpful. Oracle documents lower the gain by more than 1 percent on 8 of the 31 tasks for Qwen and 4 for GPT-5.6-Luna, including drops of 9.0 percent and 23.3 percent on Rosetta. That asymmetry is the argument for a retrieval gate: the model should be allowed to skip retrieval, not forced to consume documents every iteration.

Parallel generation changes the picture too. With one candidate per iteration, oracle documents do not help on some tasks, 14.8 percent normalized discovery gain with documents versus 15.4 without, but with 8 parallel candidates the same oracle lifts the gain from 25.8 to 40.7 percent. The model can exploit retrieved documents when it has multiple candidates to spend them on. And the bilevel design compounds: on the tasks where retrieval matters, EvoDuet retrieves about 2.9 times as many distinct pages as the search that keeps returning the same ones, and its best held-out programs reach 84.5 percent normalized discovery gain, against 27.7 percent for the naive in-loop search.

The Qwen result deserves a careful read, because it is the paper's main negative. Qwen3.5-9B shows a 14.4 percent decline with EvoDuet at one candidate per iteration, and still loses 4.7 percent even with 8 parallel candidates, despite gaining 10.3 percent from oracle documents. The authors inspect 50 randomly sampled revisions and find that Qwen frequently leaves the retrieved documents unused: the knowledge arrives, but the model does not apply it. So EvoDuet's gains are conditional on the base model actually reading and using retrieved content, and the method cannot manufacture that capability.

The scaling behavior with parallel candidates is monotonic. On the 8-task subset where they sweep the candidate count from 1 to 16, the average normalized discovery gain rises from 89.6 to 95.7 percent for GPT-5.6-Luna, and from 84.5 to 89.6 for Gemini-3.8-Flash. That suggests the method has not saturated at 16 candidates, though the cost of 16 candidates per iteration is 16 model calls plus the retrieval machinery each round. The method is also scaffold-agnostic, which the authors verify by porting EvoDuet onto two other evolutionary scaffolds, a top-k variant and EvoX, where it improves Sums/Diffs and Denoising. On Denoising specifically, EvoDuet surpasses the previously reported SimpleTES result at a cost reduction of about 6.9 times. And on the quantum compilation task, the best found program uses 14,835 Q20 SWAPs, 2.31 percent fewer than the released SimpleTES program, which uses 15,186.

One more mechanism detail. Candidates improve on their parent in 67.1 percent of iterations that retrieve a document the inner loop predicted would beat the parent, compared with 52.1 percent of iterations without documents. That is the inner loop's value in one number: query evolution is not just about coverage, it is about targeting. The gate's role shows up in the reverse direction as well, since forcing retrieval on tasks where the model already has sufficient knowledge tends to inject irrelevant constraints, which is part of why the oracle study shows negative deltas on a nontrivial minority of tasks.

What I would trust here. The diagnostic of why naive in-loop search stalls, high URL repetition plus a hard improvement cutoff around iteration 50, is a clean, reproducible finding, and the bilevel answer to it is a reasonable engineering response. The negative result on Qwen3.5-9B is reported with an actual inspection of 50 sampled revisions rather than a one-line dismissal, which raises the credibility of the positive results. The cross-scaffold porting is the kind of evidence that separates a method from a one-off prompt trick.

What I would re-verify before adopting this. The headline comparisons mix task sets: the oracle analysis runs on 31 tasks, the headline EvoDuet comparison on 21, and the parallel-candidate sweep on 8, so the numbers in different sections are not directly comparable, and I would want the 21-task table side by side with baselines at matched iteration budgets. The model lineup is current-generation frontier models plus one small open model, and the Qwen result says the method's value is gated on the base model's ability to use retrieved text, which is a strong caveat for anyone planning to run this on open-weight models. The retrieval predictions in the inner loop are learned from the evaluated outcomes of the same run, so the quality of the ranking depends on how well the task's evaluation signal generalizes across candidate programs, and on tasks where the evaluator is noisy, that feedback loop could be fitting to noise. The paper is a preprint, and the per-task best-score comparisons against previously reported numbers inherit all the reproducibility caveats of those prior runs.

Bottom line. EvoDuet addresses a real failure mode of search-augmented evolutionary search, and the bilevel design with a knowledge-gap gate is the right shape of fix. The gains are substantial for models that can actually use retrieved documents, and the honest negative result for Qwen3.5-9B tells you the method amplifies a base-model capability it cannot create.
