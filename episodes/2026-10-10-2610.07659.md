"DLoop: Looped Speculative Decoding," by Geonmo Gu, Byeongho Heo, and HeeJae Jun, from NAVER AI Lab and Korea University. This is an inference-time systems paper on speculative decoding, and it sits squarely in the reasoning-model topology lane because it changes how many drafting stages precede a single verification pass of the large target model. The central observation is that in current speculative decoding, full acceptance is common. The target model frequently accepts every token a draft stage proposes, which means the verification pass is a large fixed cost that is often spent to confirm a draft that was going to be accepted in full anyway. The paper's proposal is to let a single verification cover multiple drafting stages, decided dynamically during decoding, rather than paying for a verification after every stage.

The background for people not tracking the speculative-decoding literature. Speculative decoding speeds up autoregressive generation by having a small draft model propose several tokens in parallel, and then the large target model verifies all of them in a single forward pass. The target model's pass is expensive, and its latency grows only modestly with the number of tokens it checks, so if the draft is good, you get many accepted tokens per target pass. The cost of the scheme is the drafting: each drafting stage is itself a forward pass of the draft model, and for parallel drafters, the cost of that pass does not shrink if you decide to stop early. So there is a real tradeoff between drafting more tokens and verifying more often. The adaptive-length methods in the literature, the EAGLE family and its descendants, address this tradeoff, but they only work with autoregressive drafters that can stop at any token. They do not apply to parallel draft models, which produce a whole block of tokens in one pass.

The problem DLoop addresses is specific to that gap. A parallel draft model, the kind released alongside recent large models, produces a block of draft tokens in one forward pass. If you want more draft tokens, you run another forward pass, a second drafting stage, which conditions on the tokens from the first stage. But the draft model was trained in a setting where it sees target-model hidden states only for the tokens before the first drafting stage. For the tokens produced in later stages, it has no target-model hidden states, only its own hidden states, a setting it was not trained for. So naively looping the drafter degrades its quality, and that degradation is why no one had looped parallel drafters before.

DLoop has two components. The first is the confidence-gated drafting loop, which runs at inference. After each drafting stage, the loop scores the drafter's confidence in the tokens it just produced by summing their log probabilities. If the sum is above a threshold, the loop performs another drafting stage, accumulating more draft tokens. When the score drops below the threshold, the loop stops and the target model verifies all the accumulated draft tokens in a single forward pass. The acceptance rule is unchanged from standard speculative decoding, so the decoding remains lossless, the output distribution is identical to what the target model would have produced on its own. The loop repeats whole drafting stages, so it applies to both parallel and autoregressive draft models. The number of stages before a verification is decided during decoding, not fixed by a schedule.

The second component is the loop-aware training, and it is the part that makes the loop work. The additional drafting stages condition on the drafter's own hidden states for tokens that come after the first stage, which is the setting the drafter was not trained for. Loop-aware training exposes the drafter to its own hidden states for exactly those tokens, by unrolling the draft model over its own predicted features across the drafting stages of the loop. It is inspired by the training-time test in EAGLE-3, which unrolls the drafter over its own features within a single stage, and it extends that unrolling across stages. That extension is what lets the method apply to parallel draft models, where a single-stage unrolling is not enough.

The evaluation is across five draft-model families, EAGLE-3, DFlash, Domino, DSpark, and multi-token prediction modules, on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B as target models, across math, code, and chat benchmarks. In every setting, the loop and the loop-aware training together raise both the average acceptance length and the wall-clock speedup. The numbers are concrete: for the DFlash parallel drafter on Qwen3-4B, the baseline average acceptance length is six point one zero tokens with a four point one nine times speedup. With DLoop applied, the average acceptance length rises to ten point three seven and the speedup to six point four eight times. On the math benchmark specifically, DFlash goes from a five point zero two times speedup to an eight point one one times speedup, with the acceptance length rising from seven point one nine to thirteen point two seven tokens. The gains are not just from the loop at inference, which spends additional draft-model passes to reduce target-model passes, but from the training, which keeps the drafter reliable in the later stages it now actually runs.

Why the loop is a real change in the decoding structure, not just a scheduling tweak, is worth stating. In standard speculative decoding, the ratio of draft passes to target passes is fixed by the draft length, a constant set before decoding. The drafter produces a block of that length, the target verifies, and the cycle repeats. The cost structure is therefore locked in: you pay for a full target pass after a fixed number of draft passes, whether or not the draft was going to be fully accepted. The DLoop loop breaks that lock. The number of draft passes per target pass is now a random variable decided online, weighted toward the moments when the drafter is confident, which is exactly when the target is most likely to accept everything. The result is that the expensive target passes are spent less often and the cheap draft passes are spent more often, and the net effect is a lower total time per output token. The losslessness guarantee is what makes this safe: because the acceptance rule is unchanged, the loop never changes what the model outputs, only how fast it gets there.

One result worth highlighting for the systems-minded listener is the composition with tree verification. Tree verification is a separate technique that broadens the draft by branching, holding alternative tokens at the same position, while DLoop lengthens the draft by repeating stages. The paper shows they are complementary: applying DLoop on top of tree verification raises both the acceptance length and the speedup beyond either technique alone. For the Domino drafter with tree verification on Qwen3-8B, the average speedup goes from five point one three times with tree verification alone to five point five two times with DLoop added, and the math-benchmark acceptance length rises from eleven point eight six to fifteen point zero eight tokens.

The confidence-gated loop has a design detail that matters for the speedup to be real rather than just theoretical: the loop does not add a new forward pass per stage of bookkeeping. The confidence score is computed from the log probabilities of the draft tokens the drafter has already produced, so it is essentially free relative to the draft pass it follows. The only added cost of looping is the additional draft forward pass itself, and that pass is what you would have spent on the next stage anyway. The target pass, the expensive one, is the thing that gets amortized over more draft passes. That is the whole economic argument for the loop, and it is why the method is a systems result as much as a modeling one: it is a reallocation of the fixed cost of verification across a variable number of cheap draft steps.

The honest limits. The speedups are measured on specific target models and drafters, and the wall-clock numbers depend on the hardware and the batching regime, which the paper reports but which will differ on your setup. The loop-aware training requires retraining the draft model, so it is not a drop-in for a drafter you cannot retrain. And the confidence threshold is a hyperparameter that trades off loop length against the risk of a draft the target model will reject, so the reported numbers are at a tuned operating point.

My verdict is that this is a clean, well-motivated contribution to the speculative-decoding line: the observation that full acceptance is common, the confidence-gated loop that spends cheap draft passes to cut expensive target passes, and the loop-aware training that makes parallel drafters reliable when looped, together give a lossless speedup that stacks with the other techniques in the toolkit. If you are serving a model with a parallel drafter, this is worth a look.
