RiskChainBench: A Benchmark for Obfuscated Platform Message Restoration and Evidence-Grounded Web Investigation. The first three authors are ZhuoXin Liu, Zhiming Ma, and Ying Zhang, from Baidu, SmartFlowAI, and the People's Public Security University of China, with the remaining authors spanning Tsinghua University, JD Technology, and Northeastern University.

The paper addresses a failure chain that existing benchmarks measure in pieces. Platform abuse campaigns conceal redirection instructions using emojis, homophones, character decomposition, and redundant symbols, and then route users through disguised links to services associated with pornography, fraud, gambling, or illicit transactions. Prior benchmarks evaluate obfuscated text and risky webpages separately, which obscures the actual causal chain: a model that cannot restore the obfuscated message never reaches the right website, and a model that reaches the right website but cannot ground its risk judgment in on-page evidence is still useless. The benchmark couples the two stages into one pipeline and scores them separately, then composes them offline.

The construction is specific. There are 3,600 synthetic token-text restoration inputs derived from 600 source sessions, each paired with a corresponding human-labeled local web environment, so 600 websites in total, all served locally and resettable. The protocol has two tasks. In task one, a model restores the message: it must recover the original text, the operational intent, and the destination, the website the obfuscated message is trying to send the user to. In task two, the same underlying model acts as a vision-language-model-driven web agent that investigates the correctly associated website and produces a frozen risk report that cites evidence from the page, without access to message-side semantics or domain-reputation cues. The frozen report is the key design choice: the agent writes its report once, and that frozen output is what gets scored, so the evaluation is not contaminated by the model seeing its own message-side reasoning during the investigation. The two stages are then composed offline by applying the frozen primary-entry prediction as a gate: the task-two result only counts if task one routed to the correct website. Human labels determine task correctness, while a fixed multimodal evidence judge assesses faithfulness, sufficiency, completeness, and consistency of the cited evidence.

The results across ten models are where the interesting structure shows up. Entry top-1 accuracy, the fraction of sessions where the model correctly predicts the destination website, ranges from 35.2 percent to 95.2 percent across the ten models. Web decision accuracy, the fraction of investigations where the frozen risk report is correct, ranges from 26.3 percent to 62.8 percent. The leading systems differ across the four scored dimensions: entry recovery, full message reconstruction, website decisions, and fine-grained risk typing. No single model leads on everything. The entry-gated end-to-end accuracy, the composition of the two stages, shows the strongest model at 60.8 percent, with the field falling off to 16.7 percent. That gap between the best entry-recovery number and the end-to-end number is the benchmark's main message: routing and investigation are separate bottlenecks, and the multiplication of the two error rates is what determines end-to-end performance.

The failure-mode analysis is the most useful part for anyone building in this space. Execution failures account for 31.9 percent of web runs. That is not a judgment error, that is the web agent failing to operate: it does not produce a usable report at all. By contrast, post-decision type errors, where the agent reaches a decision but misclassifies the fine-grained risk category, account for only 0.9 percent of runs. The authors' reading, and I think it is correct, is that stable exploration and risk judgment are the principal bottlenecks, and that the exploration side, keeping a VLM agent functional across a local web environment without crashing the run, is currently the larger problem by an order of magnitude. A 31.9 percent execution failure rate on a local, resettable, sandboxed environment with no adversarial content beyond the obfuscation is a serious number. It means that before you can reason about the quality of a model's risk judgment, you have to get the agent loop itself to work reliably, and the benchmark gives you a way to measure those two things separately.

The methodology is worth examining because benchmark papers in this space have real failure modes of their own. The synthetic-then-human-labeled construction is a reasonable middle path: purely synthetic labels would let the model overfit to the obfuscation generator, and purely human-labeled web environments at this scale would be prohibitively expensive. The 600-source-session count is modest, but the 3,600 restoration inputs give the first stage some breadth. The frozen-report design is genuinely good, and it is the kind of choice that distinguishes a benchmark you can trust from one that quietly leaks the answer into the evaluation. The separation of correctness, decided by human labels, from evidence quality, decided by a fixed multimodal judge, is also sound, because a correct risk decision with bad citations is a different failure than a wrong decision with good citations, and conflating them hides where the model actually is.

What would I trust? The two-stage decomposition, the frozen-report protocol, and the failure-mode split between execution failures and judgment errors. Those are the contributions, and they are clean. The benchmark is also released with the protocol and the resettable sandbox, which means the numbers are reproducible, and for a benchmark that is the whole point. The finding that the leading model differs depending on which of the four dimensions you look at is a useful prior: it tells you that model selection for this kind of pipeline has to be per-stage, not a single leaderboard position.

What would I re-verify before building on it? First, the obfuscation set is synthetic and generated from 600 source sessions, so the restoration results are upper-bounded by how well the generator matches live abuse campaigns. I would want to see a held-out set of real, redacted campaign messages to confirm the transfer, and I would want to know the obfuscation taxonomies the generator covers versus the ones it does not. The 0.9 percent post-decision type error rate is strikingly low next to the 31.9 percent execution failure rate, and I would want to confirm that the risk-type labels are not too coarse to discriminate; a fine-grained taxonomy that collapses most errors into one or two categories would make the type-error number look better than it is. The multimodal evidence judge is fixed but it is still a model, and I would want to see its agreement rate with human raters on faithfulness and sufficiency, because those two dimensions are the ones most likely to diverge between an LLM judge and a human. And the 600-website local environment is a controlled setting; the execution-failure rate on a live web, with real latency, real JavaScript, and real anti-bot behavior, will be different, and probably worse, so I would not treat 31.9 percent as a floor.

One more point on the end-to-end composition. The gated composition, where task two only counts if task one is right, is the honest way to measure the full chain, and the resulting 60.8 percent best case is the number to hold this field to. It is also a number that will move quickly as web-agent reliability improves, because the execution-failure floor is the binding constraint, not the model's risk knowledge. A benchmark that measures the chain end to end, with the stages separately attributable, is more useful than either stage alone, and that is what this one does.

Bottom line: RiskChainBench is a clean two-stage benchmark whose main value is the separation of message restoration from evidence-grounded web investigation, and whose headline finding is that web-agent execution failures, at 31.9 percent of runs, dwarf judgment errors and are the real bottleneck in this pipeline. The frozen-report protocol and the released sandbox make it a credible baseline, but the synthetic obfuscation and the local web environment mean the absolute numbers should be treated as a controlled lower bound, not a field measurement.
