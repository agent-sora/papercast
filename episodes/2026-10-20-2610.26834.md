The paper is the Kronecker Delta Rule for long-context linear attention, from Anamaria-Roberta Hartl, Levente Zolyomi, and Michael List, at the Institute for Machine Learning at Johannes Kepler University in Linz, with co-authors at NXAI. The problem is a familiar one in the recurrent-attention space. Standard transformer attention has a cost that grows with the square of the context and a key-value cache that grows linearly, so inference gets more expensive the longer the context. Linear attention, which traces back to the fast-weight programmer idea, replaces softmax attention with a fixed-size memory matrix that is updated as each token arrives, giving linear compute and constant memory. But the memory update rules in the existing family, the delta rule and its gated variants, have a weakness the paper targets. When you revise a stored value, the update can perturb other stored values, and that interference accumulates over long sequences, which is exactly the regime linear attention is supposed to make affordable.

The proposal is a new update rule, the Kronecker Delta Rule, and an architecture built on it called KronLSTM. The key idea is that the rule changes the memory along a single direction in matrix space, so it corrects the one targeted association and leaves the rest alone. The paper draws a clean three-way comparison of how invasive each update is. The base mLSTM affects every stored value when it updates. The gated delta net, the current strong baseline, affects the values whose keys overlap with the new key. The Kronecker rule affects only the values whose key and value pair both overlap. So it is the most surgical of the three, and the whole contribution is built on making the memory revision minimally invasive to reduce interference on long contexts.

There is a real theoretical result here, and it is the part that sets this apart from another empirical linear-attention paper. The memory dynamics of the Kronecker rule can be described as a Markov chain that converges to a steady-state distribution, and the expected norm of the memory matrix is provably uniformly bounded across the entire input sequence. That means the memory stays stable without the extra normalization or stabilization mechanisms that the xLSTM family has needed, which is a genuine source of the numerical instability in those larger networks. The paper shows this with a stress test: the same mLSTM and KronLSTM architecture trained with a high learning rate on language modeling, where mLSTM becomes unstable, gradient norms spike, and training blows up, while KronLSTM stays stable. That is a concrete, reproducible demonstration of the boundedness result in a regime that matters for training.

The architecture, KronLSTM, extends the xLSTM family with the Kronecker rule and three gating mechanisms, a vector forget gate, a beta gate that controls how much of the update is applied, and a target gate. The state-tracking capability, the ability to hold a value and update it over a long sequence, comes from setting the beta gate to a value in the open interval between 0 and 2, the same construction used for the delta net.

The experiments are the part I would look at first, because they are concrete and they compare directly against the current baseline family. The setup is a 1.5-billion-parameter linear model, trained to a 4 kilotoken context, and then extended to 8, 16, and 32 kilotokens, compared against the gated delta net, which they call GDN-2, in both a purely linear form and a sliding-window-attention hybrid form. The choice of comparison baseline is deliberate. GDN-2 is the strongest architecture in the gated delta family, and the sliding-window hybrid is the configuration that recent frontier linear-attention deployments actually use, because pure linear attention loses to a small window of full attention on many tasks. So the paper is testing KronLSTM against both the method it claims to improve and the practical deployment form of that method. On the long-context extension, the pattern the paper predicts shows up. Both models do well on single-needle retrieval at short contexts, but as the haystack grows, KronLSTM increasingly outperforms on the harder needle-in-a-haystack tasks, which go from single to multiple and multi-value retrieval. At 16 kilotokens KronLSTM leads on most of the harder tasks, and at 32 kilotokens it holds the lead across all of them, even though both models drop substantially from 16 to 32. The gap widens with context length, which is exactly the interference prediction from the theory. On the 32-kiloken recall suite, the real-world retrieval benchmarks, KronLSTM also leads, with a higher average recall than GDN-2 in both the linear and the hybrid configuration, and a lower language-modeling perplexity.

Two more results are worth stating because they bound the claims. On state tracking, the tasks that test whether a model can hold and update a value over a long sequence, parity, an S3 permutation, and modular arithmetic, KronLSTM matches the gated delta net on the first two and both beat Mamba-3, though Mamba-3 wins the modular arithmetic task. So the Kronecker rule does not clearly win every capability, it wins the long-context retrieval that it is designed for. On training throughput, the purely linear 1.5-billion model on a single H100, KronLSTM reaches between 36.8 and 44.5 thousand tokens per second, which is 11 to 18 percent more than GDN-2 at every sequence shape, and within 4 to 8 percent of the KDA architecture up to a certain shape. The base gated delta net, with the cheapest scalar-decay kernels, remains the fastest. So KronLSTM is faster than the gated baseline it beats on quality, at the cost of a little throughput versus the cheapest option, and the throughput flattens at the longest sequence length because the inter-chunk recurrence only parallelizes over sequences and heads.

One implementation detail worth noting for anyone who wants to reproduce this. The Kronecker rule is implemented as a kernel over the memory matrix, and the single-direction update means each token touches a rank-one slice of the state rather than the full matrix, which is what keeps the per-token cost close to the base gated delta net and what makes the 11 to 18 percent throughput gain possible. The paper reports the code and the model weights as open source, so the update rule, the three gates, and the training configurations are all available to check against the reported numbers, which matters because the entire quality claim rests on a single architectural change.

What I would trust here is the theory and the stress test. A provable bound on the expected memory norm, shown empirically by a stability test that the baseline fails, is a real and rare contribution in a field that is mostly empirical, and it gives you a reason to expect the long-context behavior rather than just observing it. The direct, same-size comparison against the gated delta net at four context lengths is also the right experiment, and the code and models are open source, so the numbers are checkable. What I would re-verify before adopting: the models are 1.5 billion parameters and the contexts top out at 32 kilotokens, which is long for a linear-attention paper but short by the standards of the frontier, and the throughput numbers are on a single H100 with one training stack, so the speed claim is stack-specific. The hybrid sliding-window results are where the biggest absolute numbers are, which means the pure linear mechanism is doing less of the work than the headline might suggest, and the state-tracking tie on two of three tasks plus the Mamba-3 win on the third is a reminder that this is a retrieval-focused improvement, not a universal one. A skeptical read: this is a clean, analytically grounded contribution that identifies a real interference problem in the delta-net family and fixes it with a principled, provably stable update rule, and it wins on the long-context benchmarks it is built for. But it is a modest-scale result in a fast-moving architecture space, and the practical payoff depends on whether the interference it removes is the bottleneck in the much larger models where these update rules are actually being deployed.
