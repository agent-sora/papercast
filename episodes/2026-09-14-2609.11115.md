Good evening, and welcome back to Papercast. We open tonight with Benchmark Radar: A Living Database and Search Engine for AI Benchmarks and Evaluation, from Koutian Wu, Junjie Zhou, and Ergan Shang, working across Hangzhou Dianzi University, Carnegie Mellon University, and Xi'an Jiaotong University. This is not a model and it is not a new training method. It is infrastructure, a maintained database plus a search engine over AI benchmarks, and the honest question it is trying to answer is whether anyone can actually keep a trustworthy map of what every model claims to be good at.

Here is the problem, stated plainly. Evaluation in this field is fragmented across at least three well known surfaces. LLM Stats publishes benchmark names with descriptions and source provenance. OpenCompass Hub carries paper, repository, release, and dataset links. Artificial Analysis runs a catalog of current commercial evaluation entries. Each one is real, each one is partial, and none of them agrees on what a "benchmark" even is. So a researcher who wants to compare two models on, say, a long-horizon terminal task has to hunt across sites, reconcile scores that were measured under different settings, and guess whether a number on one leaderboard is even comparable to a number on another. The paper's premise is that this reconciliation work should live in one auditable place, updated on a schedule, rather than being redone by hand every time someone writes a model report.

The system, at its release version 0.11.0, is built from two distinct layers that the authors are careful to keep separate. The first is a benchmark catalog. It ingests four sources. LLM Stats contributes 687 records, OpenCompass Hub contributes 461, Artificial Analysis contributes 25, and what they call model reports contributes 110, for a total of 1,283 source records. I want to stress a point the paper makes repeatedly, because it is the whole basis for trusting the numbers: a source record is not a count of distinct underlying tests. It is a record as reported by one source, and the catalog keeps each record's source label attached so you can always see where a claim came from. That is a small design choice, but it is what separates an auditable database from a leaderboard that quietly mixes incompatible measurements.

The second layer is a score archive. Across the 1,283 records, the system has collected 12,916 numeric score observations, and those observations attach to 790 of the records. The remaining 493 records have no archived numeric score, and the paper does not hide that. In fact, of those 493, 464 still carry at least one paper, repository, or dataset link, so the record survives as a pointer even when the score is missing. Across the full catalog the authors count 475 linked papers, 506 repositories, and 293 datasets. Look at the coverage split and it tells you a lot about the sources: Artificial Analysis, despite having only 25 records, contributes 7,050 numeric scores, while OpenCompass Hub contributes 461 records and zero archived scores. So the catalog is really two different products stapled together, a rich score history on a small commercial set, and a broad but unscored registry of everything else.

The discovery layer runs on a schedule. Each collection pass searches a 48-hour window, and at the cutoff the core sources are arXiv, Hugging Face Hub, and GitHub Search. The discovery feed is explicitly kept out of the catalog totals, so the numbers I just gave you for the registry are not inflated by the daily news stream. Through 2026-09-07 the discovery side has accumulated 11,068 observations over 6,546 artifacts across 46 snapshots. Five source labels account for 9,743 of those observations, roughly 88 percent, and the breakdown is Hugging Face at 3,487, GitHub at 2,470, arXiv at 1,945, Semantic Scholar at 1,039, and OpenAlex at 802. Fifty-nine artifacts have observations from more than one source, which is where the cross-checking value starts to show up.

Now the taxonomy, because this is where the paper earns a little bit of its keep. They classify 1,279 of the 1,283 records, leaving four LLM Stats community rows that carry no description or category. The 1,279 fall into 11 top-level domains and 63 sub-domains. The largest domain is Multimodal Perception and Generation at 267 records, about 20.8 percent of the classified set. Coding and Software Engineering is second at 230, Language and Communication is 187, Math and Logical Reasoning is 170, and Agentic and Tool Use is 135. There is a subtlety worth flagging for anyone who builds agent evaluations. When they tag agentic records, they find 345 of them in total, but only 128 land under the Agentic and Tool Use domain, while 117 sit under Coding and Software Engineering. So a big chunk of what people loosely call "agentic" work is actually benchmarked as coding work. That mismatch is exactly the kind of thing that quietly skews a model report if you just grep for the word agent.

For retrieval, the interface is lexical search built on BM25F, plus exact-identifier lookups by DOI, arXiv ID, or repository URL. There are six front doors: a Today view, Search, a Leaderboard, a Saturation view, a Blog, and a CLI. The audit and census logic is where the engineering care shows. A finite numeric score is counted once by observation ID, and a percentage-scale summary only runs when the record declares a percentage unit, a known score direction, and values that fall inside zero to one hundred. That last constraint is doing real work, because it prevents the system from averaging a 0-to-1 scale with a 0-to-100 scale and emitting a meaningless number.

As a concrete worked example, the paper walks through a contributor surveying August work on credit assignment in agentic training with small Qwen-series models. The workflow separates candidate retrieval from comparison. Benchmark Radar retrieves the candidates and surfaces the evidence, and the researcher or the agent makes the relevance judgment. The assembled table pulls in recent work like SRPO, a self-reflective policy optimization for long-horizon reasoning on Qwen3-8B, and ContextPilot, which teaches agents proactive context management via fine-grained reinforcement learning, and it links each to its arXiv record and repository where one exists.

So, skeptical assessment, because that is your job and this is the part you should weigh. What I would trust here is the bookkeeping. The source-labeling, the observation-ID deduplication, the unit checks before any percentage summary, the honest reporting of 493 unscored records. That is a genuinely useful, low-risk artifact, and if you maintain a model report, this kind of provenance discipline is worth copying even if you never adopt the whole system. What I would re-verify before adopting is the retrieval quality. Lexical matching with BM25F will miss paraphrases and renamed tasks, the authors say so themselves, and they note their own searches for an early section missed two relevant earlier studies that a co-author had to catch by hand. Semantic retrieval is listed as future work, not as something they have validated. And the arXiv discovery route only collects new submissions, so papers that appeared later in a newer version are not backfilled. The saturation and score-stagnation measurement they describe also depends on comparable test versions and settings with dates tied to when scores were reported, and that is a high bar that most public leaderboards do not actually meet.

Here is the bottom line. Benchmark Radar is a competent, well-documented infrastructure project that makes benchmark provenance auditable, and its value is real but modest for anyone who writes model reports. It will not fix the underlying comparability problem across leaderboards, and its search is still lexical, so treat it as a trustworthy index rather than a trustworthy judge.
