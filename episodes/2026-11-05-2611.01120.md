StreamDuo, A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games, is from Zixian Gao, Penghao Luo, and Xiangcheng Cao, working with the broader team at Alaya Lab, the University of Tokyo, the Georgia Institute of Technology, Peking University, and the Institute of Science Tokyo.

Streaming video models can now commentate live gameplay, and the demo is genuinely impressive: a model watches an unbounded stream, decides every second whether to speak, and says something timely about what just happened. But there is a hidden assumption in every one of these systems: they are trained and tested on the same games. A commentator trained on Baldur's Gate 3 knows the characters of Baldur's Gate 3. The moment you point it at Tears of the Kingdom, the entities that make commentary informative, the names, the lore, the items, are exactly the ones it has never seen. This paper studies cross-game streaming commentary, where a model trained on one game has to commentate games it has never encountered, and it finds that current commentators transfer their timing but not their knowledge. They can say something at the right moment, but the content is empty or wrong.

Their solution is a two-agent system, and the design is clean. StreamDuo pairs a trainable commentator with a frozen research agent. The commentator is a normal streaming video model, the one that decides when to speak. The research agent is frozen, and its job is narrow: read the wiki of the game being played. When the commentator sees something it cannot name, it sends the current frames to the research agent, and the agent searches the wiki, picks a few relevant entries, and returns them about two seconds later while the stream keeps going. Only the commentator is trained; the research agent is an off-the-shelf retriever over a text corpus. That separation is the whole architectural point: knowledge acquisition is bolted on as a lookup, not baked into the weights.

The training is two stages, and both are aimed at the specific failure of cross-game transfer. Stage one uses information-gain labels. The commentator learns not just when to speak, but when to ask, and the label is constructed so that it asks only in the moments where an answer would actually help it say what the human caster said. This matters because an agent that asks constantly is slow and noisy, and one that never asks cannot recover the unknown entities. The label teaches it to ask at exactly the high-value moments. Stage two is on-policy self-distillation: they take a copy of the commentator that is shown the ideal answer, the ground-truth wiki entry, and they distill from that into the real commentator, which only ever sees the noisy answer that the research agent actually returns. This is the stage that teaches it to be robust to a lookup that is only sometimes right.

The results are the strongest part of the paper, because they are measured precisely on the cross-game axis. Trained on Baldur's Gate 3 and tested on ten unseen games, including Tears of the Kingdom, Elden Ring, Cyberpunk 2077, and Counter-Strike 2. Entity recall, the fraction of game entities it actually names correctly, goes from 0.082 for the baseline Proact-VL trained on the same data, up to 0.116 for StreamDuo. The absolute numbers are low, which is honest, but the relative improvement over the best single-model baseline is what matters. The second number is the one I would weight most: source-game leakage drops from 9.9 percent to 1.0 percent. Leakage is the failure mode where the commentator talks about the training game, Baldur's Gate 3, while you are playing a completely different title, and a drop from roughly one in ten clips to one in a hundred is a real, visible quality gain. It also achieves the best timing and the highest judged liveliness and overall quality among the streaming models they test.

Two results make me trust this more than a typical two-agent paper. First, the ablation where they swap the research agent at test time without retraining the commentator, replacing it with different Qwen 3 VL models from 2 billion up to 30 billion. The entity recall stays between 0.101 and 0.116 and the judge scores stay within 0.03 of each other. That is a strong robustness result: the commentator has learned to use a noisy lookup, so it does not depend on the lookup being a specific large model. A 2 billion retriever is good enough, which is a nice cost property. Second, there is a negative result on the information-gain labels: when you reverse the label, training the student to name an entity wherever the teacher assigns it some probability, the leakage rises to 19.3 percent, the worst of all the models. So the asking behavior is not cosmetic, it is doing the work of keeping the model honest about the training game.

The research agent itself is deliberately simple, and the simplicity is part of the design. Given the frames the commentator sends, it proposes a few search keys, runs a plain BM25 retrieval over the wiki, and picks the top entries to return. There is no learned retrieval model in the loop. That keeps the knowledge path auditable, you can see exactly which wiki entry the commentator used, and it makes the system portable to any game with a text wiki. The two-second latency budget is a design parameter, not a model capability, and the measured median of 0.36 seconds leaves a large margin, so the constraint is the streaming cadence, not the compute.
The efficiency numbers are also worth noting for a real deployment. On a single H100, the research agent's full answer, from proposing search keys, through a BM25 wiki search, to picking entries, takes a median of about 0.36 seconds, with a P90 of 0.64 seconds. That is well under the two-second budget, so the lookup is cheap and the latency is dominated by design, not by the model. The commentator's compute per second of video is reported separately, which is the number you would track for a real stream.

What would I trust and what would I re-verify? I trust the leakage result and the retriever-swap robustness result, because they directly test the cross-game claim and they are measured on the axis that matters, and the leakage drop in particular is the kind of number you can see with your own eyes if you watch the stream. What I would re-verify is the entity recall ceiling, because 0.116 means the model still names correctly only about one entity in eight, and the paper is candid about that but you want to know the distribution across the ten games, because a commentator that is great on one title and useless on five is not a general solution. I would also re-verify the judge scores, because liveliness and overall quality are judged by an LLM judge or human scorer, and I would want the judge's identity, the rubric, and the inter-rater variance before I relied on that ordering for model selection. The engineering gotcha is that this needs a per-game wiki corpus at deployment; the method is only as good as the wiki it can reach, and for obscure or new games the lookup returns nothing, so the system degrades gracefully to the base commentator, but the cross-game gains are exactly zero in that regime.

Two-sentence verdict. StreamDuo is a well-motivated two-agent system that fixes the cross-game knowledge gap in live game commentary by pairing a trainable commentator with a frozen wiki retriever, and it shows large, honest gains in entity recall and especially a tenfold drop in source-game leakage. I would trust the leakage and retriever-robustness results, re-verify the per-game entity-recall distribution and the judge scores, and note that the whole approach is bounded by the quality of the game wiki it can reach.
