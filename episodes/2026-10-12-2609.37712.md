PolyOCR-Venus: Unified OCR Foundation Models for Text-Centric Visual Intelligence, from the GuangJian Team at Ant Group. This is a foundation-model technical report, so it earns its place on the show regardless of upvotes. The premise is that optical character recognition is no longer about plain-text transcription. It is evolving into what the authors call text-centric visual intelligence: the ability to recognize, localize, and reason over textual information in complex real-world visual environments, from scene text to dense documents. The paper releases a family of unified OCR foundation models of varying scales, from a two-billion-parameter model up to a nine-billion-parameter one, alongside a new benchmark revision and a post-training recipe that blends reinforcement learning with on-policy distillation.
The training recipe is the technically interesting part. It is a two-stage framework. The first stage is capability acquisition through supervised fine-tuning, which establishes broad OCR capability across heterogeneous document and scene-text data, fed by a large-scale data engine that converts raw visual resources into quality-verified OCR supervision. The data side uses a three-phase quota schedule of roughly sixty million instances, twenty million per phase, where the early phase weights plain transcription and structure tasks more heavily and the reading-order share increases over time. The second stage is competence-guided refinement, and this is where the paper introduces its method, Competence-Guided Policy Optimization, or CGPO. CGPO combines verifier-based group relative policy optimization, the same GRPO family used in other verifiable OCR post-training, with adaptive on-policy distillation. The routing is sample-wise: for each training sample the system estimates a competence relationship between a teacher model and the student, using teacher reliability and the teacher-student likelihood gap, and decides whether that sample should be optimized by reinforcement or by distillation from the teacher. The ablation supports the design: plain GRPO scores eighty one point seven two on CC-OCR, and adding the fixed-weight on-policy distillation term moves it to eighty two point one eight, so the complementary supervision is doing measurable work.
The data engine deserves its own paragraph, because for a unified model the data is usually what makes or breaks the claim. The challenge is that OCR tasks are wildly heterogeneous in format. Text recognition is free-form, document parsing is layout-sensitive, key information extraction is a structured output, and table understanding needs a specific serialization. A naive mix of all of these in a single supervised fine-tuning pass tends to produce a model that is mediocre at everything. The paper's data engine addresses this by converting heterogeneous visual resources into quality-verified OCR supervision and then scheduling the training in phases. The schedule is roughly sixty million instances across three phases of about twenty million each, and the composition shifts across the phases: the early phase weights plain transcription and structural tasks more heavily to build a strong base, and the share of reading-order and reasoning-intensive tasks increases toward the later phases, so the model first learns to read reliably and then learns to reason over what it reads. This staged curriculum is the difference between a model that can transcribe and a model that can handle a long, complex document with tables and key fields in one pass. The quality-verification step, where the generated supervision is checked before it enters the training set, is what keeps the sixty-million-instance count from being sixty million of noise, which is the usual failure of large-scale synthetic OCR data.
The unified instruction-following framework is the architectural glue that makes a single model handle all these tasks, and it is worth understanding what it actually is. Rather than training separate heads for recognition, detection, and extraction, PolyOCR uses one shared MLLM backbone that takes the image and a natural-language instruction and emits the task-appropriate output, whether that is a transcription string, a list of bounding boxes in a structured format, a parsed document layout, or a key-value extraction. The instruction defines the output schema, so the same weights serve every task in the family, and the data engine's job is to teach that single interface across the full task range. The scale spread in the release is a deliberate deployment ladder. The two-billion model is aimed at latency-sensitive, high-volume transcription where most inputs are clean text and a small model is enough. The two-point-seven-billion is a middle point with more reasoning headroom. And the nine-billion is the model you reach for when the document is dense, the tables are nested, or the key information extraction is ambiguous and needs the extra capacity to disambiguate. This is the standard pattern for a foundation-model family, and the interesting question for a deployer is always where on that ladder the accuracy-per-parameter curve flattens, which is exactly the trade-off the paper's bar charts leave a little ambiguous.
The whole setup is a data-formatting problem rather than a model-capacity problem, and the pipeline is what closes it. The reward side follows the same unified logic. Because the output space is heterogeneous, the reward has to be too: text recognition is graded by normalized edit distance, which is a soft score that tolerates minor character-level variation; detection and layout are graded by IoU, which is the standard geometric overlap for boxes; key information extraction and structured tasks are graded by exact match on the extracted fields, because a partially-wrong structured answer is not partially right. The paper describes the reward design as a per-task, format-aware function, so the same model is optimized under a different evaluation criterion depending on which task its output is being scored on, which is the part that makes a unified post-training stage coherent rather than a bag of incompatible losses.
The evaluation spans five suites: their own OCRBench v2.1 revision, CC-OCR, an in-house key information extraction benchmark, OmniDocBench v1.6, and MDPBench. OCRBench v2.1 is itself a contribution: it is a revision of OCRBench v2 with manually verified annotation corrections and task-aligned scoring metrics, which matters because OCR benchmark annotation errors are a known source of inflated or misleading scores. The cross-benchmark comparison puts PolyOCR against a wide field including general-purpose frontier models like GPT-5.6-Sol, Qwen3.8-Max, and Gemini-3-Pro, and specialist OCR models like OvisOCRv2, PaddleOCR-VL-1.6, MinerU2.5-Pro, and Qianfan-OCR. On the document-heavy metrics PolyOCR-9B leads the published field: ninety six point nine on OmniDocBench v1.6, eighty two point four on CC-OCR, ninety three point six F1 on the in-house key information extraction benchmark, and eighty seven point zero on MDPBench. The two-billion and two-point-seven-billion variants track close behind on several suites, which is the usual but important claim for a family release: the small model gives you most of the capability at a fraction of the serving cost, with the nine-billion model reserved for the hardest document work.
The skeptical read. First, part of the evaluation surface is self-referential: one of the five suites is their own benchmark revision and another is an in-house key information extraction benchmark, so the headline leads on those two cannot be independently verified by a third party, and the annotation corrections in OCRBench v2.1, while valuable, change the ground truth in a way the authors control. The external suites, CC-OCR and OmniDocBench, are where the claims should stand on their own, and there the nine-billion model is genuinely at or near the top of a crowded field. Second, the competence-guided routing in CGPO introduces a teacher dependency, and the ablation only shows a fixed-weight version of the distillation term; the full adaptive routing is what ships, but the evidence for the routing logic specifically is thinner than the evidence for the two-stage recipe generally. Third, the scale spread between the two-billion and nine-billion models is presented mostly through bar charts, so the exact capability-per-parameter trade-off is hard to pin down for someone choosing a deployment size. Bottom line: PolyOCR-Venus is a solid, well-scoped foundation-model release for the document and scene-text workhorse case, with the two-stage SFT-plus-competence-guided post-training recipe and the cleaned OCRBench v2.1 being the pieces worth reusing. I would trust the CC-OCR and OmniDocBench numbers and treat the in-house and self-benchmarked leads as directional until someone else reproduces them.
