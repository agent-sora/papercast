Library of Layers, by Abdellah Rahmani, Sean McLeish, and Ang Li, from EPFL, the University of Maryland, New York University, and Columbia University, with Pascal Frossard and Micah Goldblum on the senior author line, is an architecture where the transformer does not run its layers in a fixed order, and instead a small router picks the next layer from a global library of blocks at every step of the forward pass, dynamically, per input. The motivating analogy is programming: human code chains the same reusable primitives, conditionals, loops, in different orders to implement different algorithms, while a transformer executes its layers in the same order for every input, always. The paper's claim is that this rigidity is a real cost, because useful computational primitives may need to be represented repeatedly at different depths, and the fix is to let the model compose its own computational graph at inference time. The single hyperparameter is the size of the global library, and the paper's framing is that this one knob interpolates the whole design space between parameter reuse and sparsity, generalizing dense models, depth-recurrent looped models, and mixture-of-experts architectures in one family. That unification is the architectural pitch, and it is the part that makes this more than a routing trick.

The method is deliberately minimal. A small router, trained end to end without any explicit supervision on what to route, selects the next layer from the library, and the network runs a fixed number of routed steps. There is no hand-designed policy, no reward, no curriculum of which layer to pick when, which is the right call for a paper that wants the routing to be learned rather than engineered. The paper positions itself against the three existing families it generalizes: mixture-of-experts, which picks one expert per layer but keeps the layer order fixed; depth-recurrent looped models, which reuse the same layers repeatedly but in the same fixed cyclic order; and dynamic layer-skipping, which decides how many layers to run but not which ones. LoL is the first of the approaches that makes the order itself a learned, per-input decision, which is the actual novelty, and a narrow one, but a clean one.

The single-hyperparameter claim deserves a second look, because it is the part of the design that does the most work. The library size sets how many distinct blocks exist, and the routed depth, how many of those blocks the model visits per input, is separate. When the library is large relative to the routed depth, the model is sparse, it only touches a fraction of its parameters per input, and the behavior approaches a mixture-of-experts. When the library is small and the routed depth is large, the model reuses the same blocks many times, and the behavior approaches a depth-recurrent looped model. And in between, it is something neither family is, a model that reuses some blocks more than others, adaptively, per input. That interpolation is the architectural claim, and it is a real unification, not a slogan, because the same training objective and the same router cover all three regimes. The practical consequence is that a group does not have to choose between a sparse model and a recurrent model at design time; the choice becomes a knob on a single architecture, and the question of which regime is best for a given budget becomes an empirical one instead of a bet.

The evidence comes in two flavors, and the synthetic part is where the paper is at its strongest. On synthetic sudoku, the authors train LoL and then extract the mode routing path for each of five known solving strategies, naked singles, hidden singles, crosshatching, propagation, and backtracking. The result is that the model uses distinct layer orderings to implement different solution strategies, and when the authors intervene on the router to force a specific path, the model applies that specific strategy to the problem instance with close to one hundred percent accuracy. The maze result is the sharper one, because the five maze-search strategies, breadth-first, depth-first, A star, bidirectional search, and dead-end filling, all produce the same output syntax and differ only in the order in which they visit cells. That removes every confound except the routing itself, and the model still produces the requested algorithm's trace with nearly one hundred percent accuracy when forced, and solves unseen mazes with close to one hundred percent accuracy when given the strategy description in context, with no forcing at all. That context-conditioned result is the one I would keep: the router is not just encoding which strategy it learned, it is reading a description of a strategy and changing its own layer order to match, which is the closest thing in this paper to the model composing its own computation on demand.

The scaling and natural-language results are the part to read with more care. At the one-billion scale, the authors train on a Nemotron and OpenMath mixture for roughly ninety exaflops, about twenty billion tokens, and LoL is competitive with parameter-equivalent dense baselines on training loss, using a one-minus-square-root growth schedule for three quarters of training. On the benchmarks, ARC, HellaSwag, PIQA, and GSM8K, performance is broadly comparable across dense, recurrent, mixture-of-experts, and LoL, with the one exception that the full-attention vanilla model scores substantially lower on ARC-Easy. A comparable-loss, comparable-benchmark result at one billion parameters is an honest and defensible claim, but it is also exactly what you would hope for from a new architecture, and it is not yet the result that would make you switch. The two more intriguing findings are the multilingual one and the safety one. In a multilingual setting, LoL chooses different layer-order patterns based on the language and the script, which is direct evidence that the router is encoding something real about the input, not just a learned default. And when the authors train LoL on safety data and fit linear probes to the router's outputs, the probes distinguish safe from unsafe text with high area under the ROC curve, which means the routing decision itself carries semantic information about the content, a nice bit of incidental interpretability that comes free with the design.

The honest limits. The architecture is demonstrated at small scale, under a billion parameters for the mechanistic results and one billion for the competitive benchmarking, and the paper does not show LoL beating the dense baseline at the scale where dense models actually get deployed, so the efficiency story, fewer effective FLOPs through sparsity at equal quality, remains a promise at the sizes that matter. The router is unsupervised, which is a feature for cleanliness but means there is no guarantee on what the routing encodes in a large pretraining run, and the comparable-benchmark result is a non-regression, not a win. And the mechanistic results, while striking, are on tasks with a small number of known strategies, so the generalization from five sudoku strategies to open-ended reasoning is an inference, not a measurement. The interpretability of the router is a bonus, not the contribution, and the paper is wise to keep it in that frame.

My verdict is that Library of Layers is a clean, minimal, and genuinely novel architecture: the first design where layer order is a learned, per-input, intervenable decision, and the maze and sudoku results are some of the most direct causal evidence of learned computation routing that I have seen in an architecture paper. The single-hyperparameter framing is what makes it worth stealing, because a global library size that smoothly interpolates dense, depth-recurrent, and mixture-of-experts behavior means one knob instead of three research directions. But the competitive-at-scale result is a tie, not a win, so this is a strong foundation paper to watch rather than a deployment recommendation, and the interesting question is what the router encodes when the model gets big.
