Learning Meta-Skills for Agent Harness Design in Test-Time AI4AI. The first three authors are Cheng Qian, Kunlun Zhu, and Beibin Li, working between Apodex and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, with Zhenhailong Wang and Heng Ji on the byline.

The question is test-time AI-for-AI: given a Builder model and a Target model, both with frozen weights, how can the Builder learn to construct better execution environments, or harnesses, for the Target on tasks it has not seen? The harness is the scaffolding around the target agent: what tools it gets, what context it is given, what intermediate checkpoints are enforced. The paper's idea is to make the Builder's experience reusable by distilling it into meta-skills: natural-language principles that specify when support is needed and what resources to provide. The Builder learns these principles from the Target's execution feedback on a development set, then applies the frozen skill bank to construct harnesses for unseen tasks.

The headline numbers, on Harness-Bench and NewtonBench with GPT-5.6-Sol as the Builder: the full-bank meta-skill Builder achieves a 65.31 percent macro-average score, exceeding the no-skill Builder by 8.95 percentage points and full-bank independently learned Target skills by 10.93 points. The comparison that matters most is the 12.02-point average margin over direct delivery of the same skill bank to the Target. That gap shows the value is not in the skills themselves, which the Target could just receive as context, but in the Builder learning to translate them into executable support: deciding which skills apply to which task and wiring them into the harness.

The experimental setup is specific. The Builder is GPT-5.6-Sol in all main settings. The Targets are Gemini-3.6-Flash, Qwen3.8-Flash, and GPT-OSS-120B. For each Builder, Target, and benchmark combination, the meta-skills are learned on a development split and evaluated on a fixed test split. The baselines include a no-skill Builder, independently learned structured Target skills on an equal budget, and mined free-form Target skills, so the comparison isolates the meta-skill construction step from the raw skill content.
The mechanics of a meta-skill are worth spelling out, because they are simpler than they sound. Each meta-skill is a natural-language principle with two parts: a condition, when this kind of support is needed, and a resource, what to provide. The Builder sees the Target's development executions, including the failures, and iteratively extracts principles that would have changed the outcome. At test time, for each new task, the Builder reads the task description, selects the applicable principles from the frozen bank, and assembles a harness around the Target that instantiates them: extra tool definitions, intermediate verification steps, or context injections at the points where the development executions showed the Target stalling. Nothing about the Target changes; the support is external and task-specific, which is why the same model can serve both roles and why the direct-delivery baseline is a fair comparison: in both cases the Target ultimately sees the same textual content, only in the meta-skill setting the Builder decides where and whether to apply it. The skill bank stays frozen at test time, so the cost of construction is paid once per development set, and each new task costs the Builder one selection-and-assembly pass on top of the Target's own runs.

The ablations decompose the result into useful pieces. First, refinement. The Builder can learn meta-skills in multiple passes over the development set. The first pass adds less than 1.1 points on average, but the second pass adds 13.01 points for the Gemini Target and 12.33 for Qwen. The average gain from zero to two passes is 10.42 points. Refinement is not monotone, though: on Harness-Bench, Gemini improves with each pass, while Qwen drops 4.06 points on a later pass, so more learning is not automatically better and the pass count is a hyperparameter you have to tune per Target.

Second, component ablations. Removing parts of the harness degrades all three Targets, by 13.36 points for Gemini, 4.79 for Qwen, and 1.03 for GPT-OSS, which suggests the gains are not concentrated in one harness component. Third, the self-improvement setting, where the same model serves as both Builder and Target, using Gemini-3.6-Flash and Gemini-3.1-Pro. There, meta-skills yield average gains of 18.71 points over no-skill construction and 14.14 points over direct skill delivery, larger than the cross-model gains, which the authors read as a path to system-level self-improvement: a single model learning to build better environments for itself.

The transfer section is the most honest part of the paper. They test four transfer directions: reusing the original Builder, transferring across Builders, recipient Builder learning, and transferring across both Builders and Targets. The results are mixed. The Sol-to-Qwen bank yields a 13.36-point gain when used by the original Sol Builder but a 4.45-point decline when transferred to Gemini-Pro, and on Harness-Bench the same transfer instead gives a 2.72-point gain. When a recipient Builder learns its own bank from the same development executions, the imported bank is improved by 3.69 points on Harness-Bench and 9.93 on NewtonBench, and the transferred bank scores only 0.68 points below the natively learned bank on NewtonBench. So skills transfer across Builders reasonably well, especially when the recipient does its own refinement, but frozen banks do not transfer symmetrically, and the sign of the transfer can flip between benchmarks.

The error analysis adds a mechanism. On NewtonBench, cases with no valid submission fall from 394 to 254, which is 35.5 percent of attempts, while correct discoveries rise from 439 to 535, 21.9 percent. The transitions include 145 recoveries from no valid submission to a correct law, against only 46 regressions in the other direction. The reading: the harness changes mostly close the execution gap, the agent completes the scientific loop more reliably, which then creates the opportunity for correct discovery, rather than the harness making the model's reasoning itself smarter.
What I would trust here. The experimental design is the strongest part: the direct-delivery baseline is exactly the right control, because it separates the value of the skill content from the value of the Builder learning to use it, and the 12.02-point margin over direct delivery is the paper's actual result. The transfer experiments, with their mixed and sign-flipping outcomes, are reported rather than curated, and the refinement ablation showing that the second pass does most of the work is a useful warning against assuming one-shot skill extraction.

What I would re-verify before adopting this. The main results use a single frontier Builder, GPT-5.6-Sol, and the self-improvement section uses Gemini models, so the generality of the meta-skill approach across Builder classes is under-tested. The development set is doing a lot of work: meta-skills are learned from Target execution feedback on it, and the whole pipeline inherits whatever is in that distribution, which means the frozen skill bank is as portable as the development tasks are representative of the test tasks. The transfer results say this directly, since a bank that helps one Builder hurts another. The two benchmarks, Harness-Bench and NewtonBench, cover quite different task types, harness construction versus scientific discovery, and the category-level breakdown shows the gains are concentrated, with positive point estimates for gravity and radioactive categories and mixed effects elsewhere, and GPT-OSS improves by 5.20 points overall but declines by 2.23 on vertical categories. If you are going to use this, treat the macro-average as a summary of an uneven distribution, and build the skill bank per deployment domain rather than expecting one frozen bank to carry across them.

Bottom line. The paper's claim is narrow and supported: experience with a Target agent's failures can be distilled into meta-skills that let a Builder assemble better harnesses, and that construction step is worth more than handing the same skills to the Target directly. The transfer results define the boundary of the approach, and they say the skill bank is a per-domain asset, not a universal one.
