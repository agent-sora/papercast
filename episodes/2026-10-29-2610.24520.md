HABIT-Bench, by Junming Zhang, Rubin Wei, and Xu Cheng, from the LUMIA Lab at Shanghai Jiao Tong University and the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, is a benchmark for a problem that personal agents keep failing at and mostly pretending they have solved. Users reveal their habits through their choices, and a good agent should infer those habits from scattered, weak evidence spread across many conversations, then apply them only when they actually hold. The benchmark measures exactly that, and the headline result is that the best memory method they test gets thirty three point six one percent macro accuracy across four domains, which is a bad number for a task that sounds easy.

The motivation is that habit memory is not recall. LongMemEval and LoCoMo test whether an agent can retrieve facts from a long history. PersonaMem tests whether it can track an evolving profile. But a habit is implicit. You do not say, I always order the oat milk latte, you just order it, six times, sometimes not, and the agent has to infer the default, the exceptions, when it started, and whether it has since changed. That is a different capability from retrieval, and it is the one that determines whether personalization feels right. The benchmark builds synthetic user lifelines from task-oriented dialogue data combined with controlled habit graphs, so every habit has a known ground truth, and the histories span hundreds of sessions across four domains, food, finance, software, and travel. Each probe is a four-choice question with a visibility cutoff and a target time, so the agent has to resolve what the user would do given the evidence up to a specific point, and the tasks deliberately include temporary exceptions, habits that have changed over time, and suggestions the user made but never accepted, which is the subtle case, where the right answer is not to apply a preference the user floated and dropped. The construction is what gives the benchmark its control. Each lifeline is seeded from real task-oriented dialogue prompts, so the surface language is realistic, and a latent habit graph is overlaid on top, which specifies the behavioral default, its applicability conditions, its exceptions, its temporal validity, and whether the user actually endorsed it. That separation between a realistic surface and a controlled habit structure is what lets them write down the ground truth for every probe, which is something a purely organic longitudinal dataset could not do, and it is also the main limitation, because the habit structure is designed by the authors and may be more legible than the habit structure in real user behavior, where defaults are messier and less consistent.

The evaluation protocol is clean. Methods ingest the sessions in chronological order into a per-user memory, and at each probe they retrieve under their own recorded budgets, and a shared answerer makes the four-choice prediction. They use exact-match scoring rather than an LLM judge, which removes a whole class of judge bias, and random guessing gives twenty five percent, so the numbers are anchored. They run eight memory methods with two backbones, Qwen 3 at 235 billion, active 22 billion, and GLM 5.3 Flash. The best method gets thirty three point six one percent with GLM, and the best with Qwen is in the same range. That is the gap they are documenting, between having access to the past and actually using it. The positioning against prior benchmarks is deliberate and it is a capability matrix rather than a leaderboard. LoCoMo and LongMemEval measure recall and multi-session reasoning, MemoryAgentBench adds incremental learning and selective forgetting, PrefEval looks at implicit preference inference, PersonaMem tracks evolving profiles, PERMA and DynamicMem study evolving habits, and BenchPreS and OP-Bench examine when personalization should be withheld. None of them jointly require the two things HABIT-Bench requires, namely, reconstructing a habit from weak longitudinal evidence and then deciding whether that habit applies to a new request given exceptions and changes. That conjunction is where the failure lives, and it is the reason the benchmark exists as a separate thing rather than a slice of an existing one.

The diagnostic controls are where the benchmark is interesting. They supply the annotated evidence directly to the answerer, so the model sees exactly which sessions support the habit, and accuracy only rises to between thirty nine point two six and fifty eight point two five percent across domains. They also give the model the oracle habit state, the private ground truth, and accuracy jumps to ninety eight point four one to ninety nine point two six percent. That comparison isolates the failure. It is not that the answerer cannot map evidence to the right choice, given the right evidence it is nearly perfect. It is that the memory systems cannot reconstruct the relevant habit from the history. So the bottleneck is evidence selection and habit induction, not final decision making. They quantify the evidence selection side too. For the source-attributed methods, Hit at five, whether any of the top five retrieved sessions is annotated as supporting evidence, ranges from thirty seven point seven seven to forty one point four four percent, and the recall of the annotated evidence set is only eleven point one seven to thirteen point eight nine percent. The memory systems are mostly not retrieving the sessions that matter.

The limitations are honest. The lifelines are source-grounded synthetic histories, constructed from real dialogue prompts seeded with latent habit graphs, not longitudinal records of actual users, so the distribution of habits is controlled and may be easier to pick up than the messier signal in real interaction. The tasks are four-choice, which bounds the difficulty of the final decision, and the human review covered sampled tasks rather than the full set. The four domains are narrow, and habits in food, finance, software, and travel do not exhaust what an agent would need to track. But the benchmark does what it sets out to do, it separates retrieval from induction, it gives you a number for a capability that was previously measured only indirectly, and it shows the number is low.

The human validation protocol is worth describing, because it is the part that protects the benchmark from the usual failure of synthetic evaluation, namely, the tasks being unsolvable in principle. Two human reviewers independently inspect the request, the four alternatives, and a chronological evidence packet, without seeing the gold answer or any system predictions. They select the uniquely supported action and score the item on eight criteria covering answerability, evidence sufficiency, scope, boundaries and exceptions, choice balance, naturalness, source grounding, and privacy. An item that does not have a uniquely defensible answer gets no choice rather than a forced guess, which is a good design decision, because it removes the items where the benchmark would be testing the reviewer's patience rather than the model's capability. A third reviewer adjudicates disagreements before the merged record is frozen and compared against the gold label, so the items that survive are the ones where a careful human, given the evidence, lands on the same answer the habit graph says is correct. That validation is what lets the authors claim the remaining difficulty after oracle evidence is a property of the task, not an artifact of ambiguous annotations, and it is a template for any synthetic benchmark that wants its scores to mean something.

What I would take from this is the diagnostic structure, not just the leaderboard. If you are building a personal agent, the oracle evidence and oracle state controls are a template for how you should debug your own memory, because they tell you whether your failure is in the retrieval layer or the reasoning layer, and those are very different problems with very different fixes. The thirty three point six one percent number is the part to remember. Even with frontier backbones, even with a year of controlled interaction history, the best memory systems get the habit wrong more often than not. That is a real gap between access and use, and it is a useful place to point the next round of work, because it means the next improvement in personalization probably does not come from a bigger model, it comes from a better way to turn weak, longitudinal evidence into a habit the agent can actually apply.
