Bellman Policy Optimization. The first three authors are Zhuoqing Song, Haotian Xu, and Xikun Zhang, from Apodex US, Inc., with Song also affiliated with Princeton University. The fourth author is Lidong Bing.

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards is now the standard recipe for teaching language models to reason: you sample a group of answers to each prompt, let a deterministic verifier score the final answer, and push the policy toward the high-scoring ones. The dominant implementation is GRPO and its variants, which normalize rewards within the group and apply a PPO-style clipped surrogate on token-level importance ratios. The standing complaint against this family of methods is not that it works, it does, it is that the whole apparatus is a patchwork of heuristics. The advantage estimate is a group mean and standard deviation, the clipping bounds are hand-tuned, and the importance ratio between the rollout policy and the training policy is a known source of instability whenever the two drift apart, which in practice is always, because rollout and training run on different engines in mixed precision. This paper starts from a different ancestor. Policy Mirror Descent is an older, theoretically cleaner objective that updates the policy using action values and a KL penalty, and its exact solution is a reweighting of the behavior policy by the exponential of the advantage. The problem with PMD has always been the word values: to apply it to autoregressive generation you need the value of every intermediate state, prompt plus partial response, and the obvious fix, training a separate critic model, adds memory, compute, and a second source of estimation error that prior work has shown to be inaccurate on reasoning tasks. The authors' claim is that for terminal rewards specifically, the Bellman equations let you delete the intermediate values entirely without changing the answer.

The derivation proceeds in two stages. First, they observe that for terminal rewards, the Bellman equations express each token advantage as a difference of value functions at consecutive states, and those differences telescope along the whole response: the intermediate terms cancel, leaving only the terminal reward and the initial value, which is just the expected reward for the prompt and can be estimated from the sampled group. Substituting this into the PMD optimality condition and eliminating the state-dependent partition function yields a trajectory-level objective: a squared residual comparing the terminal reward minus the estimated initial value against a sum over tokens of log probability ratios and a reverse KL term. The key theorem is that this critic-free objective and the original advantage-based PMD objective have the same unique optimal solution on all states reachable under the rollout policy. The proof of necessity is neat: the token-level residuals form a martingale under the rollout policy, its terminal value is the trajectory residual which is zero at the optimum, and because response length is bounded, the martingale must be zero at every step, forcing each token residual to vanish. Second, they approximate the objective into something you can actually run. They linearize the squared residual around the rollout policy, estimate the initial value by the group mean reward and the prompt weighting by the inverse group reward standard deviation, which together reproduce the GRPO advantage, then replace the full reverse KL with a binary KL approximation, the one from the DPPO line of work, that partitions the vocabulary into the chosen token and its complement. There is a clean identity here: the gradient of the log probability plus the binary KL collapses to a ratio of complementary probabilities. Specifically, the per-token gradient becomes the negative advantage times the ratio of one minus the rollout probability of that token to one minus the current policy's probability, times the gradient of the log probability. That ratio can blow up when the policy probability approaches one, so they add epsilon smoothing to both numerator and denominator and cap the resulting weight at C. The final loss is the GRPO loss with the importance-sampling ratio replaced by this smoothed, truncated mismatch-correction weight. Conceptually, instead of asking how much the policy has drifted from the rollout policy, it asks how much the probability mass on everything except the chosen token has drifted, which is a different and arguably better-behaved quantity.

The evaluation is a controlled one-variable comparison, which is the right design: same base model, Qwen3 30B A3B Base, same training data, the English subset of DAPO Math 17K, same batch geometry, 256 prompts per rollout batch with 16 responses per prompt split into 8 minibatches, 400 training steps, 16,384 maximum response length, and they vary only the policy loss. The competitors are GRPO ClipHigher, GSPO, CISPO, and DPPO. They score on AIME 2024, 2025, and 2026, estimating pass at one by sampling 32 responses per question and averaging correctness. BPO peaks at 50.5 percent average across the three years, against 39.5 for GRPO ClipHigher, 43.5 for GSPO, 47.4 for CISPO, and 46.4 for DPPO, so the gain over the strongest baseline is 3.1 percentage points and over GRPO is 11.0. It also wins on each individual year: 57.4 on AIME 2024, 41.0 on 2025, and 53.0 on 2026. At the end of training rather than at the best checkpoint, BPO is at 49.4 percent against DPPO's 45.5, so the lead narrows but holds. The ablations on Qwen3 4B Base show the results are not an artifact of one hyperparameter: the smoothing epsilon and the cap C are tuned at epsilon 0.1 and C 3.0, with reasonable behavior across a range.

The skeptical read. This is a clean derivation from a real theoretical object, and the equivalence theorem is the part I would trust: the critic-free objective having the same unique optimum as advantage-based PMD is a structural result, not an empirical one, and the martingale argument is sound. What I would re-verify before adopting is the practical approximation, because that is where the real engineering lives. The linearization around the rollout policy, the binary KL in place of the full KL, the group mean as the value estimate, and the complementary probability weight are all approximations stacked on top of each other, and none of them is ablated in isolation in the main paper. The 3.1 point gap over CISPO, the strongest baseline, is within the noise you would expect across seeds at this scale, and the paper does not report error bars or multiple seeds on the main table, so I would treat the absolute ranking as suggestive rather than settled. The 11 point gap over GRPO ClipHigher is more convincing, but GRPO ClipHigher is a weaker baseline than the others in this table, so it flatters the method. The setting is also narrow: one base model, one math dataset, one benchmark family, terminal rewards only. Whether the complementary probability weight behaves the same way on non-math reasoning, on agentic tasks with intermediate rewards, or on larger models is entirely open. For a team already running a GRPO-style RLVR pipeline, the change is cheap enough to test: it is a loss function swap, not an infrastructure change, and the absence of a value model is a genuine simplification. Bottom line: BPO is the most principled critic-free RLVR objective I have seen this year, derived from PMD rather than patched onto PPO, and its 50.5 percent AIME average on a 30B base model is a solid result, but the main table is single-seed and single-domain, so the 3 point edge over the best baseline is not yet a claim I would stake a production pipeline on.

One more implementation detail worth noting before the verdict, because it changes how you would roll this out. The weight in the BPO loss is stop-gradiented: the paper applies the gradient to the log probability of the token but holds the weight itself fixed, which means the mismatch correction acts as a per-token gain on the standard policy gradient rather than as a jointly optimized term. That is a deliberate stability choice, and it is why the cap C exists at all. The training curves in the paper show BPO rising fastest in the first hundred steps and holding its lead through step 400, which is the pattern you want from a method that claims better credit assignment: the gap opens early, suggesting the weight is doing something real during the regime where the rollout and training policies are most divergent, not just at the end. The practical BPO also inherits GRPO's clipping mask verbatim, with the weight playing the role the importance ratio used to play, so teams familiar with the GRPO failure modes, where tokens whose ratio drifts out of bounds get masked, get the same guardrail under a different quantity.

The honest limitations are the same as for most RLVR method papers: no code release is mentioned in the body, the comparison is confined to one model scale where a 30B base model with three billion active parameters sits in a specific spot on the compute curve, and the dataset, DAPO Math 17K, is a curated math set whose difficulty distribution may not match what your production workload looks like. If the method is as good as the theory says, the gains should widen on larger models, because the rollout-training mismatch gets worse at scale, but that is exactly the regime the paper does not test.
