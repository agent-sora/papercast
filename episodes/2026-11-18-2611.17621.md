Today's paper is AscendGYM: A Cost-Aware Kernel Optimization Benchmark for Real-World Workloads on Ascend NPUs, from the AI Training Platform Team at the Shenzhen Loop Area Institute, with Bingwei Zhang and Jing Li among the listed authors. The task it defines is narrower than it sounds, and that is the point. Instead of asking a coding model to generate a kernel from a PyTorch spec, AscendGYM hands the model a correct, working kernel implementation and asks: can you make it faster for this specific workload, within a token budget, without breaking correctness? The initial suite has 48 kernels across three difficulty levels, and every kernel's test cases use data types and tensor shapes collected from real training and inference runs, so a speedup on the benchmark maps directly onto a workload someone actually runs. The design question behind that is that optimization priorities for the same kernel vary with the input configuration: an attention kernel processes different data sizes during prefill and during decoding, and those regimes favor different ways of organizing the computation. A benchmark built from synthetic shapes cannot see that variation, which is why the authors anchor every task to a real workload trace, in the spirit of FlashInfer-Bench, which draws workloads from real inference traces.

The motivation is the gap between existing Ascend benchmarks and what kernel engineers actually do. KernelBench evaluates generation from a spec. NPUKernelBench and CANN Bench extend that to Ascend, checking compilation, correctness, and performance. All of those start from the spec. Real optimization starts from existing code, analyzes where the time goes on a particular hardware generation and input configuration, and iterates. That workflow depends on specialized expertise and sustained effort, and the benchmark authors argue it is the more automatable, more valuable task. AscendGYM reproduces the execution environment of how Ascend kernels are actually invoked in practical pipelines, which is a meaningful difference from running a kernel in isolation with synthetic shapes. The evaluation also reports correctness as a first-class dimension: a candidate implementation only counts toward the speedup metrics if it passes validation on the task's test cases, so the benchmark distinguishes the gain achieved from the resources invested from whether the candidate was even correct, and a kernel that is faster but wrong contributes nothing.

There are two evaluation modes, and the distinction matters if you are choosing what to report. Model evaluation uses a built-in harness that provides search orchestration and experience accumulation on top of the raw model, so you are measuring the model with a standard scaffolding around it. Model-harness evaluation treats the model and your custom agent harness as a combined system, which is how you would actually deploy an optimization agent. The paper's main results are on the 17 tasks in Batch 1 of the 48-kernel suite, evaluated across a grid of six models and three popular agent harnesses: Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode, with models including Opus 5, GPT-5.6, GLM-5.3, Qwen at 122B and 35B, and DeepSeek V4 Flash. The 17-task Batch 1 is the slice that has results in the paper; the full 48-kernel suite is staged across difficulty levels, which matters when you compare per-task numbers, because a 17-task slice is not the same distribution as the 48-kernel suite.

Now the metric, which is where the cost-aware framing lives. Headline number is the geometric mean speedup, and the finding you should remember is this: approximately 70 percent of the evaluated model-harness combinations achieve a geometric mean speedup below 1.2x. That is the sobering result. The gains that do appear are concentrated in modest improvements with limited task coverage at higher speedup thresholds. The second metric is the Speedup-Token Score, STS, which combines performance gains achieved within a given token budget with the cumulative token consumption at each improvement, and it is computed under three explicit budgets: 10 million tokens, tight; 20 million, moderate; and 50 million, generous. STS is zero if no improvement is achieved at all. The third is Cost-to-Gain, CTG, which measures the additional tokens consumed to push speedup past a threshold relative to a reference checkpoint, with thresholds at 1, 5, and 10 percent.

The budget sweep is where STS earns its keep. At the 50 million token budget and a 10 percent gain target, Opus 5 with OpenCode has a median cost of 3.8 million tokens over the tasks it attains, versus 6.6 million for Opus 5 with Claude Code. But, and this is the nuance the paper flags explicitly, the OpenCode configuration attains the target on only a subset of tasks while Claude Code covers all of them. The lower conditional median does not establish greater efficiency on the same tasks. That is a genuinely careful result, and it is the reason the authors report CTG together with attainment counts rather than cost among successful tasks only. If you are going to compare optimization agents, that coverage-versus-cost distinction is the one that survives contact with real workloads.

On the gain side, the geometric mean speedup analysis is equally blunt. Two combinations, Claude Code with Opus 5 and Codex with GPT-5.6, accelerate every task in the batch. But the number of tasks they push past 2x speedup differs sharply: Claude Code with Opus 5 clears 2x on seven tasks, Codex with GPT-5.6 on three. So even the strongest system's big wins are a minority of the suite, and the rest of the improvement mass is the sub-1.2x long tail that the 70 percent headline captures. That is a useful prior to carry into your own planning: if you are budgeting for an LLM-driven kernel optimization pass on Ascend workloads, model a high probability of a small win and a low probability of a large one, rather than the other way around. They also report fastp, the fraction of tasks above a given speedup threshold, alongside the geometric mean, because identical coverage of accelerated tasks does not imply identical improvement magnitudes. At the +10 percent threshold, attainment is thin across the board, with the best configurations reaching only about half to two-thirds of tasks.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the central negative finding, that 70 percent of combinations stay below 1.2x, because it is consistent with every kernel-benchmark result I have seen and because the paper is honest about the initial implementations being already-decent baselines: the suite ships with reference implementations that are not naive, so a sub-1.2x result is not an artifact of a weak starting point. I would re-verify three things before using this benchmark in your own evaluations. First, the suite is Ascend-specific, so results do not transfer to CUDA kernel work; if your hardware is NVIDIA, this is a design reference, not a leaderboard. Second, 48 kernels across three difficulty levels is a small benchmark, and the main numbers are on a 17-task Batch 1, so the per-cell numbers in the grid have wide confidence intervals; treat specific model rankings within a harness as suggestive, not stable. Third, the harnesses themselves are versioned third-party agents, and the metric is sensitive to harness behavior, so a model's STS score is really a score for the model-harness pair as configured on the release date. The repository is public, with the 48-kernel suite and both evaluation modes, which makes it easy to re-run a single cell of the grid on your own workloads before believing any aggregate.

Bottom line: AscendGYM is a well-scoped benchmark for the actually-important task of optimizing existing kernels under budget constraints, and its headline result is that current frontier models still deliver mostly modest gains on real workloads. Use its metrics design, STS and CTG, as a template for any cost-aware agent evaluation you build, and expect the absolute speedups to stay small until kernel-optimization models get better.
