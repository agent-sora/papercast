CoworkOS, Scaling Agent Coworkers for Real-world Automation, from Renmin University of China, Fudan University, and DeepSeek. The first three authors are Jiajie Jin, Guanting Dong, and Chengjun Pan, out of the RUC NLPIR group, and there are forty authors total. This is the top-upvoted paper of the day by a wide margin, and it is worth asking what exactly it is. It is not a new model. It is an agent harness, a runtime system that wraps a frontier model in the machinery for long-horizon work: state management, context management, task queues, subagent delegation, and acceptance criteria. The interesting claim is that the harness is doing a lot of the work, and the paper tries to prove that by training the wrapped model through recursive self-improvement and watching the benchmark scores climb.

The framing of the paper is the cowork setting. They describe three stages of agent system evolution. The first is Agent Workflow, where the contribution is reasoning and tool trajectories: chain-of-thought, tree-of-thought, ReAct, Reflexion, and the workflow frameworks like DSPy, AFlow, and MetaGPT. The second is Agent Assistant, where the agent has persistent memory, messaging, and code execution across continuing interactions, the territory of LangGraph, AutoGen, and OpenClaw. The third, which this paper addresses, is Agent Cowork, where agents sit inside professional work: humans and agents contribute to evolving artifacts, adjust plans as the work develops, and share initiative. The paper names the systems that have proven the earlier stages: coding and work-environment agents like Claude Code, Codex, OpenHands, and DeepSeek Harness for real-world productivity, and cowork agents such as Kimi Code, ZCode, Qoder, and Hermes Agent as the emerging category. The reading they offer is that these stages are an expanding design lens, moving from automation by the agent itself toward sustained interactive work involving humans, agents, and the environment all at once. The paper identifies two requirements for this setting. The first is complex productivity tasks, producing software, documents, designs, and other artifacts that demand reasoning, tool use, and verification. The second is continued monitoring and decision-making: monitoring a training run over days, following a project across sessions, watching external information and deciding when to act. The design question is whether a single cowork agent can handle both while keeping humans in the loop.

The architecture is three layers. The Main Agent is the point of accountability to the user: it interprets instructions, sets the overall plan and execution direction, and holds the full task context. The Coordinator Runtime is the harness proper: it maintains state, manages the inbox where outcomes durably queue, routes work at safe model decision boundaries, and handles cancellation. Subagents are optional workers that the Main Agent can delegate to, synchronously or asynchronously. The division of responsibility is explicit: the model decides actions, delegation, and acceptance, while the runtime maintains state and feedback. That separation is the whole thesis. The model is the strategist, the harness is the operating system, and the two are supposed to co-evolve.

Where the paper gets concrete is the case studies. They produce twenty-four application cases across six categories: frontend websites, games, Office presentations, interactive visualizations, CAD, and Blender rendering. The cases span both halves of the design vision: challenging productivity artifacts that require planning, tool use, and verification, and sustained monitoring work where the agent has to keep its context healthy across sessions and decide when an external event is worth acting on. The montage in the paper shows two views of each case, different pages, scenes, or inspection states, with the harness comparisons and detailed inspection evidence collected in the appendix. That is the long-horizon productivity claim made tangible, and it is worth noting that the artifacts are real deliverables, not toy demos. The harness and the case corpus are public, with the repository under the RUC-NLPIR group and a project page hosting the demo gallery, so the claims are inspectable. But the headline experiment is the recursive self-improvement loop, RSI. In the sustained-work case study, CoworkOS uses GLM-5.3 as the backbone to conduct automated post-training on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, a mixture-of-experts model with thirty-five billion parameters, three billion active. The harness, in other words, is put in charge of training a model. Over five rounds, the model's average score across eight benchmarks rose from thirty-three point one nine to forty-two point nine five, a gain of nine point seven six points.

Let me unpack the per-benchmark numbers, because they are uneven. The eight benchmarks are the standard agent and tool-use gauges: AutomationBench, BFCL V4, OfficeQA Pro, SkillsBench, Tau-3 Bench Banking, Harvey LAB, and the two others in the appendix. AutomationBench goes from eleven point zero zero to forty-four point one five, a gain of thirty-three point one five points. That is the largest single improvement and it is the benchmark most aligned with the harness's own domain, so I would discount it a bit as self-referential. BFCL V4, the function-calling benchmark, climbs from around fifty-eight to seventy-four, a gain of seven point three five points. OfficeQA Pro moves from fifty to sixty-five, plus six point four seven. SkillsBench goes from around fifteen to forty, plus fourteen point nine one. Tau-3 Bench Banking goes from fourteen point four three to twenty-two point six eight, plus eight point two five. Harvey LAB, a legal work benchmark, goes from three point three three to eleven point zero eight, plus seven point seven five. The round-by-round average is not monotonic, which is a detail the paper shows honestly: thirty-three point one nine at round zero, thirty-six point nine four, then a dip to thirty-five point nine zero, then thirty-nine point two two, forty point seven one, and forty-two point nine five. Round two regresses. That is what recursive self-training actually looks like, and it is worth noting that the trajectory is noisy, not a clean climb.

The skeptical read. The first question is how much of that gain is the harness versus the training signal. The RSI loop trains Qwen3.6-35B-A3B using experience generated under CoworkOS, so the harness is both the student's gym and, arguably, a source of the curriculum. A harness that queues work, retries, and delegates is also a harness that generates more training rollouts per unit of wall time. Part of the nine point seven six average gain may simply be more and better-organized post-training compute, not a new capability. The paper does not include an ablation where the same RSI budget runs without the harness, so that question is open. The second question is generalization. Eight benchmarks, five rounds, one backbone pair. The self-referential benchmark, AutomationBench, dominates the headline delta. And the round-two dip shows the loop is not yet stable in the way the word recursive implies. The honest summary is that this is a strong systems paper with an encouraging, but under-controlled, training result attached to it.

What is genuinely useful if you want to implement or test the ideas. The three-layer split, Main, Coordinator, Subagents, with durable outcome queueing at safe decision boundaries, is a clean reference architecture for anyone building a long-horizon agent runtime. The inbox-plus-routing design, where subagent outcomes queue durably and the main agent incorporates them only at decision boundaries, is a concrete answer to the context-rot problem in multi-session agents. And the RSI loop, harness-guided automated post-training, is a template worth replicating in your own stack: take a working agent harness, let it generate post-training data and orchestrate the training rounds, and measure whether the average across a fixed benchmark panel moves. If it does not move, or if it moves only on the harness-adjacent benchmark, you have your answer about how much of the gain was plumbing.

The verdict. CoworkOS is a credible, well-engineered harness for long-horizon cowork agents, and the three-layer architecture with durable state management is a useful reference design. The recursive self-improvement result is the paper's most interesting claim and also its least controlled: a nine point seven six average gain across five rounds is real but noisy, and the largest single-benchmark delta is the one most likely to measure the harness measuring itself.
