The paper is Spectral-Spatial Interpretation, or SSI, from Haotian Ma, Ruqi Yang, and Philip Townsend at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, with Yang in Computer Sciences and Townsend in Forest and Wildlife Ecology, which is a hint at where the evaluation data comes from. The problem is post-hoc explanation of vision models on multispectral data, where the input has multiple physically meaningful channels, like the near-infrared and short-wave infrared bands on a Sentinel-2 satellite scene, or the red, green, and blue channels on a fine-grained bird photo. Existing explanations answer one of two questions. Spatial attribution, Grad-CAM and its relatives, tells you where the model looks, but it aggregates over channels, so you lose which band was doing the work. Spectral attribution, global channel rankings built from mutual information or attention weights, tells you which channels matter on average, but it averages across space. The paper's point is that both collapse real structure, because channel importance is spatially localized. Near-infrared matters at a vegetation boundary and means nothing over water; the same band is decisive in one region and irrelevant in another. Even a two-stage pipeline that multiplies a spatial heatmap by a global channel vector assumes a rank-one structure, meaning the same channel profile everywhere, which is exactly the assumption that fails on multispectral scenes.

SSI fixes this by treating the attribution target as a joint score for every position-channel pair. For each spatial position, it freezes the rest of the image at its observed values, then defines a small game whose players are only the channels at that one position, and applies the standard Shapley value to that local game. The result is a full attribution matrix, one number per position and channel, from which you can recover a spatial map by summing over channels, showing where the evidence lies, and a spectral profile by summing over positions, showing which channels contributed, without ever assuming the rank-one factorization. The theory is modest but clean. Because the Shapley allocation runs within each position, it satisfies the standard fairness properties locally, and the channel attributions at a position, summed together, exactly recover the contextual contrast you would get by replacing that whole position with the baseline. That aggregation consistency is the property that makes the full matrix trustworthy rather than a collection of inconsistent local scores. It also deliberately differs from the Owen value, a hierarchical Shapley variant that uses the same position-channel grouping but averages over external spatial coalitions; SSI targets localized spectral evidence by solving the small per-position games instead.

A few implementation details matter for reuse. The two tasks the score supports are deletion and insertion. Deletion asks what happens when you remove the evidence a position-channel pair provides, replacing it with the baseline; insertion asks the mirror question, what does adding that pair's contribution on top of the baseline do. The joint area under the curve over both tasks is the headline metric, because a method can look good at deletion by finding regions where the model is fragile, and only the pair of curves together tells you the attribution is causally real rather than an artifact of where the model is sensitive to perturbation. The per-position games have a second consequence that the evaluation exploits: because each game involves only the channels at one position, typically a handful of players, the Shapley estimate for each cell is cheap to sample to high precision, and the paper reports that at its total budget the per-cell estimates carry uniform errors below one one-hundredth. That precision is what lets the budget comparison be fair. A permutation-based hierarchical estimator has to sample over external spatial coalitions, and its variance grows with the number of blocks; SSI never samples those coalitions at all. On the burn-scar data the difference is concrete: the competing estimator with two hundred block-respecting permutations needs up to seventy-six thousand eight hundred model evaluations, while SSI's budget tops out at four thousand four hundred eighty, because the work decomposes into many tiny independent games rather than one large coupled one. The choice of baseline is the other knob, and they run both a per-channel mean baseline and a zero baseline; the results hold under both, which matters because on remote-sensing data a zero baseline is an unphysical scene and a per-channel mean baseline is a spatially uniform scene, and a method whose rankings flip between the two is telling you about the baseline, not the model.

The evaluation also runs against a set of gradient-based baselines on the burn-scar data, where SSI performs best on all ten reported metrics, covering joint and rank-one deletion, channel deletion, grid-sum insertion, spatial localization, and spectral hit rates, which matters because gradient-based methods are the cheap incumbent in any attribution toolchain and a method that does not beat them is not worth the sampling overhead. The burn-scar case study is where the method's motivation is easiest to see. On a Sentinel-2 scene, the near-infrared and short-wave infrared bands carry the burn signature, but only in the regions where vegetation existed before the fire; over water or bare ground those same bands are uninformative. A global channel ranking would still rank the near-infrared band first overall, which is technically true and operationally useless, because it does not say where to look. SSI's attribution matrix does say where to look: the channel-aggregated spatial map concentrates on the burn boundary, and the per-channel views show which band is doing the work at each position. The paper also points to imaging spectroscopy sensors like EnMAP, where the number of spectral channels is large enough that the local-game structure, and the resulting evaluation-budget savings, become a practical concern rather than a theoretical one, and the qualitative examples on CUB-200 show the same pattern at the other extreme, a single fine-grained visual feature, like a bird's bill color, supported by a specific channel in a specific region, that a channel-averaged heatmap would blur into the surrounding pixels.

The evaluation runs on CUB-200 for fine-grained birds, EuroSAT for land cover, and HLS Burn Scar for burn-scar mapping, under both the per-channel mean and the zero baseline. SSI achieves the best joint deletion and insertion area under the curve over dense, flat, and rank-one baselines on all three datasets, the strongest spectral hit rates and channel-deletion scores, and the best spatial localization on the two annotated datasets. The burn-scar result is the striking one: SSI beats MC-Owen on every retained metric while spending a fraction of the compute, and the paper attributes the extra gain to both the observed-context modeling choice and the smaller games avoiding the external-coalition sampling variance that permutation-based Owen estimators carry.

The skeptical part. This is a method paper on a niche, and the niche is real but small: multispectral and channel-rich vision is a specific application area, and the contribution generalizes to RGB in name more than in value, where three channels make the local games trivial. The Shapley framing is doing less work than it appears to, because the allocation rule is standard; the actual contribution is the choice of game, fixing observed context and restricting players to the channels at one position, and a reader should judge the paper on that modeling decision rather than on the fairness properties, which follow from the definition. The baselines are reasonable but include no learned attribution method, and the gradient-based methods in the appendix are the kind of cheap comparisons that flatter any careful method. The six-percent budget claim is dataset-specific, tied to the block structure of the burn-scar evaluation, and the per-dataset AUC differences, while consistent in direction, are the numbers to scrutinize if you are deciding whether the method is worth the implementation cost for your own data.

The verdict: this is a tidy, well-grounded attribution method for multispectral vision, and its real value is the observed-context position-channel game, which gives you a local interpretation and exact aggregation consistency that global channel rankings and rank-one pipelines cannot. If you work on remote sensing or any channel-rich imaging, SSI is a strong default explanation method, and the budget argument means it is cheap enough to run as a sanity check on every model; if your data is plain RGB, the gain over standard spatial attribution will be small.
