R2Orch, short for Recursive Self-Play Orchestration, by Mingda Zhang, Yun Huang, and Qiang Huang, at The Chinese University of Hong Kong in Shenzhen, Fudan University, and the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, with co-authors at Tsinghua and the National University of Singapore, is a training system for agent orchestrators that ends with a strange property: the final model is deployed with zero skills in its context. The paper's claim is that you can train a Director, a policy that edits a graph of sub-agents, through self-play on tasks a Proposer model invents, and then systematically withdraw the in-prompt skills the Director relied on during training, one at a time, each time proving with a controlled paired evaluation that the Director performs just as well without that skill. The end state is the same Director, the same task distribution, but a scaffold that has shrunk to nothing, and the authors report that it wins on all twelve of their benchmarks in that zero-skill state.

The problem setup is specific. An agent graph is a directed graph where each node is an agent with a role prompt and a model route, each edge is a hand-off, and one designated output node's answer is returned. The Director is trained by reinforcement learning to produce a sequence of atomic edits, add an agent, set a prompt, set a model route, consider a relation, delete an agent, set the output node, and finish, building that graph from empty. Frozen Worker models then execute the graph on a task, and a verifier scores the result. The training target is written down explicitly: maximize the verifier's correctness over tasks drawn from a fixed distribution, with the skill context set to empty. Everything in the method is a way of approaching that target with a scaffold that gets progressively removed.

The mechanism is two nested loops sharing one randomized rollout group. In the inner loop, the Proposer picks a task, and the Director, with some retrieved skills in context, generates a group of rollout trajectories. The group is split: a majority, the hidden part, reads the context with one candidate skill removed, and a minority, the visible part, reads the full context. The Director is updated with a group relative policy optimization objective, and because the context is randomized per rollout, most of each update is already training the Director without the candidate skill. In the outer loop, that same paired structure is a controlled experiment: the difference between the visible and hidden success rates is the skill's measured benefit, accumulated into a per-skill ledger, and when the Bayesian posterior over that benefit says the skill's remaining contribution is at or below a tolerance, the skill is withdrawn from the scaffold. New skills are admitted from distilled failures, with an admission rule that is the mirror image of withdrawal, and the cycle repeats: failures become skills, skills get internalized into the Director's parameters, and each withdrawal exposes new failures that steer the Proposer to new tasks.

The second key piece is the Proposer's reward, called Recursive Learnability, which is where the advantage-collapse problem is handled. Group relative policy optimization gives zero advantage to any task whose rollouts all reach the same outcome, and the paper's observation is that a skill scaffold makes this worse: if self-play keeps tasks at the frontier measured with the skills in context, then withdrawing a skill pushes the tasks it supported toward all-wrong rollouts, and the advantage collapses. So the Proposer is rewarded not for task difficulty today, but for tasks that stay learnable tomorrow, specifically tasks whose hidden-part rollouts keep reaching different outcomes once the candidate skill is gone. The learnability of a task is scored by a function of its hidden-part success rate that peaks at a success rate of one half, the maximum-variance point, and the Proposer is updated by a proximal clipped step against a reference distribution, with the reward computed on the hidden context, i.e. on the next Director rather than the current one. The authors prove this is an unbiased estimator of the gradient of the expected reward under their distinct-pair sampler, and they note the subtlety that the raw pair-dependent estimator and the independent-pick estimator have different stationary points, so the regularized objective, not the raw gradient, is what the practical update tracks.

The accounting result is the paper's cleanest theorem. They decompose the zero-skill success into the starting success, plus the sum over rounds of the inner gain, what self-play adds on a fixed scaffold, minus the outer cost, what each scaffold change takes away, and they show self-play tracks each scaffold's equilibrium under a two-time-scale stochastic approximation argument with the usual assumptions: decreasing step sizes, a Lyapunov-stable equilibrium per scaffold mode, and mode changes that eventually slow down. The withdrawal decision is bounded by the expected positive part of the posterior effect, so the outer cost is controlled, and they carry a drift term for when the Director itself keeps changing under a fixed evidence window of thirty cycles. The practical rules are concrete: a minimum record count before a skill is even eligible for a withdrawal decision, withdrawal taking precedence over admission when the two overlap, and skill versioning by content hash so evidence never pools across edits to the same skill.

On the numbers. They train on Qwen3.5-9B as the main backbone and run five seeds per method, with twelve benchmarks split into six in-distribution, HotpotQA, Natural Questions Open, MedQA, AIME 2026, MBPP Plus, and ALFWorld, and six held out of distribution, TriviaQA, MuSiQue, GPQA, MATH-Hard, SWE-Bench, and WebShop. The in-distribution average, with zero skills in context at test time, is 89.79 percent for R2Orch against 86.00 for the best skill-internalization baseline PATS, 77.87 for AgentFlow, 82.34 for FlowSteer, 61.04 for plain group relative policy optimization on the same backbone, and 61.35 for the untrained backbone. The largest margin over the best skill-internalization baseline is on ALFWorld, 81.33 against 74.00 for PATS, a seven-point gap, and the paper reports its lead is largest on AIME 2026, WebShop, and ALFWorld. The ablations are the part worth reading: removing the whole withdrawal loop, keeping the skills fixed, makes the Director trail on every in-distribution benchmark, which is the direct evidence that the internalization, not the self-play, is doing the work, and removing the Recursive Learnability objective drops the Proposer to choosing solved or collapsed tasks. They also report that the trained Director improves six other worker backbones without any retraining of those workers, which is the strongest generalization claim in the paper.

The skeptical read. The initial skills are hand-designed per dataset, and the paper says plainly that deriving them automatically for a new domain is open, so the system's cold start is still a human bottleneck, and the zero-skill end state inherits whatever biases the hand-crafted scaffold introduced during training. The whole paired-contrast evaluation is clever, but it measures the skill's benefit on the Proposer's current task distribution, which is itself moving every round, and their own drift term acknowledges the evidence goes stale; the thirty-cycle window is a hyperparameter that trades stability against freshness, and nothing in the paper says how sensitive the withdrawal decisions are to it. The out-of-distribution benchmarks are held out from training but they are still standard public sets, so the claim that the Director generalizes is a claim about these twelve tasks, and the SWE-Bench and WebShop entries are the only genuinely open-ended ones. The two-time-scale convergence argument is standard machinery, but the assumptions, Lyapunov-stable equilibria for each scaffold mode with mode changes that slow down, are exactly the assumptions you cannot verify empirically in a run, so the theorem is a justification story rather than a guarantee about the actual training loop.

My verdict is that this is a genuinely well-constructed self-improvement loop, and the paired-contrast skill withdrawal, doing a controlled A/B evaluation inside the rollout group at no extra rollouts, is the reusable idea, with the zero-skill end state as a clean and testable definition of what internalization means. The caveats are that the cold start is hand-crafted, the evidence window is a moving target, and the convergence result is conditional in the usual way, but the twelve-benchmark results, and especially the finding that the scaffold matters more than the self-play, make this one of the more honest self-improvement papers of the cycle.
