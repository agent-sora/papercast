## Cold Open

Today we're looking at ClawGym II: Exploring Black-Box RL on Agent Harness, by Huatong Song, Fei Bai, and Ming Yang, with seventeen more colleagues spread across the Gaoling School of Artificial Intelligence at Renmin University of China and a lab called IQuest Research.

Here's the uncomfortable premise. The agents everyone actually deploys are not naked language models. They're models wrapped in a harness: a planning loop, tool invocations, memory reads and writes, retry logic, maybe a sub-agent or two. And yet almost all reinforcement learning for agents is done on the naked model, on synthetic traces, with the harness treated as something that happens later, at deployment, to someone else. This paper declines that arrangement. It trains the full stack, model plus harness, as one unit, using reinforcement learning that never opens either of them up. The harness stays a black box. The model stays a black box. All the learning signal comes from the outside: did the finished trajectory complete the task, yes or no.

That refusal raises the obvious question, which is also why the paper exists: how do you make reinforcement learning work at all when your policy is a sprawling scaffold that takes hundreds of steps per episode?

## Why Harness RL Is Hard

Three reasons, and the paper is upfront about all of them. First, episodes are enormous. A single rollout through a real harness on a real task can run for hundreds of turns, and because the whole running conversation ships back to the model on every one of them, total token spend grows much faster than the episode does. Second, the environments are live software: browsers, terminals, office suites. They are slow, stateful, and they break. Third, there are no gradients. A white-box setup can shape rewards mid-episode and backpropagate through the policy. Here, the entire harness-to-model conversation is opaque; you get one scalar at the end and you'd better learn from it. There is also nowhere natural to hang intermediate rewards, because inside a scaffold, mid-episode events do not map cleanly onto progress, which is why the paper commits to terminal outcomes and eats the credit assignment problem whole.

Underneath all three sits the quiet motivation: deployment parity. Fine-tune a naked model on synthetic traces, then bolt on a harness at deploy time, and the thing you optimized is not the thing your users run. Training through the harness closes that gap by construction; whatever gets better is literally the shipped system.

The team's answer is less an algorithm than an infrastructure discipline, and honestly, that remains the paper's real contribution.

## The Machinery

Rollouts run inside isolated sandboxes, so thousands of episodes proceed in parallel and a crashed environment takes down one episode, not the fleet, while a poisoned rollout becomes a discarded sample rather than a corrupted batch. The harness is never modified. Instead, its API endpoint points at a proxy that records every model request and every tool response, so complete trajectories are reconstructed from outside, exactly as the harness emitted them. Nothing inside the harness is patched, versioned, or forked for training's sake, so the day the upstream tool updates, the pipeline survives unchanged.

The economics come from a prefix-tree reconstruction scheme. Rollouts share gigantic prefixes, since the same system prompts, tool definitions, and early steps recur across episodes, and the tree stores each shared segment once, which removes most of the redundant storage and replay cost from the pipeline. Alongside that sits a token accounting rule the authors call token-in-token-out: every segment is counted twice, once as the harness composed it and once as the model actually received it, and the two views must reconcile. Get this wrong and every downstream analysis quietly lies to you, because retries, truncations, and caching layers all drift the two ledgers apart.

Two statistical safeguards matter more. Because rollouts arrive from older checkpoints and occasionally earlier harness builds, updates carry an importance-sampling correction instead of pretending off-policy data is on-policy, a correction that earns its keep the moment batches start mixing checkpoints and harness builds. And advantage estimation uses mix-harness grouping: trajectories from different harnesses are grouped together, so the optimizer cannot quietly specialize for the quirks of one scaffold. That last choice anticipates the paper's most interesting failure finding, which we'll get to.

## The Numbers

The base model is Qwen3-30B-A3B, a mixture-of-experts design with thirty billion total parameters and roughly three billion active per token, trained inside two established open coding-agent harnesses, OpenClaw and Claude Code. On ClawGym-Bench, Pass@1 rises by 9.98 points in one harness and 14.81 in the other. On PinchBench, an external suite the model never trained on, the gains are 11.71 and 17.28. That averages out to ten to fifteen points of Pass@1 across setups, a striking exchange rate when you remember the entire reward is one bit per finished episode. The training curves hold steady out to 200 to 400 steps, which in this regime counts as boring in the best way: no late collapse, no reward hacking spiral, and no entropy crash forcing an early stop.

Transfer is where I perk up. JobBench-Easy goes from 20.46 to 27.20, about seven points stacked on an already-running base. OfficeQA-Full goes from 8.53 to 21.54, better than doubled, on tasks the training never saw, in a task family about office documents rather than code. Whatever the harness learned, some of it generalized past its own benchmark and past its own modality.

## Where the Skepticism Lands

Start with the ablation the paper handles most candidly. Comparing PPO and GRPO under this setup, PPO's entropy falls off a cliff early while GRPO keeps exploring far longer. But the authors flag the confound themselves: one harness entered training with a cold-start initialization and the other didn't, so part of that entropy story may belong to the missing warm-up rather than to the optimizers. Elsewhere the ablation tables split the credit cleanly: mixing harnesses inside each advantage group is what buys transfer, while the cold-start warm-up is mostly what keeps the opening hundred steps from thrashing.

Then the transfer experiment, which cuts both ways. A checkpoint trained white-box scores 59.90 inside its own training loop and only 50.33 when moved to a held-out harness, a loss of nearly ten points, while the black-box mix-harness recipe reaches 62.62. Read generously, their approach wins by respecting harness boundaries. Read plainly, it confirms that naive agent RL memorizes whatever scaffold it grew up in, and that this failure mode is large enough to dominate algorithmic choices.

Worth flagging too: the headline benchmark, ClawGym-Bench, comes from the same ecosystem as the training sandbox, so the external PinchBench results deserve the most trust, and conveniently, they are the larger gains. And since group-relative baselines get noisy when groups are small and rewards are single bits, it is fair to ask how much of the movement between adjacent checkpoints is variance rather than learning.

Two gaps remain. Every headline number comes from a single base model family, so nothing here says whether the recipe survives a smaller dense model or a frontier-scale one. And for a paper obsessed with cost accounting, it never prices its own wins: no wall-clock or dollar figure per point of Pass@1, despite machinery that exists precisely because harness rollouts are extremely expensive. There is also a trade the paper makes without dwelling on it: abandoning gradients means abandoning dense shaping and most interpretability of why the policy improved. The bet is that fidelity to the deployed stack beats visibility into it, and the transfer table suggests that bet pays, at least at this scale.

## Verdict

This is an infrastructure paper whose ablations outrun its headlines, showing that the harness, not the model, has become the unit agents silently overfit during training. Treat its numbers as one-model-family evidence, but treat its discipline, proxy capture, prefix trees, and mix-harness grouping, as the emerging checklist anyone doing serious agent RL will be compared against.
