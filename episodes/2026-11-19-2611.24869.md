WorldRuler: Interactive World Modeling under Configurable Rules, by Mingliang Zhai, Chuanhao Li, and Jiaming Tan, at Alaya Lab with the Beijing Institute of Technology and the Shanghai Innovation Institute, is a system for making generative video world models follow user-defined rules rather than just the physics of the real world. The pitch, in the paper's own example, is that the same scene and the same action space can produce completely different games depending on the rules, Cat Mario versus ordinary Mario, and that a world model that only reproduces real-world behavior cannot deliver customizable gameplay. So WorldRuler treats the rules as first-class, executable structure. The core contribution is an executable 3D rule graph that organizes rule definitions, the dependencies between rules, and the state of objects in the scene, and it wires rule execution, video generation, and perceptual feedback together in a closed loop. The graph supports jointly saving and restoring both the rules and the world state, so a game you can save and reload actually reloads with the rules intact, which the paper names as the Rule-State Persistence problem.

Let me make concrete what the two problems are, because the whole design hangs on them. Rule-State Persistence is the unglamorous one: a rule-based game has a save file, and the save file has to encode not just the pixels but the rule definitions, the dependencies, and which objects are in which state, so that when you load, the restored world behaves under the same rules. Most generative world models have no notion of a save file at all, they just generate the next frames, so this is a genuine gap. Rule-to-Visual Realization is the harder one: when a rule fires, the designated objects and attributes have to change on screen, and they have to change even when the affected object has not appeared in the frame yet. The running example in the paper is a switch you flip that changes a door somewhere off-screen; the model has to keep that rule-induced state and realize it the first time the door becomes visible. That is the case that breaks most current world models, because they have no persistent, addressable state to consult.

The mechanisms. Dynamic object binding grounds a rule-specified entity, an abstract object in the rule graph, to a concrete visual instance in the generated video, and uses the stored state to constrain generation from the object's first appearance onward, so the object does not visually reset or drift once it has changed. Object attribute state replacement is the control mechanism: the paper shows a formula, Equation 8, where the predicted velocity in the diffusion sampler is adjusted by the difference between the target attribute state and the current one, scaled by the noise level, so the generated frames are steered toward the rule-specified attribute. Counterfactual reconstruction training is the training trick that keeps this from corrupting the rest of the scene: the model is trained on paired counterfactuals, the same scene with and without a rule effect, with losses, Equations 9 through 11, that penalize changes to attributes that should not change. That is the interference control, the thing that keeps a rule about the door from also warping the sky.

The data. The authors build RuleVerse, a dataset of rule-driven interactions, with an automated collection and annotation pipeline built on GTA-V and Habitat. It contains 3,200 pairs and 6,400 videos, with 2,900 pairs for training and 300 for testing, and it explicitly includes cases where a rule takes effect before the affected object becomes visible, which is the exact hard case from the Rule-to-Visual Realization problem. That is a thoughtful design choice, because it means the training set is not biased toward the easy, immediately-visible rule effects. The pipeline is automated end to end, which matters for a dataset whose whole value is in the rule annotations, because hand-annotating rule-to-visual pairs at this scale is not feasible, and the fact that the collection runs on two different engines, a commercial game and a research simulation, is the paper's first hint that the rule graph is meant to be engine-agnostic rather than tied to one scene generator.

The numbers, with the caveats. On the Habitat subset of RuleVerse, WorldRuler reaches an average Rule Success Rate of 65 percent and a Full Completion Rate of 48 percent, beating the strongest structured-state baseline, SWM, by about 21 points on rule success and 30 points on full completion. The breakdown is where it is most striking: effect accuracy goes from 20.74 percent under SWM to 58.47 percent, timing accuracy from 15.67 to 37.36, and hold accuracy, whether the changed state persists over the required observation window, from 17.05 to 50.83. Those are not marginal gains, they are the difference between a model that occasionally realizes a rule and one that does so reliably enough to be usable. On the GTA-V subset, WorldRuler hits 67.42 percent average rule success against 44.19 for SWM and 43.43 for MiniMax-H3, with the full-completion components at 60 percent for stages, 60 percent for order, and 80 percent for goal. The paper also runs an external evaluation on the public WorldState-Bench to show the approach generalizes beyond its own dataset.

The skeptical reading. First, the absolute numbers, even the good ones, are far below reliable: a 48 percent full completion rate means that in roughly half the cases the full rule sequence does not complete. This is a proof of the architecture, not a deployed game engine, and the paper should not be read as one. Second, the baselines are structured-state world models, SWM and CWM, plus a strong general video model, MiniMax-H3, which is a fair comparison, but the comparison is on rule-following, a capability the baselines were not specifically designed for, so part of the gap is task-mismatch rather than pure method superiority. Third, the rule graph is hand-structured for the rules in the dataset, and the paper does not test open-ended, user-authored rules at the breadth real gameplay would demand, so the generality of the graph schema is unproven beyond the rule families in RuleVerse. Fourth, the closed-loop perceptual feedback is the part most likely to be the weak link at scale, because feeding generated frames back into the rule state introduces compounding error, and the paper evaluates on relatively short rule sequences.

For implementation: if you are building a rule-based generative game, the recipe is (1) represent your rules and object states as an explicit, addressable graph rather than hoping the video model infers them, (2) bind each rule entity to a visual instance and constrain generation from first appearance, (3) use a counterfactual training objective so rule effects do not leak into unrelated content, and (4) persist the graph as part of the save state. The evaluation stack is rule success rate and full completion rate, broken into identity, trigger, effect, timing, and hold components, which is a much more diagnostic metric than a single aggregate. The cheapest validation is to take an existing structured-state world model and add the rule graph and the attribute-state-replacement control, then check whether the hold accuracy, the persistence component, moves, because that is the component that is currently the worst and the one the paper improves the most.

To summarize the bottom line. WorldRuler is a clean, well-motivated step toward world models that obey user-defined rules, with an explicit rule graph, a save-and-restore story, a counterfactual training objective that limits interference, and a purpose-built dataset that includes the hard unseen-object cases. The gains over structured-state baselines are large on the components that matter, effect and hold accuracy, but the absolute full-completion rate is still low and the generality of the rule schema is untested beyond the dataset. If your goal is customizable generative gameplay, this is the paper to read for the architecture, and the hold-accuracy number is the one to watch when you try to reproduce it.
