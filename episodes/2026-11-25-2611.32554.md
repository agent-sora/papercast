PartAgent: Agentic 3D Part Segmentation via Expert Integration and Closed-Loop Refinement, from Yang Cao, Zhe Zhu, and Yiheng Zhang, at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Tencent, the Hong Kong Polytechnic University, and Sun Yat-sen University. The claim: a training-free agent that fuses a frozen 3D region model with frozen 2D semantic models, then iteratively refines the partition under VLM supervision, beats the strongest competing 3D part segmenter by 8.5 points in mean intersection over union on PartObjaverse-Tiny-WT.

The task is partitioning a 3D object into semantically meaningful parts, legs, arms, panels, doors. It needs two kinds of evidence at once. Pretrained 3D experts produce spatially grounded regions, but a region may cover only a fragment of a component or span several components at once. 2D experts, run over multiview renderings, give semantic cues, but predictions from different views disagree about boundaries and identities. The paper's framing is that both problems, integrating the evidence into a coherent partition and then repairing an imperfect one, are decision problems well suited to an agent that can observe and act.

The first module is Structural-Semantic Expert Integration. It takes a mesh, runs a frozen 3D region expert to get candidate regions, and a frozen 2D segmentation model over multiple rendered views to get semantic masks. It then maps the 2D evidence onto the surface through image-to-surface correspondences, so each face on the mesh carries semantic labels from every view that sees it. The mapped evidence refines the 3D regions: a region that the 2D evidence says is two parts gets split, and fragments that the evidence says belong together get merged. The refined regions form leaves, and the module composes them into candidate partitions at multiple granularities, organized as a mask tree with a directed acyclic graph of composition relationships. A VLM selects the initial partition, and the alternative candidates are kept in a bank for later revision.

The evidence fusion has a specific scoring rule worth knowing, because it is where most of the robustness comes from. For each face on the mesh and each semantic category, the module accumulates a score across all rendered views: the share of that face's projected pixels assigned to the category in each view, down-weighted when the face projects to only a few pixels, and down-weighted again when the face is viewed nearly edge-on, since a near-grazing view is a poor semantic witness. A face receives a category only if its total score is positive, the top category is unique, and the margin between the top two scores, normalized by the total, clears a confidence threshold; otherwise the face stays unassigned rather than misassigned. That is a conservative design that trades recall for precision at the fusion step. Category support then propagates across adjacent faces, and each 3D expert region is refined by splitting where its faces disagree and grouping where they agree, with one explicit rule: a shared category never by itself merges two distinct 3D expert regions, so the 3D expert remains authoritative on structure and the image evidence only refines and labels within it.

The second module is the refinement loop, Observation-Driven Closed-Loop Partition Refinement. The agent observes the current state: the partition projected into the views, geometry renders, the leaf identifiers, and the multiview semantic evidence. A VLM policy then selects a targeted action from a small set: inspect a region in more detail, repair a 2D segmentation mask where the evidence is wrong, compose or split part groupings, or reselect an alternative candidate partition from the bank. Each proposed update is verified before adoption, and both acceptances and rejections feed back into the next action choice, so the loop learns from its own mistakes within an episode. The paper is explicit that the loop terminates on a finish action, so this is a bounded search, not an unbounded agent.

The evaluation uses four standard benchmarks: 3DCoMPaT200, PartNeXt, PartNet-E, and PartObjaverse-Tiny. On the weighted PartObjaverse-Tiny-WT split, PartAgent reaches 68.41 mIoU, 8.53 points above the strongest competing method, which is PartField at 59.88. The category breakdown on that split is consistent rather than concentrated: humans at 70.97, animals at 71.00, daily objects at 70.92, building and transportation at 56.40, plants at 62.93, food at 75.40, and electronics at 78.00, with the weaker categories being the geometrically harder ones, building and transportation and plants, which is the pattern you expect when 2D semantic evidence is doing the grouping work and the geometry resists it. Across the four datasets it has the highest mIoU on each one, with a four-dataset average of 69.36, 7.28 points above the best competing average. The paper also shows qualitative segmentations on meshes generated by Hunyuan3D, which matters because the framework is training-free and the experts are frozen: the question of generalization to out-of-distribution, generated geometry is at least posed.

The candidate construction is worth a closer look, because it is where the geometry and the semantics actually get reconciled. The module builds a graph whose selectable nodes are the refined 3D leaves, the raw 3D expert regions, and semantic group nodes, each node standing for a mask that is the union of its descendant leaves. A valid initial partition is a set of these nodes whose masks cover the whole mesh and are pairwise disjoint, so the VLM is not free to invent parts; it chooses among a bank of geometrically consistent candidates that span different granularities, from fine leaf-level tilings up to whole semantic groups. That constraint is what keeps the output from degenerating: every candidate partition is a real tiling of the surface, and the semantic evidence only decides which tiling is the right one. The refinement loop then operates on the same graph, so a repair action that fixes a 2D mask or a reselect action that swaps to a coarser or finer candidate always lands back on a valid partition, and the verification step is checking a concrete geometric object rather than a free-form description.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? The first caveat is in the method's own name: it is closed-loop in the workflow sense, a VLM looking at its own outputs and deciding what to fix, which is exactly the pattern where agents can confabulate improvements. The verification step mitigates this, but the paper does not report how often verification rejects a proposed update or whether rejections correlate with actual quality changes, so the loop's precision is unmeasured as written. Second, the whole system is a pipeline of frozen models plus a VLM policy, and the VLM's identity, prompt, and number of loop iterations are the load-bearing hyperparameters; a 7 to 8 point mIoU gap is large enough to survive a decent re-implementation, but the per-dataset breakdown matters, because part benchmarks have very different failure modes, and a mean of 69.36 hides how much of the gain comes from semantic grouping versus geometric precision. Third, cost: a training-free agent that renders multiview images, runs 2D segmentation per view, and iterates a VLM loop is expensive at inference time compared to a single forward pass of a native 3D segmenter, and the paper leads with accuracy rather than wall-clock or token cost. A method that needs a dozen VLM calls to win eight points of mIoU has a price tag that a production team has to see before adopting it.

Bottom line: PartAgent is a clean demonstration that an observe-act-verify agent over frozen 2D and 3D experts can outperform specialized trained segmenters on part segmentation by a double-digit margin on the hardest benchmark, with a portable recipe that needs no training data and no fine-tuning budget. The open questions are the loop's reliability statistics and its inference cost, both of which the paper should have reported more prominently.
