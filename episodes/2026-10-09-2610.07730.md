"SanSi: a looped typed decision model for System 1.5 thinking," by Shuyu Gan, Young-Jun Lee, and Dongyeop Kang from the University of Minnesota. The name SanSi is the pinyin for a phrase from the Analects meaning think thrice before acting, which fits the idea. This one sits right in the reasoning-topology space, on looping, so it is in scope on the standing rule. The setting is a specific and increasingly common kind of model call: a typed decision. You give the model a state, like a passage or a set of rules, a question, and a declared list of options, and it returns one probability per option in a single forward pass, with no generated text. A single pass is fast, but it gives every decision the same fixed amount of compute. A lot of real decisions need several dependent steps, and one pass is not enough for those. Their question is what lies between a single pass, which they call System 1, and full generated reasoning, which is System 2. The middle option is looping, applying the same layers recursively several times before reading out the answer. This is the same looping idea you have seen in the other papers today, but here it is applied not to free-form generation and not to a puzzle, but to the specific, increasingly common task of a calibrated yes-or-no-or-choice decision that a system wants to use for routing, escalation, or acceptance. That is a very practical target, because the whole point of a typed decision model is that you can act on the number directly.

The concrete example they use is a liar chain. Omar is honest, Bert says Omar tells the truth, Ben says Bert lies. Whether Ben tells the truth only comes out if you follow the chain one statement at a time. A single-pass model, their commercial reference called the Jev API, drops from a hundred percent on one step to 62.5 percent on three steps and is at chance from six steps on. The fix in the paper, SanSi, turns a pre-trained looped language model, Ouro, into a typed decision model. The backbone stays frozen; they only train LoRA adapters and a small per-loop readout, 61 million parameters total on the 1.4 billion parameter backbone. The key design is that the option probabilities are read after every loop, and each loop is trained with a proper scoring rule, the sum of cross-entropy and the Brier score. That means one single run with eight loops produces the answer at every budget from one loop to eight, so you can pick your compute budget at test time from the same model. The backbone is 24 Transformer layers applied T times, and running more loops adds computation but no parameters, so the typed contract is preserved. The readout itself is small and principled. They take the final normalized hidden state after each loop, grab the row at the last prompt token, and project it through the frozen language-model head rows for the option letters, plus a rank-16 LoRA correction and a per-loop temperature scale that starts at the identity. Because the probability at each loop is computed only from that loop's hidden state, a single pass with eight loops yields the full trajectory of decisions, from one loop to eight, without any separate model per budget.

The results are on 10,027 test decisions from 59 sources. On the full suite SanSi reaches 72.0 percent accuracy. With identical data, recipe, and seeds, looping adds 13.5 points over SmolLM2 1.7B, a non-looped model of the same shape trained the same way, and it brings SanSi within 1.8 points of Qwen 3.5 4B, a single-pass model with three times the parameters. That framing is the point of the paper: single-pass models buy accuracy by adding parameters, whereas SanSi buys it by looping the same parameters. The gain is not uniform. It is smallest on simple classification, at 3.1 points, and largest on multi-step reasoning at 15.1 points, long documents at 16.7, and knowledge questions at 17.6, which is exactly where one pass is the limiting factor. The cost is 7.7 times the compute of a single pass, so this is a deliberate trade-off between compute and accuracy. The suite itself is worth a note. The 10,027 test items are split into in-distribution items, near transfer items that are harder or reworded versions of the trained types, far transfer items from 30 sources the model never saw, including FOLIO, BBH, and MMLU, plus a small block of public JevBench items. The fact that the gain holds across the far-transfer group, and that knowledge questions, which appear only in the test set, are among the biggest winners, is a stronger signal than a purely in-distribution number would be.

There is one more thing worth flagging about the setup, because it strengthens the claim. They keep the comparison honest by training every model, SanSi and all the single-pass references, with the exact same recipe, the same training items in the same order, LoRA of the same rank on the same modules, the same readout and the same loss, with three seeds each. They even train a control, Qwen 4B on their data, using the Kev recipe, to check that the reference numbers do not depend on their training pipeline. And they train a larger version, SanSi 2.6B, on the bigger looped backbone with the same recipe, so the result is not an artifact of the small model. That level of control, running the non-looped baselines under the identical recipe rather than just comparing against released checkpoints, is what makes the 13.5 point gap attributable to looping itself rather than to a better training recipe.

There are three secondary findings I found more interesting than the headline. First, the answers settle fast. Reading after just three loops already captures 88 percent of the total gain from loop one to loop eight, and most answers are decided by the fourth loop, with harder items settling later. So the practical recommendation is that a few loops buys most of the benefit. Second, on two depth-controlled tasks, the loops solve depths the model never saw in training. On liar chains of nine to 16 steps, where training only went up to eight, SanSi is right on 72.6 percent of items while the larger single-pass Qwen 4B sits at 50.0 percent, which is the chance level for those chains. That is a clean demonstration that looping generalizes to longer chains rather than just memorizing training-length ones. Third, used purely as the reward for training a generator with reinforcement learning, with no gold answers, SanSi raises the generator's F1 from 39.5 to 47.3, a 7.7 point gain, which suggests it is a reasonable judge for policy optimization.

The honest caveats. The calibration result is a double-edged finding. The loops do make the model better at noticing when the evidence for a decision is missing, but they only improve calibration up to about the third loop; after the answer settles, confidence keeps rising, so the model can become overconfident on a decision it has already locked in. That is a real reliability concern for the exact use case where you rely on the probability for routing or escalation. The benchmark is also a curated decision suite, heavily weighted toward the multi-step and long-document cases where looping is designed to help, so the 13.5 point figure is on a favorable distribution, and the 7.7 times compute cost is not trivial. The parameter count is small, under 3 billion, so this is a mechanism study rather than a claim about frontier-scale decision models.

Here is the bottom line. SanSi shows that looping a frozen pre-trained backbone, and reading a typed decision after every loop, recovers accuracy that single-pass models only get by adding several times the parameters, with the gains concentrated exactly on the multi-step and long-context decisions where a single forward pass fails, and the model generalizes to chain lengths it never trained on. If you are building typed-decision or routing systems where you control the compute budget, the read-every-loop design and the settle-by-loop-three finding are directly useful, but watch the post-settle overconfidence before you trust the probabilities for anything high-stakes.
