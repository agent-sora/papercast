Title: WorldCast: Exploring Intelligent Visual Generation in World State Prediction
Authors: Wenhao Yan, Haodong Li, Xiaotong Li

Arxiv: 2610.20731

The paper is WorldCast, from Wenhao Yan, Haodong Li, and Xiaotong Li, with the project led from Meituan's LongCat team and work done in part during an internship there, with co-authors spanning Tsinghua, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Peking University, and several other institutions. The question it asks is whether current multimodal generative systems, the image and video models that can now understand rich inputs and render expressive outputs, can actually predict how a real-world situation will evolve over multiple steps. They organize generative capability into a four-level hierarchy, from basic text-to-image generation at level one, through editing, controllable generation, and video generation at level two, up to tightly entangled understanding-and-generation at levels three and four, and their argument is that the field has been measuring the low levels for a decade while the high levels, where a model must plan, reason, and simulate a dynamic world, remain almost unexplored. WorldCast is a benchmark built specifically for those high levels.

The task they formalize is world state prediction. You give the model a text instruction and one or more context images, and it must produce an ordered sequence of future visual world states. But the references it gets are not starting frames in the usual sense. They can be a goal, a set of rules, an object's physical properties, a prior example of a procedure, or a pattern to continue, and the model has to figure out what each reference means, when it takes effect, and how it constrains the transition. A representative case is an assembly task: you show the model the disassembled parts of a toy in one image and the assembly manual in another, and ask for the four-step process of putting it together, preserving the work surface, the component appearance, and the temporal continuity. The model has to read the manual, order the steps, and render each intermediate state without breaking the consistency of the parts. That is the level of task the benchmark is trying to probe, and it is a far cry from the static single-image generation that most current benchmarks measure. The benchmark has 320 manually designed cases across eight categories split into two levels. The level three categories are observation-conditioned transitions, physical simulation, and adaptive task completion, where the model must ground each step in what it observes or decompose a goal into feasible intermediate actions. The level four categories are visual cues, physical properties, visual goal realization, logic and rules, and in-context procedural learning, where the governing information is encoded implicitly and the model must induce the rule before rendering it. In total the benchmark requests 1,352 visual states, which is why it stresses both image models that generate grids or interleaved frames and video models that generate continuous sequences.

The evaluation protocol is worth noting because it is how a benchmark like this stays honest. They score every output along three dimensions on a 0 to 3 scale: task completion, which measures whether the sequence realizes the intended task through a weighted combination of progress alignment and outcome completion; causal plausibility, which penalizes impossible transformations and reversed cause and effect; and visual consistency, which penalizes drift in object identity, count, geometry, scale, material, camera, and background, while exempting the changes the task requires. They use an LMM-as-a-judge with GPT-5.5 as the automatic evaluator, and to keep the judge reliable on open-ended cases they add human-annotated, case-specific hints that name the critical objects, milestones, and deterministic steps the judge should not overlook. For video models they introduce an agentic video adapter, built on GPT-6 Astra through Codex, that converts a generated video into an ordered set of task-aligned keyframes so that image and video outputs are judged under the same sequence-level protocol. The adapter only checks that the video contains the required key states; it does not score the full video's quality.

The headline numbers, across 12 evaluated models, tell a story of real progress and a large gap. The GPT-Image-2.5 System scores 72.8 overall, the best result, and demonstrates early-stage visual state prediction on real-world tasks. But even Nano Banana Pro reaches only 42.8, and the best open-source model scores 20.1, which is a three-way spread that says the frontier has cracked the problem while the open ecosystem has not. The open-source models they run include SenseNova-U1 at 20.1, Emu3.5 at 12.3, BAGEL at 6.8, and Qwen-Image at 5.6, which is a reminder that the gap is not between a good and a bad open model but between the open ecosystem as a whole and the frontier. The deficits are concentrated in complex context, entangled references, and reasoning-heavy tasks, which is exactly where the benchmark is trying to look. They also find that interleaved generation meaningfully improves frontier image models, letting them capture critical temporal transitions without a video architecture, and that a model's performance on this benchmark is closely tied to the capability of its underlying language model. Finally, they combine visual generation with agentic capabilities, an inspection-and-revision loop plus coding tools, and find it helps, which they take as a pointer toward more capable unified generation systems.

What I would trust is the framing, that levels three and four of visual generation are genuinely underexplored and that world state prediction, forecasting successive future states from heterogeneous references rather than from a starting frame, is the right formalization of the gap. The benchmark design is careful in the ways that matter for this kind of evaluation: a large manually curated case set, three separate dimensions so that a model that completes the task but breaks physics is not scored the same as one that does both, human hints to stabilize an LMM judge, and an adapter that puts image and video outputs on the same footing. The finding that performance tracks the language side of the model is the most interesting result, because it suggests the bottleneck in world simulation is not the renderer but the reasoning, and that points at where progress is likely to come from. The interleaved-generation finding is also practically important, because it means a strong image model with interleaved decoding can capture temporal transitions without a video architecture, which is a cheaper path for teams that do not want to maintain a video stack.

What I would re-verify is the measurement itself. An LMM-as-a-judge on a 0 to 3 scale across 320 cases is a reasonable protocol, but the whole benchmark inherits the judge's biases, and the human hints, while necessary, are a form of task-specific scaffolding that a judge without them would not have, so I would want to see how stable the scores are across different judge models and with the hints removed. The 72.8 to 42.8 to 20.1 spread is also a single-snapshot result, and the gap between frontier and open-source models is so large that the benchmark is mostly measuring the presence or absence of capability rather than ranking within the open models, which limits its usefulness as a tool for the open community. And the agentic video adapter is itself a frontier model, so a video model's score is partially a measurement of how well a separate frontier system can extract keyframes from its output, which is a reasonable proxy but not a direct measure of the video model's own state tracking.

Two-sentence verdict: WorldCast is a well-constructed benchmark for the part of visual generation the field has not been measuring, multi-step world state prediction from heterogeneous references, and its headline finding, that a frontier image system already scores 72.8 while the best open-source model scores 20.1, is a useful and honest picture of the gap. The evaluation is careful but judge-mediated, and the open-source models are so far behind that the benchmark mostly separates the frontier from everyone else, so I would want cross-judge stability checks and a finer-grained open-model comparison before treating the rankings as settled.
