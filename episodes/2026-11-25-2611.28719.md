KMed-31B Technical Report, from the KMed team at NAVER. The claim: a 31 billion parameter medical large language model, post-trained from an open-weight base for on-premises deployment, that beats every open-weight model it compares, including models with more than ten times the parameters, on HealthBench Professional, and posts the highest unadjusted HealthBench Hard score among the compared models, including proprietary ones.

The deployment constraint shapes the whole paper. Clinical services often cannot send patient data outside the institution, so the model has to run on premises with limited hardware, which rules out frontier closed models and points at relatively small open-weight models. The problem the authors identify is that these models have uneven strengths: Gemma-4-31B-IT matches a frontier model on multiple-choice medical questions but falls far behind on open-ended clinical benchmarks, while a different 30 billion model approaches a 397 billion model on the open-ended side but is weak on the multiple-choice side. And post-training makes it worse: improving one task lowers the other. They cite prior work where rubric-based reinforcement learning raises HealthBench Hard from 9.46 to 33.17 while dropping multiple-choice accuracy, and they reproduce the same pattern when fine-tuning their own base on consultation dialogues, even with multiple-choice examples mixed into the data. Their diagnosis is that the interference comes from how the tasks are posed and supervised, not from any particular model or optimizer.

The organizing principle is a two-by-two split. Medical tasks differ in expected output and in available feedback. Question answering and diagnosis prediction have reference answers, so the feedback is answer matching. Clinical inquiry, open-ended consultation with lay users and clinicians, has no single correct answer and is judged by rubrics that specify criteria for accuracy, completeness, and appropriateness. These are distinct domains, in the same sense that mathematical reasoning and open-ended chat are distinct in general-purpose post-training, and domains trained together in one run interfere. So the recipe assigns one specialist per task category, trained with the reward its feedback supports: the reference-answer specialists go through reinforcement learning with answer-matching rewards, and the clinical inquiry specialist goes through a staged recipe from supervised fine-tuning to rubric-based reinforcement learning and then to a rubric-based on-policy distillation stage, where each query's rubric is regenerated from fixed reference responses from several models together with the specialist's current rollouts.

The data table makes the scale concrete, and it is small by frontier standards, which is the point. Open-ended question answering uses 9,300 reinforcement prompts built from an open-ended conversion of the MedQA-USMLE training split, where the answer options are stripped and the text of the correct option becomes the reference answer. Multiple-choice uses 15,500 items from a difficulty-filtered subset of MedMCQA. Diagnosis prediction uses 13,000 cases from MedCaseReasoning, with the case presentations rewritten specifically to remove explicit answer cues, which is a data-hygiene step that matters more than it sounds. Clinical inquiry is the big one: 50,000 supervised query-and-response pairs from the synthetic corpus, then 10,000 rubric-judged reinforcement prompts, then a final 2,000 for the rubric-regeneration distillation stage, where the shrinking prompt counts across the three stages show the recipe moving from imitation to judgment to self-improvement. All reinforcement stages use group relative policy optimization, and the non-thinking branch carries a separate specialist for each of the four categories, with the thinking branch covering three of them.

For clinical inquiry they build Synthetic Health Dialogue, a simulated corpus of consultations whose patient cases are grounded in the clinical skills objectives of the Korean Medical Licensing Examination. The simulator separates the information available to the user from what the assistant can observe, so the relevant context is initially missing, and the demonstrations are generated to encourage the model to seek missing context and check the premises of a request. That is a thoughtful data design for a task where the right behavior is to ask, not to answer.

The training mechanics are specified in enough detail to rebuild. The reference-answer specialists are trained with reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, using a leave-one-out variant of group relative policy optimization: for each prompt the policy samples a group of responses, and the advantage of one response is its reward minus the mean of the other responses in the group, normalized by their standard deviation, with a degenerate case when the other rewards are all equal. The policy loss is a clipped surrogate with a KL penalty toward the run's initial checkpoint, with the KL coefficient set per category. That per-category coefficient is a small but telling detail: the authors are tuning how hard each specialist is pulled toward its own base, category by category, which is exactly the kind of control you need when the categories have different feedback quality.

To get a single deployable model, they integrate the specialists with Multi-teacher On-policy Delta Distillation. On the student's own rollouts, the specialist for each task category supplies a token-level signal based on its log-probability difference from a fixed reference checkpoint, the pretrained base model. The delta is the point: each specialist contributes only what it acquired relative to the base, so integrating them does not re-impose the base's behavior and then fight over the delta.

The results. Relative to its base, Gemma-4-31B-IT, KMed-31B raises HealthBench Hard from 22.95 to 41.87 in non-thinking mode and from 23.80 to 51.48 in thinking mode, and HealthBench Professional from 37.92 to 50.20 and from 41.97 to 59.18 respectively, while the four multiple-choice benchmarks are maintained or slightly improved. In both modes it has the highest unadjusted HealthBench Hard among the compared models including proprietary ones, and it beats every compared open-weight model on HealthBench Professional, including models with more than ten times the total parameters. The paper also reports a price-performance plot at a blended 3-to-1 input-to-output token ratio, placing KMed-31B near the frontier models at a fraction of the cost, and it applies the same recipe to a second base, Qwen3.8-27B, where both HealthBench scores also rise, which is the transferability check on the recipe itself. The report explicitly describes the data construction and settings in detail so institutions can rebuild the recipe from open-weight foundations.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? First, the HealthBench numbers are unadjusted, the paper says so, and HealthBench scores are sensitive to response length; a model that writes longer consultations can move these numbers without moving underlying quality, so I would want the length-adjusted comparison before ranking KMed-31B against the proprietary models it is plotted next to. Second, the multiple-choice benchmarks are described as maintained or slightly improved, which is the honest framing of the interference problem: the recipe does not eliminate the trade-off, it contains it, and the exact deltas on the four benchmarks are the number to check, because a specialist-per-category design that loses two points on one benchmark to gain nineteen on another is a product decision, not a free lunch. Third, Synthetic Health Dialogue is grounded in the Korean Medical Licensing Examination objectives, which is a strong and specific grounding, but it is Korean primary-care-flavored data, and the generalization of the clinical-inquiry specialist to other regulatory environments is an open question that the second-base experiment does not address. Fourth, the MOPD2 integration is the novel mechanism, and the ablation that matters is student quality with and without the delta signal, because without it the whole claim is just multi-teacher distillation with extra steps.

Bottom line: KMed-31B is a well-motivated open-weight medical model whose recipe, one specialist per task category plus delta-based multi-teacher on-policy distillation, directly attacks the documented interference between reference-answer and rubric-judged medical tasks, with large, specific HealthBench gains and a second-base replication. The unadjusted scores and the Korean grounding of the synthetic data are the two caveats to carry into any adoption decision.
