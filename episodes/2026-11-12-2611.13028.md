GTAGym: Real AAA Game Tasks Environment for Evaluation and Reinforcement Learning, by Jiajun Chen, Shicheng Fang, and Jiahao Lu, from the Shanghai Innovation Institute and Fudan University, is a game-agent benchmark and RL environment built from the main-story missions of Grand Theft Auto 5, with automatic outcome checking so it can be used for both evaluation and training.

The motivation is that evaluating whether an agent actually completes a task in a AAA game is hard because task outcomes are usually hard to verify automatically. Most prior game benchmarks either live in purpose-built sandboxes like Minecraft, where engine state is available, or they evaluate commercial games with human or vision-model judgment, which does not scale and does not close the loop for reinforcement learning. GTAGym's answer is to reconstruct objectives taken from real GTA V missions in a browser-based 3D environment built on Babylon.js, where every task has executable success and failure predicates that read the engine state directly. That gives you programmatic outcome checking on mission-derived tasks, which is the combination the paper argues has been missing.

The benchmark, GTAVBench, has two tracks. The online 3D track contains 103 tasks across nine categories, reconstructed from 75 main-story missions that the authors manually decomposed into subtasks from walkthrough videos. The categories split into navigation, interaction, and gun use: road, on-foot, aquatic, vertical, aerial, and indoor navigation, plus following, interception, and evasion. The image-based track adds 47 shooting and sniping tasks built from GTA V gameplay imagery, so the full suite is 150 tasks. Navigation routes are hand-traced on the source minimap and re-laid out in the available city geometry to match turn counts, and dynamic tasks use predefined NPC behaviors like collision-induced slowdown.

There are two evaluation protocols. In the standard GTAVBench branch, the agent gets a fixed system prompt, a textual task instruction, and a screenshot-plus-reply history, and it emits JSON actions that an adapter validates and executes, with the 3D simulation paused during inference and advanced during action execution. The GTAVBench-AGI branch is harder: the instruction appears only in the first screenshot as an in-game objective, the way it would appear in real play, and a Codex harness automatically compresses the interaction history as it grows. Scoring is micro-averaged accuracy over the 150 tasks, with per-category breakdowns, and episodes end on success, failure, or budget exhaustion.

The models evaluated are six: two GPT variants, DeepSeek, GLM, Kimi, and Qwen 3.8 27B. The result is a large gap between the GPT models and everyone else. The best configuration, the top GPT model under the AGI harness, reaches 70.9 percent overall accuracy, which is still 18.9 percentage points below the human mean of 89.8 percent. The two GPT models reliably complete on-foot, indoor, and vertical navigation, but they collapse on aerial navigation, where they score at or near zero, and they struggle with interception and evasion, the tasks that require sustained regulation of vehicle motion or tracking an evolving target. The smaller models do much worse across the board, with the 27B model at 24.2 percent on the standard branch. One striking efficiency point: under the JSON protocol, DeepSeek spends roughly 26 times as many output tokens per task as the top GPT model without matching its success rate.

The AGI protocol, where the goal is in the image and the context is compressed, actually improves overall accuracy for all six models by 0.9 to 10 percentage points, most consistently on navigation, where the long instruction chain is the failure source. But it cuts tool-use success sharply, because compressing the instruction into a single image loses the step-by-step detail the action chain depends on, so the protocol is a trade between reasoning quality and action grounding rather than a free lunch.

A few implementation details matter if you are going to run this yourself. The action space is keyboard and mouse: the agent specifies a hold duration of up to one second for each key, keys execute concurrently, and the turn duration is the longest scheduled hold, with the simulation paused during inference by default. Online history is capped at ten screenshot-reply pairs on the standard branch, dropping the oldest five when exceeded while preserving the instruction. Navigation and interaction tasks allow up to 300 interaction rounds, gun-use tasks up to 20. The environment is served through a web server that exposes a shared screenshot-and-action interface, so you can run many agents in parallel against the same task pool, and the task-specific completion predicates check things like movement mode, height or depth, target distance, and interaction events, with following and interception tasks running target and pursuer controllers on predefined routes.

The RL environment generator is the piece that makes this usable as anything other than a benchmark. Each task template fixes the scene, assets, interaction logic, evaluator, and episode budget, and exposes sampling parameters, things like movement mode, route length, turn count, and actor speed, that the user constrains to numeric intervals or finite candidate sets. The generator samples within those bounds, rejects infeasible configurations, and reports when no instance can be found, then saves the resolved configuration and seed so an environment can be reproduced exactly. Task outcomes serve as sparse rewards, success and failure end an episode, and budget exhaustion is recorded as truncation. That is a workable recipe for generating batches of on-distribution RL instances from a single hand-built template, and it is the part I would expect other groups to actually reuse.

The RL half of the paper is smaller in scope but concrete. They use the template generator to produce 50 on-foot navigation training instances, spatially separated from the benchmark routes so nothing is memorized, and they fine-tune the Qwen 27B model with LoRA, rank 32, under GRPO inside the Codex harness, with a learning rate of 10 to the minus 6. The reward is terminal success plus a shaped distance-progress term for unsuccessful rollouts and a small penalty for exceeding 100 interaction rounds. After training, held-out on-foot accuracy rises from 54.2 to 72.9 percent, overall from 34.2 to 38.9 percent, and there is some cross-category transfer: vertical navigation jumps from 46.7 to 73.3 and aquatic from 57.6 to 69.7, though road driving stays at 3.3 percent and aerial and interception stay at zero, and shooting actually drops from 73.6 to 68.1. The transfer to native GTA V is the headline of this part: on 100 native navigation and interaction tasks, recorded success goes from 15 to 23 percent.

The failure-mode analysis names four recurring mechanisms, the most instructive being late descent correction in aerial navigation, where even the strongest model reaches the runway but cannot arrest a rapid descent fast enough, which is a real control problem, not a perception one. The paper also shows that in blocked trajectories the base model tends to repeat ineffective actions while the trained model uses action feedback to switch strategies, which is the kind of behavioral change you would want from RL.

Skeptically, this is a benchmark paper with a modest RL demonstration, and I would separate the two contributions in my head. The benchmark is the durable artifact: 150 automatically-verified tasks derived from a real commercial game, with a parallel web server for evaluation, is genuinely useful infrastructure, and the finding that every model fails aerial and pursuit-style dynamic control is a useful signal about where game agents are weak. But the reconstruction is hand-built in Babylon.js to resemble the original missions, so it is a simulation of the missions, not the game itself, and the gap between that and the 15 to 23 percent native GTA V transfer is exactly where the sim-to-real question lives. The RL result is a single-seed, single-category fine-tune of one 27B model with a shaped reward, and the cross-category transfer is modest and uneven, with shooting getting worse. I would want the native-transfer number on more tasks and seeds, and I would want to know how much of the on-foot gain is the LoRA versus the Codex harness, before treating the RL claim as more than a proof of concept.

Bottom line: GTAGym is a solid, reusable contribution in the benchmark infrastructure category, with automatic engine-state verification on mission-derived tasks that the field has not really had, and it cleanly localizes current game agents' weaknesses to sustained dynamic control. The RL fine-tune is a proof of concept with real but modest transfer, and the sim-to-native gap is the open question that decides whether the reconstruction is training on something that matters.
