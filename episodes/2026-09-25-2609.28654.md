Training Object Permanence in World Models. The first three authors are Haotian Zhang, Fengyuan Yu, and Dezhi Luo, from the University of Southern California, Carnegie Mellon University, and Columbia University, with a very long tail of coauthors across more than a dozen institutions, including Johns Hopkins, Oxford, Toronto, and Berkeley.

The question this paper starts from is deceptively simple. A toddler knows, without any training data, that a ball hidden under a cup is still there when the cup lifts, and that a solid ball cannot pass through a solid wall. Those two priors, object permanence and solidity, are among the first things infants reliably demonstrate, and developmental psychologists have studied them for decades. Video generation models are increasingly treated as world models, systems that supposedly understand how the physical world works. But do they actually have object permanence in them, or do they just generate frames that look plausible while quietly violating the physics? No one had a clean way to ask, because no one had built a benchmark that isolates exactly those two cognitive abilities while holding everything else constant.

That gap is what WROP fills. WROP, which stands for World Reasoning with Object Permanence, is a data infrastructure of one hundred and fifty hand-designed tasks, divided into six task families, each built around a generator written in Blender. The generators render the core cognitive scene, the occlusion, the barrier, the moving object, and then randomize everything that is not the cognition: lighting, camera angle, speed, background, and other nuisance parameters. Each task yields over ten thousand samples, which adds up to a released training corpus of about one and a half million samples, plus a separate three hundred question exam with human Elo ratings. The point of the randomization is that a model cannot memorize the answer by pattern matching the pixels; it has to get the underlying physics right. The six families are drawn from core cognition, the branch of developmental psychology that studies the infant's built-in expectations about objects, and they probe things like identity and count preservation through occlusion, whether objects remain themselves behind a screen, solidity violations where a ball meets a barrier, and identity swaps when objects are rotated, covered, and uncovered again.

The evaluation is the most careful part of the paper. The authors run a blind pairwise Elo study across fourteen video generation models, grouped into three families: three reference-to-video models, seven editing models, and four continuation models, the last being the class that takes an input clip and generates what happens next, which is the natural setting for judging permanence. Twenty crowdsourced raters, screened with qualification thresholds and embedded attention checks, did the pairwise judgments, and the inter-rater reliability numbers are reported, not hand-waved: the raters answered about ninety percent of the attention checks correctly and agreed with themselves on roughly ninety-three and a half percent of repeated items. On that leaderboard, two reference-to-video models, Wan 3.0 Prime and MiniMax H3, share the top two positions at one thousand seven hundred twenty four points each. The paper's own model, PWM-WROP, a sixteen billion parameter continuation model fine-tuned on the WROP data, ranks first among continuation models and third overall, behind only that statistical tie at the top. One model in particular, Wan 3.0 Prime, leads in the container permanence category and ranks second in collision, which the authors read as evidence that the categories separate real, distinct abilities rather than one general competence.

Where it gets interesting is the failure analysis, because the exam is designed to make violations legible. The classic case is three colored balls rolling in parallel lanes into an opaque tunnel: the model must continue the video with all three balls coming out the other side, in the same order, with the same identities and the same count. A weaker model will drop a ball, swap two colors, or produce four balls from three. Another classic is the solidity violation, a ball reaching a barrier and passing straight through it. One of the models on the board, Seedance 2.5, exhibits a particularly instructive failure: it tracks the ball by its position on the turntable rather than by which cup is covering it, so when the cups rotate and the covering cup moves, the model lifts the wrong cup and the identity of the ball gets lost. That is not a rendering glitch. That is the model failing to bind identity through occlusion, which is exactly the cognitive skill the benchmark is built to probe. Another failure mode the raters flagged is objects materializing from nothing on reveal: an oversized screen appears over an empty region of the scene, and when it retreats, three objects that were never hidden suddenly exist behind it.

The release is unusually generous, which is what makes this a resource rather than just a leaderboard. Along with the one and a half million sample corpus and the exam, the authors publish the model answers, the per-question scores, the model weights, and PWM, a native PyTorch training stack they built to run the fine-tune on AWS Trainium2, the second generation of Amazon's accelerator. That last part matters more than it sounds: training video models on commodity, non-NVIDIA silicon is a real constraint for a lot of groups, and a reference implementation that works on Trainium2 is a small but concrete contribution. The fine-tune itself is deliberately narrow. They take a continuation model, feed it the WROP data, and measure the effect on the two target abilities without claiming to have improved the model at everything else, which keeps the causal story clean: the gains on the exam are attributable to the permanence and solidity data.

There is also a methodological detail worth calling out because it shows how much the authors thought about the evaluation. The tasks are structured as input-to-target pairs, where the input is a short clip and the target is the correct continuation, but at inference the models are not handed the target. They only get the input, and they have to generate the continuation themselves. That rules out a trivial failure mode, where a model just copies the answer frame out of the reference. For models whose APIs stitch the source video onto their output, the authors trim the source span off before scoring, so the model is always graded on what it actually generated. It is the kind of bookkeeping that separates a benchmark you can trust from one that inflates the scores of the models that happen to leak the answer through their own plumbing, and it is the sort of detail you only notice if the authors thought about how a model could game the setup, which is a good sign about the rest of the evaluation design.

The honest caveats. This is a benchmark and a training release, not a claim that video models now understand physics. The authors are explicit that final-frame image metrics, similarity and FID against a reference clip, measure proximity to a target, not physical correctness, and that a model can repaint a scene faithfully and still be wrong about the physics. Resolution differences across providers remain a confound, which is why they standardize most comparisons at the lowest native resolution in the pool and treat the higher resolution results as supplementary. And one and a half million synthetic Blender samples is a lot of data, but it is a narrow slice of the physical world, built around a handful of clean, laboratory-grade tasks. The claim is much more modest, and much more useful: here is a way to ask whether a world model has these two specific priors, here is the data to train them, and here is a first map of where fourteen current models stand. If video models are going to be world models, someone had to build the exam, and this is a solid one, with the data and the training stack to go with it.
