OmniEcho, Audio-Visual Spatial Understanding for Omni-Modal Embodied Agents. The first three authors are Ruixun Liu, Yuxuan Wang, and Jiacheng Xie, with the work coming out of Peking University's State Key Laboratory of General Artificial Intelligence, Alibaba's Token Hub, and Tsinghua University; part of the work was done during an internship at Alibaba.

Humans do something with their ears that embodied agents still cannot: localize. You can close your eyes, hear a chair scrape, and turn your head in the right direction, then fuse that with what you see to reason about the room. The paper's starting point is that we do not actually know how to measure or model this in an agent setting. Most spatial audio research is done on synthetic, anechoic, or single-source data, and most embodied navigation research ignores audio entirely, treating the world as something you can only see. OmniEcho attacks both halves at once: a benchmark to measure spatial audio-visual understanding, and a model to learn it.

The benchmark, OmniEchoBench, is built on real data, which is the hard part. It spans one hundred and ninety-seven real-world spatial audio-visual scenes, with two thousand nine hundred and seventy-two question-answer pairs and nine hundred navigation samples. The audio is recorded in first-order ambisonics, a four-channel format that captures sound from all directions, collected from thirty real-world environments. The perception side is split into six tasks, and the names tell you exactly what is being probed: camera rotation reasoning, where the model has to tell whether the listener turned left or right; source direction, the coarse question of which way a sound is coming from; three-dimensional localization, which adds elevation and distance on top of direction; source motion, whether the sound is moving and in which direction; a cognitive-map task, where the model must build a spatial layout of the room from a sequence of observations; and the overall composite. The navigation side follows the InternNav setting, where the agent takes a sound as its goal, rather than a text instruction, and must walk to the source while deciding on its own when to stop.

The model, also called OmniEcho, is an omni-modal system built on a thirty billion parameter Qwen3 omni backbone. Its core addition is a first-order ambisonics spatial encoder, a new pathway that reads the four ambisonic channels and extracts spatial information, running alongside the backbone's pretrained semantic audio pathway, which handles what is being said or made. The two are meant to be complementary: semantics tells you the sound is a keyboard, the spatial encoder tells you it is to your left and slightly behind you. Training happens in three stages. Stage one aligns the new FOA encoder to the semantic space, so the spatial features and the semantic features can talk to each other. Stage two trains a query-conditioned localization head that, given a description of a target sound, attends over the encoder and predicts that source's azimuth, elevation, and distance. Stage three integrates the whole thing into the omni backbone, and the authors freeze the FOA encoder during this stage, an ablation finding that freezing preserves the spatial representations learned in the first two stages instead of letting multimodal integration overwrite them. The final stage-three run took about four days on thirty-two A100 GPUs, which is a modest compute footprint for a paper of this scope.

The data pipeline is worth a paragraph on its own, because it is what makes the whole thing reproducible. The authors build a controllable spatial audio renderer that takes a scene, source trajectories, and listener poses, and synthesizes the corresponding four-channel ambisonic signal while keeping the geometry consistent between the sound, the video, and the agent's path. On top of the real recordings they generate a synthetic training corpus of roughly one hundred and forty-five thousand samples, using an LLM as a director to vary the scenes, and they check that the synthetic distribution matches the real benchmark along three axes, source type, scene layout, and motion type, reporting the distance between the two distributions rather than just asserting they are close. The ablations on the model side back up the design choices. Dropping the spatial encoder, or switching from feature-based spatial input to raw ambisonic waveform input, both cost performance, and the full configuration reaches the best overall score of twenty-eight point five on their perception evaluation.

On the perception side, OmniEcho reports state-of-the-art results on spatial audio-visual understanding, the question-answering tasks over the real scenes. The numbers behind that claim are the most interesting part of the table. On the four-channel ambisonic plus vision setting, the model scores forty-one point seven on camera rotation reasoning, twenty-two point six on source direction, twenty-four point seven on source motion, and forty-seven point five on the cognitive map task, for an overall of twenty-eight point five, which beats every other system they test, including a seven billion parameter omni baseline that scores twelve point six overall. Two of those subtask numbers deserve attention, though, because they are low in absolute terms. Source direction at twenty-two point six and, worse, three-dimensional localization at fourteen point two. Direction, the coarse question of which quadrant a sound comes from, is a problem humans solve almost effortlessly, and a model that gets it wrong three quarters of the time is not really localizing; it is guessing among a few options. And the fourteen point two on full three-dimensional localization, which must recover elevation and distance in addition to direction, is the number that tells you exactly where the hard part of the problem still is. The model's own ablation confirms it: the frozen spatial encoder is what lifts the score from the native-audio-only baseline, so the architecture is doing real work, but the underlying spatial signal in a single ambisonic capture is limited, and distance in particular is underdetermined by the four channels.

The navigation result is more mixed, and the paper is honest about it. On the simulated VLN-CE R2R benchmark, OmniEcho reaches a success rate of twenty-two point two percent, which slightly exceeds the classic sequence-to-sequence and CMA baselines, and it has the shortest trajectory length of the group at five point five four, which makes sense: if you can hear where the target is, you walk less. But its path efficiency, the SPL score of sixteen point three, is below CMA's twenty point zero, and the navigation quality score, NE, of seven point three one, while better than the older baselines, still trails the stronger text-guided systems. The authors' own reading: sound-guided navigation has closed most of the gap to traditional vision-language navigation in raw success rate, but there is a clear remaining gap in path efficiency, and fine-grained spatial localization, especially distance estimation, is the open problem that is holding it back.

Why does this matter beyond the numbers? Because audio is an almost free sensor. Cameras are expensive, power-hungry, and blind in the dark; a microphone can tell you where a sound is without any light at all, which is exactly the regime where vision-language agents go quiet. The paper's broader argument is that spatial audio is a first-class signal for embodied reasoning, not a curiosity, and that the bottleneck is not the signal but our inability to evaluate and model it cleanly. The benchmark gives the field that clean measuring stick, and the fact that the perception numbers are so uneven, strong on the reasoning tasks, weak on raw localization, tells you exactly where the next round of research should aim. The model shows what a spatial encoder plus a semantic pathway can do on a strong omni-modal backbone, and the released synthetic renderer plus the matching-distribution check makes the pipeline reproducible, which is more than most benchmark papers offer. The limitations, honestly stated, are that the scenes are still a small number of real rooms, that distance estimation remains weak, and that the navigation gains, while real, are modest against the strongest text-guided systems.
