EvoTeam, Multi-Agent Collaboration via Evolving Teams, from Jilin University, the Nanjing University of Science and Technology, and the Shanghai AI Lab. The first three authors are Qinuo Cao, Xin He, and Dianxuan Fu. This paper attacks a specific gap in multi-agent systems research. A lot of work on experience-driven evolution improves individual agents: they learn from prior trajectories, refine prompts, acquire reusable skills, and get better at single-agent tasks over time. But evolving better individual agents does not necessarily produce better teams. Collaboration depends on how complementary capabilities are organized and coordinated toward a shared objective. Even fully competent agents fail when responsibility assignments are wrong, information handoffs are incomplete, or verification is missing. EvoTeam's argument is that evolution has to extend from what individual agents can do to how the team organizes, communicates, and collaborates.

The design splits experience into two kinds that evolve separately. Individual skills are reusable across teams: a planning skill, a code-review skill, a verification skill, things that attach to an agent role regardless of who else is on the team. Collaboration experience lives in team contexts: the learned rules for how a particular team configuration routes work, hands off, and checks its own output. The core mechanism is team-indexed evolution. Instead of one global experience pool, the system indexes accumulated collaboration experience by team, so that experience accumulates within compatible team contexts while individual skills remain portable across teams. When a new task arrives, the system selects an existing compatible team or creates a new one, runs the task, reflects, and updates both the team's indexed collaboration rules and the individual skills involved. The team selection step is what makes the experience reusable: similar tasks route to similar teams, which carry the right accumulated coordination rules.

The evaluation is broad, which is where the paper earns its keep. Four benchmarks covering genuinely different collaboration requirements: GAIA, a general assistant benchmark measured by answer accuracy; LOCA-Bench, stateful application interaction through MCP, measured by final-state success; LoCoBench, code feature implementation and cross-file refactoring, measured by a functional-correctness-and-quality score; and BeyondSWE, repository-level software maintenance, measured by resolution rate with required tests passing without regressions. All metrics are normalized to a zero-to-hundred scale. The default backbone is GPT-5.6-Luna, with averages over three independent rollouts per instance, and evolution and evaluation use disjoint splits. The baselines are the current frontier of experience-driven multi-agent methods: AgentSquare, ReCreate, Meta-Team, Skill-MAS, and OWL, all run under the same evolution and held-out protocol so that the comparison isolates the collaboration-evolution mechanism rather than differences in compute or model.

The headline results. Under individual-benchmark evolution, EvoTeam achieves the highest average score of fifty-eight point eight five and leads on four of the six evaluation metrics. Its margin over Meta-Team, the strongest competing method by average, reaches five points on GAIA, seventy-eight point zero zero against seventy-three point zero zero. It also beats the unevolved multi-agent baseline on every metric. Under mixed-benchmark evolution, where all four evolution splits are interleaved in a shuffled sequence, EvoTeam ranks first on all six metrics with an average of fifty-nine point five eight, beating Meta-Team by four point zero three. And notably, its average improves under the mixed setting while Meta-Team's declines, which is the paper's evidence that team-indexed experience composes across heterogeneous tasks better than pooled individual experience does.

The cross-backbone transfer result is the most surprising. They evolve the collaboration artifacts with one backbone and evaluate with another, using DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and GPT-5.6-Luna in both directions. EvoTeam beats the unevolved multi-agent baseline in all six transfer configurations. On GAIA, the gain from transfer is five points when GPT evolved and DeepSeek evaluated, and eighteen points in the other direction. And cross-backbone performance stays within four points of same-backbone performance in every paired comparison: seventy-seven point zero zero versus seventy-eight point zero zero on GAIA for the GPT-evaluation case. The claim is that the evolved collaboration rules are backbone-agnostic, that they encode structure about how to organize work rather than quirks of one model. That is a strong and genuinely useful claim if it holds.

The ablations complete the picture. They strip the system in two stages: remove the team-specific interaction rules but keep the reusable agent teams, and remove both. Across the six benchmark settings, the full framework performs best in five of six, and it beats the fully stripped variant in all six. The rule ablation costs the most on GAIA and LOCA, where the learned handoff rules are worth one point zero zero and one point zero eight points respectively, with smaller losses on the coding tasks, and even with no learned rules at all, keeping just the reusable agent teams still beats the fully unevolved baseline. So the two components are doing different work: the teams give you a reusable organization matched to the task, and the interaction rules give you the accumulated knowledge about how those teams actually hand work off across successive executions. The cost analysis is the other quiet win. Under individual-benchmark evolution, EvoTeam achieves the highest score at the lowest estimated cost per task on GAIA and both LoCoBench tasks, and on BeyondSWE DepMig it outperforms Meta-Team and ReCreate at a lower cost, while the cheaper baselines like AgentSquare and OWL score lower. So the reuse mechanisms are buying performance and efficiency at the same time, which directly answers the overhead objection: team-indexed bookkeeping is not costing extra, it is paying for itself.

The skeptical read. The baselines are fair and current, and the disjoint evolution-evaluation splits rule out the most obvious form of overfitting. But there are real open questions. First, the margins over Meta-Team, four to five points on average, are meaningful but not decisive, and Meta-Team is a strong experience-driven baseline from the same research lineage, so the paper is benchmarking its own line of work. Second, the cross-backbone transfer is the thinnest-evidenced claim: two backbones, three tasks, and a within-four-points stability result from a small set of paired comparisons. Third, the two ablation exceptions are small but telling: removing the rules slightly improves one LoCoBench refactoring setting, and removing both components beats removing rules alone on one BeyondSWE migration setting, which suggests the learned rules are occasionally noise rather than signal, at least for some task families.

What to take away if you want to implement the idea. The two-level split is the portable concept: separate task-level skills that attach to roles from team-level coordination rules that attach to team configurations, and index the latter by team so experience is reused where it applies. The team-selection step is the second: route incoming tasks to compatible existing teams rather than always composing a fresh team, so that coordination experience has a place to accumulate. The paper's own evaluation split is worth copying too: an evolution stream of one hundred sixteen instances across the benchmarks, a frozen final system evaluated on held-out groups with native metrics, and fixed per-case time, message, and cost budgets so the methods compete on the same footing. And the final piece is cross-backbone transfer as a probe for whether your learned coordination is structure or model-specific habit.

The verdict. EvoTeam makes a well-supported case that multi-agent systems need to evolve their collaboration structure, not just their individual members, and the team-indexed experience design is a clean, implementable mechanism. The margins are solid rather than spectacular, but the cost analysis shows the reuse overhead paying for itself, and the cross-backbone transfer result is the standout finding: learned coordination rules appear to be properties of the team structure, not of the specific model running it. For anyone running persistent multi-agent pipelines, the question to ask after reading this is whether your system's experience is indexed by agent, by task, or by team, and whether your teams are being re-derived from scratch on every run.
