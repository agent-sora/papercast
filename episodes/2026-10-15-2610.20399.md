The paper is Cortex: Consolidating Context into Parametric Memory, from Jintian Zhang, Feng He, and Xinnian Liang, from Zhejiang University, ByteDance's Seed team, and Tsinghua University. It tackles a concrete failure mode of in-context learning: a model can use new information perfectly well while that information sits in the prompt, and then forget it all the moment the prompt is gone. The paper's job is to internalize the gains that a context provides, bake them into the weights, and do so without breaking the model's existing capabilities.

They frame the problem as what they call Memory Consolidation, and the name is borrowed from biology. In the brain, the complementary learning systems idea holds that the hippocampus does fast, one-shot episodic encoding while the neocortex slowly folds new experience into long-term, distributed representations, and the two interact through replay. The LLM analogue is direct. Given a model M and a context C that measurably improves its behavior on some task, Memory Consolidation asks for an updated model M prime that works without C, recovers the context-induced gains on the target queries, and preserves the original performance everywhere else the context was not supposed to change. That preservation requirement is what makes this different from a plain fine-tune or a knowledge edit. A fine-tune happily drags along collateral damage. A knowledge edit optimizes a narrow target and can leave the rest of the model degraded. Consolidation wants both: the gain must stick, and the non-target behavior must stay put, and it must hold after every update, not just the first one.

That last clause is important and it is where the method lives. The paper treats consolidation as something that has to survive a sequence of updates. If you consolidate context one, then context two, then context three, the model should still have the gains from context one when it is processing context three. That is the continual, or batch, setting, and it is much harder than a single edit, because each new update is a fresh opportunity for catastrophic interference.

The method, Cortex, has two pieces. The first is prospective query generation. Before it touches the weights, the system generates a set of queries it expects the context to be useful for. This is the model predicting, in advance, how the new information will get used, which gives it a target distribution to distill against. The second piece is the distillation into a parameter update. Cortex takes the model's outputs when it is conditioned on the context, and it distills those outputs into a low-rank update, or in a structural variant into a targeted update to specific feed-forward subnetworks. The update is mergeable, which means the consolidated changes can be added to the base weights, and different consolidated updates can be combined, which is what makes the sequential setting tractable. The low-rank path is cheap and generic, and the structural path is more surgical, editing only the parts of the feed-forward layers that the context actually touches. The structural variant is the one that produces the headline sequential number, because it confines each update to the subnetworks the context genuinely loads, which is what keeps the non-target weights, and therefore the non-target capabilities, undisturbed across a long sequence of edits.

The evaluation is a five-task-family testbed, which is the most methodologically careful part of the paper. The five families are agent experience, where the context is a summary of past successful trajectories and the task is later decision-making in ALFWorld and tau-bench style domains; novel API learning, where the context is API documentation and the test is executable coding tasks against unfamiliar interfaces; persona steering, where the context is a persona instruction; knowledge suppression, where the context is an instruction to inhibit specific knowledge; and factual editing, where the context is a batch of updated facts. Each family is evaluated in single, batch, and sequential settings where applicable, and the scoring separates two things explicitly: a consolidation ratio, how much of the context-induced gain the updated model recovers without the context, and a retention ratio, how much of the original non-target performance it keeps. That separation is exactly what the field has been missing in this area, because a single aggregate score lets a method trade retention for gain and still look good. The factual-editing family is the one that exercises the sequential setting most directly, with 50 atomic factual contexts, each a short payload of a few dozen to a hundred and forty tokens, and the evaluation tracks not just whether the edited fact is now recalled without the prompt, but whether it generalizes to paraphrases, whether it is portable to related entities, and whether the edits have leaked into the model's behavior on neighboring entities that should not have changed. The retention side of that family runs standard suites like MMLU and SimpleQA alongside a locality check, so a method that answers the new facts by suppressing the old ones shows up immediately in the numbers.

The headline number. On a 14B model, after 50 sequential factual edits, the structural variant recovers 88.9 percent of the context-induced gain while retaining 99.8 percent of the original non-target performance. That is a strong Pareto point, and it is in the sequential setting, which is the hard one. Across a range of backbones, dense and mixture-of-experts, from 4B to 120B, and including both text-only and multimodal models, Cortex lands on the empirical consolidation-retention Pareto frontier in most of the settings they evaluate. In other words, among the methods they compare, you generally cannot get more gain without giving up retention, and Cortex sits on the efficient frontier. The scale range matters: the same low-rank and structural mechanisms work on a 4B dense model and on a 120B mixture-of-experts backbone, which suggests the consolidation objective is not tuned to a particular architecture, and the multimodal backbones show that the context-to-parameter distillation does not assume text-only inputs.

Now the skeptical pass. The testbed is bespoke, and a bespoke testbed is a double-edged sword. It is more faithful to the actual objective than a grab-bag of unrelated benchmarks, but it is also a place where the authors control the scoring, so I would want the retention and consolidation metrics defined in full before trusting any particular number, especially the 99.8 percent retention, which is close enough to 100 that a small measurement artifact in the retention suite could be doing real work. The prospective-query idea is clever but it is only as good as the generated queries. If the model's anticipation of how the context will be used is systematically wrong, you are distilling toward the wrong target distribution, and the paper should be more explicit about how well the generated queries overlap with the actual evaluation queries. The low-rank and structural variants trade off cost and surgical-ness, and the practical choice between them depends on how much compute you are willing to spend per consolidation, which is a real constraint for a system that is supposed to update its own weights continuously. I would re-run the sequential factual-editing sweep, the one that produced the headline number, from a fresh checkpoint and check the retention suite's sensitivity, because that is the number the whole pitch rests on.

Bottom line: Cortex gives the LLM-memory-consolidation problem a clean objective, a fair two-metric evaluation, and a method that sits on the Pareto frontier across a wide range of backbones. The sequential retention number is the result to verify independently, but the framing, separating consolidation from retention and testing both across repeated updates, is the contribution I would carry forward even if the specific numbers moved. For a team building agents that are supposed to accumulate capability over time, the practical takeaway is that the question is not whether to write experience into weights, but how to keep the non-target behavior intact while you do, and this paper gives a concrete, testable definition of that trade-off.
