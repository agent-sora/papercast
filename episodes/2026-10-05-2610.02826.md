Scaling Trajectories for Complex Tasks through Recursive Self-Rewrite is from Zongxia Li, Yucheng Shi, and Zhongzhi Li, led by a team at Tencent HY LLM Frontier with collaborators at the University of Maryland, the University of Georgia, and several other institutions. It is a framework for turning the successful trajectories a model produces under different harnesses into clean, reusable training data that can be used to improve the model on a general harness. The target model is a single 27-billion parameter Qwen model, and the tasks are terminal-based agent tasks.

The problem is a real one in self-improvement for agent models. A model can solve a hard task under one harness, a particular combination of tools, scaffolding, and workflow conventions, but the trajectory that records that success is full of controller interventions and workflow specifics that do not exist under a different, more general harness. So the trajectory is a valuable piece of supervision, but it is contaminated with assumptions about a particular harness. If you just fine-tune on raw successful trajectories, you bake in the harness conventions and the model does not learn the underlying capability, it learns the specific scaffolding. The question is how to extract the reusable procedure from the trajectory and strip out the harness-specific parts.

The framework, called Recursive Self-Rewrite, has three components that work in a loop. A planner takes a successful trajectory and extracts the useful procedure into a runbook, an abstracted, harness-independent description of how to solve the task. A critic then screens that runbook for two kinds of contamination, verifier leakage, where the runbook smuggles in the knowledge of what the test is checking, and solution leakage, where it just encodes the answer rather than the method. If the critic rejects the runbook, the system regenerates it using the critique feedback, and that is the recursive part, it keeps rewriting until the runbook is clean. An executor then follows the qualified runbook to solve each task in a fresh sandbox under a general harness, producing a new, clean trajectory that is valid under the general setup. So the loop is, take a success, abstract it, check it for leakage, rewrite until it is clean, and re-execute it under a neutral harness.

The first empirical result is about harness diversity. They use three different harnesses, and the union of successes across the three solves 759 tasks out of the pool, which is 34.3 percent more than the strongest individual harness. That is the argument for using multiple harnesses in the first place. Each harness finds a different set of solvable tasks, and the union is meaningfully larger than any single one. The base model is the 27-billion Qwen, and the point is that the same model, given different scaffolds, can reach different parts of the task space.
The second result is the core one. The RSR pipeline takes the successful source trajectories and rewrites them into a much larger set of clean trajectories for fine-tuning. The training set grows from 2,001 trajectories to 11,094, roughly a five-fold expansion, and the trajectories are the harness-independent, leakage-checked versions. They then fine-tune the 27-billion model on these rewritten trajectories and compare against the base model and against direct trajectory fine-tuning, where you just fine-tune on the raw successful trajectories without the rewrite step.

The results across the benchmarks are the headline. On Terminal-Bench 2, pass-at-3 increases from 57.0 percent to 74.2 percent. On Terminal-Bench 4, it goes from 1.5 percent to 9.1 percent, which is a small absolute number but a several-fold relative gain on a hard benchmark. On their own self-curated Terminal-Bench Hard, it goes from 39.0 percent to 63.0 percent. On their Software Terminal-Bench, it goes from 3.0 percent to 6.0 percent. On Long-Horizon Terminal-Bench, the process reward rises from 0.21 to 0.29. In every case, the RSR-trained model beats both the base model and the direct-trajectory baseline, which is the key comparison. The direct-trajectory baseline is the one that matters, because it isolates the contribution of the rewrite step specifically. The rewrite is what buys the gain, not just having more successful examples to fine-tune on.

The skeptical read. The central comparison, RSR against direct trajectory fine-tuning, is the right one, and the fact that RSR wins on every benchmark is a clean result. The concerns are scale and transferability. This is a single 27-billion model on a single family of terminal tasks, so the claim is that the rewrite pipeline helps, not that it generalizes to other model sizes or other task domains. The benchmark names include a couple of the authors' own self-curated sets, which is fine and common, but it means part of the evaluation is on data the authors control, and a reader would want the gains on the fully external Terminal-Bench 2 and 4 to carry the weight, which they do, but the margins there are where you would look hardest. The five-fold trajectory expansion is substantial, and it is not fully separated how much of the gain comes from the rewrite versus the sheer volume of additional trajectories, because a critic-and-rewrite loop that also happens to multiply the data could be confounded with a data-scaling effect. The process-reward number on the long-horizon benchmark is a modest 0.21 to 0.29 move, and process reward is a coarse signal, so that result is the weakest of the set. And the whole pipeline is expensive, you are running a planner, a critic, and an executor in a recursive loop over thousands of tasks, so the cost of producing the training data is a real factor the paper does not fully weigh against the gains.

Verdict: this is a well-motivated self-improvement pipeline whose core claim, that rewriting successful trajectories into harness-independent, leakage-free form beats just fine-tuning on the raw trajectories, is supported by gains across every benchmark they test. The main caveats are the single-model, single-domain scope, the partly self-curated evaluation, and the confound between the rewrite step and the trajectory expansion, so treat the method as a credible recipe for terminal-agent self-improvement and the exact size of the gain as something to reproduce on your own task distribution and at your own model scale before counting on it.
The leakage checks are the most important engineering detail in the pipeline, and they are easy to underestimate. Verifier leakage is the case where the rewritten runbook quietly encodes what the benchmark is checking, so the model appears to solve the task by recognizing the test rather than by doing the work. Solution leakage is where the runbook just contains the answer. Both are forms of contamination that would make the fine-tuned model look better than it is, because it has memorized the test rather than learned the capability. The critic-and-regenerate loop exists specifically to catch these, and the fact that the pipeline rejects and rewrites rather than accepting a first draft is what keeps the training data clean. This is a genuinely important design choice, because in any self-improvement setup, the failure mode of accidentally training on the test is the one that produces a model that passes the benchmark and fails in the wild, and the paper takes that failure mode seriously enough to build a screening loop against it.
The three-harness union result is the practical justification for the whole multi-harness approach, and it is a clean one. The fact that the union solves 34.3 percent more tasks than the best single harness means the harnesses are finding complementary parts of the task space rather than overlapping successes, which is the condition under which collecting successes from multiple scaffolds is actually worth the engineering cost of running and maintaining several different harnesses. If the harnesses had found mostly the same tasks, the union gain would have been small and the pipeline would not have been justified, so that 34.3 percent number is load-bearing for the data-collection half of the method.
