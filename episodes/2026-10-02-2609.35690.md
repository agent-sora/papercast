Agent Priors-guided Policy Learning, or APPL. The first three authors are Puming Jiang, Tianrun Hu, and Haozhe Du, all working at the National University of Singapore, with the remaining co-authors spread across the same group.

The problem is robot learning from a handful of demonstrations, where two kinds of generalization have to happen at once. Compositional generalization recombines skills to solve new tasks, and skill generalization makes the policy behind each skill work in situations it has not seen. The two depend on each other, but information is lost between them. Where a skill actually works is determined by the structure its policy was trained with, yet the composer never sees that structure. It sees the skill only through a separate description, a name, an instruction, or a symbolic operator, and that description omits the very assumptions that bound the skill's reach. So composition can call a skill in a situation where the skill's own training assumptions no longer hold, and nothing catches it. The closest precedent the paper points to is SymSkill, which jointly learns predicates and operators, but it fixes the policy structure as a design decision rather than treating it as something that can be proposed per skill and verified against evidence.

The key idea is to use each policy's structural prior as part of the interface between composition and the skill. A structural prior states what a behavior depends on. For example, a grasp depends only on the gripper's pose relative to the object, not on where the object sits in the world. When that prior is built into training, it shapes where the policy generalizes. When it is stated in language, it tells the composer where the policy is expected to apply. APPL instantiates this with two roles. A construction agent takes complete demonstrations, segments them into reusable skills, proposes several structural priors for each skill, and then trains and verifies one diffusion policy per prior. A runtime agent then selects among these prior-specific policies and composes them toward new task goals, using the stated interfaces to check whether a given state falls inside a policy's hypothesized applicability region.

The prior is more than a training flag; it is a structured record. For a representation-based prior, the construction agent specifies a transformed observation, the relevant coordinates the policy actually reads, and a language description of the hypothesized applicability region. Each prior therefore produces a separate conditional diffusion policy trained on demonstrations, and each stored policy carries its prior's stated interface plus verification evidence gathered by running the policy from demonstrated entry states and checking where it succeeds. The deployment objective is not available at construction time, so the language description of where the policy applies is what the runtime composer actually reasons over. That is the contract: the same assumption that made the skill generalize during training is the one that bounds the applicability check at runtime. The implementation detail worth noting is that the construction agent is allowed to propose several priors per skill and keep the one that verifies best, which means the prior is selected by evidence rather than asserted, so a wrong structural guess is filtered out before the policy is ever deployed rather than discovered at runtime.

The experiments test both roles of the prior. On six adapted MetaWorld tasks, the baselines are a vanilla diffusion policy, which they call B0, and B1, which adds a learned encoding of three predefined role-relative vectors, so it is one fixed relational prior. The headline is few-demonstration out-of-distribution skill generalization. With two demonstrations, APPL's best policy reaches 89.6 percent out-of-distribution success, against 37.9 percent for the fixed relational prior and about 29 percent for vanilla. That margin holds at every demonstration budget they test, and the first proposal alone already beats B1 by 18 to 22 percentage points, so the gain is not an artifact of the verification loop.

What the agent designs and what the feedback adds are separate questions, and the paper reports them separately. The first proposed prior, without any verification or selection, already clears B1 by a wide margin, which says the structural assumption itself is doing work. Adding the verification evidence and usage notes then adds a further increment, which says the interface is doing work on top of the prior. The distinction matters for how you would adopt this: if you only had the prior and no runtime verification, you would still capture most of the skill-generalization gain, whereas the composition gain, which requires the runtime composer to check applicability regions and choose among policies, is where the verification evidence and the language interface carry most of the weight.

The second experiment is five long-horizon ManiSkill tasks, each with twelve successful demonstrations. Under shifted object configurations, APPL gets 50.0 percent success where the full-task diffusion policy gets 10.0 percent, and on task-level variants with intermediate starts or requested early termination it reaches 92.5 percent. On out-of-distribution skill composition, APPL solves 8 of 16 previously unseen skill pairings, against 3 of 16 for the baseline that has the same verified policy library but no interface information. The ablation is the part that most directly supports the mechanism. Removing the interface information drops motion out-of-distribution success from 50.0 to 20.0 percent, task-level success from 92.5 to 65.0, and composition from 8 of 16 to 2 of 16. Replacing the runtime agent with a fixed rule gives 22.5, 52.5, and 1 of 16. So both the interface and the runtime selection matter, and the shift robustness difference is statistically significant, with paired McNemar p-values below 0.001 against both diffusion policy baselines.

Skeptically, this is a small task set, six MetaWorld and five ManiSkill tasks, and the composition benchmark is only 16 pairings, so 8 versus 3 is a difference of five tasks, which is directionally clear but statistically thin. The structural priors are proposed by an agent and then verified, but verification is done from demonstrated entry states, and the paper itself notes that verification scores can misrank policies under shift, which means the interface is only as good as the assumptions it encodes, and a wrong prior is baked into both the policy and the stated applicability. One of the ablations, SinglePrior, which learns one full-task policy with an agent-designed prior but no composition agent, is the right comparison to watch, because it separates the contribution of the structural prior from the contribution of the runtime composer, and the paper reports that the interface information, not the raw prior, is what drives most of the composition gain. The honest framing is that this is a clean proof of concept that training-time structural assumptions can serve double duty, as inductive bias and as a compositional contract, not a general recipe that scales to open-world manipulation.

A practical consequence of the design is that the policy library grows with the number of priors per skill rather than with the number of skills, which changes the cost structure of scaling the system. If each skill yields several candidate priors, each with its own verified policy, then the runtime composer is selecting from a larger pool than the number of skills would suggest, and the applicability checking has to be fast enough to be useful at the frequency a robot is re-planning. The paper does not report the runtime cost of the composer's applicability checks, so the practical latency of the composition step is an open question, and a system that is correct but slow to compose is not a system a robot can use in a time-limited setting. That said, the verification evidence and the usage notes are a structured form of documentation for the policy library, which is more than most skill-learning systems produce, and it is a reasonable artifact to hand to a downstream operator or a higher-level planner.

The bottom line: APPL uses each skill's structural prior both to shape where its policy generalizes and to tell the composer where that policy applies, and on MetaWorld and ManiSkill that lifts few-demonstration out-of-distribution success and enables skill compositions a library-only baseline cannot reach. The mechanism is well isolated by ablation, but the evidence is small-scale, so treat it as a promising bridge between skill learning and skill composition rather than a solved generalization problem.
