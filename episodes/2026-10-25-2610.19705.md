RouteWeaver: Weaving Mode Selection and Execution into Unified LLM Routing, by Xiaohan Wang, Haozhen Zhang, and Qingyuan Liu, from Nanyang Technological University, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign.

The problem this paper addresses is LLM routing: given a pool of heterogeneous language models with different strengths, costs, and latency profiles, decide which one or ones to call for a given query. Existing routers tend to commit to a single execution paradigm. Single-round routing picks one model and fires one inference. Multi-round routing chains several calls, conditioning each next model on the intermediate output. Agentic routing lets a controller model orchestrate a sequence of tool-augmented calls. Each paradigm has its sweet spot. Math problems often need one strong call. Knowledge questions can be answered by a cheap model. Complex reasoning may need multiple rounds with self-correction. The paper's argument is that hard-coding the paradigm into the router limits adaptation, so the router should jointly learn which paradigm to use and how to execute within it.

RouteWeaver unifies all three paradigms into a single policy. The policy is an LLM itself, prompted to first emit a mode token choosing single-round, multi-round, or agentic execution, and then emit the model selections within that mode. The training objective is the core contribution. They develop COMET-GRPO, which stands for COordinated Mode-Execution Training. Standard GRPO computes a single trajectory-level advantage for the whole rollout. COMET-GRPO decomposes that advantage into two separate signals: one for the mode-selection decision and one for the within-mode execution decisions. The idea is that credit assignment should differ across the two decision levels. A bad mode choice poisons the entire trajectory regardless of how well the within-mode selections went, and a bad within-mode choice is only as bad as the mode that was chosen. By separating the gradients, the mode selector learns when each paradigm is appropriate without being derailed by execution noise, and the execution policy learns model selection within a fixed paradigm without being penalized for the mode choice.

On top of COMET-GRPO they layer progressive exploration. Early in training, the mode is externally assigned: the paper feeds the router a fixed mode and only trains the within-mode execution. As training proceeds, the fraction of externally assigned modes decays, and the policy gradually takes over autonomous mode selection. The rationale is that a router that has not yet learned how to execute each mode well will make poor mode decisions, and the mode-selection signal gets contaminated by execution errors. Progressive exploration forces the execution policy to stabilize first, then opens the mode-selection channel. The ablation confirms this matters: without progressive exploration, the routing policy collapses entirely to single-round execution, as if the model finds it easier to avoid the harder mode-selection decision than to get it right.

The implementation details worth noting: the router policy is a single LLM, so the entire routing decision, mode choice and model selections, is a single forward pass emitting a structured output. The worker models themselves are not modified; the router treats them as a fixed menu. This is a design choice that keeps the system easy to update, since adding or retiring a worker model only requires changing the router's menu, not retraining the workers. The GRPO training runs on a mixture of the five evaluation domains, and the advantage decomposition uses the same trajectory sampling as standard GRPO, so the compute overhead of COMET relative to vanilla GRPO is small. The mode token is a lightweight addition to the vocabulary, and the within-mode selections are emitted as the names of worker models, which means the router's output is directly interpretable: you can read off which mode was chosen and which models were called for any given query, a property that matters for debugging routing failures in production.

Evaluation spans five domains: mathematics, coding, knowledge, logical reasoning, and factual recall. The strongest fixed-paradigm baseline is Router-R1 at 55.3 percent average accuracy. RouteWeaver hits 61.4 percent, an 11.03 percent relative improvement over Router-R1. The relative improvements over the strongest single-round, multi-round, and agentic baselines individually are 26.08, 11.03, and 31.20 percent, respectively. The per-domain breakdown shows gains of 12.52 percent on math, 10.23 on coding, 12.47 on knowledge, 10.84 on reasoning, and 5.20 on factual recall. The strongest fixed-paradigm baseline actually changes across domains, which is exactly the heterogeneity the unified router is supposed to exploit.

There is a cost knob, parameterized by alpha, that trades accuracy for inference cost. At alpha zero, the router uses its default model selections and pays full cost. At alpha 0.1, worker inference cost drops by 23 percent with only a 1.0 point accuracy drop. At alpha 0.3, cost falls 61 percent with a 3.1 point drop. At alpha 0.5, cost falls 86 percent but accuracy drops 11.2 points. The held-out generalization test is interesting: on benchmarks not seen during training, RouteWeaver reaches 53.7 percent average, an 18.28 percent relative improvement over Router-R1 at 45.4 percent. The per-benchmark improvements are 11.4, 7.1, and 6.4 points. The mode distribution at inference is revealing: 99 percent of reasoning queries use multi-round execution, 72 percent of knowledge queries use single-round, and agentic execution accounts for 75 percent of AIME queries but only 12 percent of BBH queries. The router has learned genuinely different routing behaviors for different problem types, not just a uniform shift. One more detail on the held-out result: the held-out benchmarks use different problem distributions than the training mix, yet the routing behavior generalizes, with agentic execution dominating hard math and staying rare on the easier benchmark, which suggests the mode-selection policy learned problem-type features rather than memorizing benchmark-specific routing tables.

The ablation on the two COMET-GRPO components, run with both variants at 58.8 percent and 55.6 percent average, is worth dwelling on. Even the worse variant still exceeds the strongest fixed-paradigm baseline of 55.3 percent, which tells you that the unified-routing formulation itself carries most of the gain, and the COMET decomposition and progressive exploration refine it. The paper reports a 5.8 point edge for the full method over the best ablated variant, which they attribute to decision-level credit assignment being the binding constraint once the execution policy is stable.

The skeptical read: the baseline set is all router-level baselines, so the comparison is fair within that scope, but the paper does not compare against a simple ensemble that calls two or three models in parallel and takes the best answer, which is a natural competitive alternative for many production workloads. The five evaluation domains are standard but relatively clean; real routing traffic has noisy queries, ambiguous intent, and mixed difficulty that may stress the mode-selection channel differently. The COMET-GRPO decomposition is elegant but the hyperparameter sensitivity of the advantage split is not deeply explored. The progressive exploration schedule is described qualitatively; a practitioner reproducing this would need the exact decay curve and the warmup fraction. The 86 percent cost reduction at alpha 0.5 comes with an 11.2 point accuracy drop, which may or may not be acceptable depending on the deployment. If you are building a router for a cost-constrained API service, the alpha schedule gives you a concrete operating-point curve, which is more than most routing papers offer. If you are building a router for a high-stakes domain where the accuracy floor matters more than the cost ceiling, the default alpha-zero setting is what you would run, and the 11 percent relative gain over Router-R1 is the number to take to your team. The progressive exploration ablation is the most practically important finding: if you skip it, your router collapses to single-round and you have just built a fancy single-round selector with extra steps.
