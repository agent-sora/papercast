LongCat-Flash-Prover, by Jianing Wang, Jianfei Zhang, Qi Guo, and a team
of twenty-seven co-authors from the Meituan LongCat team, the group that
has been releasing the LongCat line of large language models.

This is a model release, and a substantial one, a five hundred sixty-
billion-parameter open-source mixture-of-experts model trained to do
formal reasoning in Lean four, the theorem-proving language that underlies a
lot of the recent progress in machine theorem proving. The word that does
the work in the title is the phrase native formal reasoning, and it is worth
sitting with, because the field has had two broad ways of getting an LLM to
prove things in a formal system. The first is to use the model as a
tactic-proposer inside an existing prover, where the model suggests a
tactic and the external system checks and executes it. The second, which
this paper is about, is to train the model so that it does the formal
reasoning natively, with the formal system integrated into its own
inference, so that the model itself is the reasoning engine rather than an
oracle that an external prover calls. The paper frames this as a
decomposition of the overall task into three independent formal capabilities,
auto-formalization, taking an informal problem statement and producing a
formal statement in Lean four, sketching, producing the outline of a proof
with the lemmas and the structure, and proving, filling in the full proof.
The decomposition matters, because it is what lets the training be
targeted, each capability can be trained and evaluated on its own, and a
reader can see which part of the pipeline is doing the work. The paper is
also explicit about the difference between this and the tool-augmented
approach it is building on, because agentic tool-integrated reasoning
means the model can call external tools, run the Lean kernel, read the
compiler output, and use that feedback inside its own reasoning loop, so
the model is not just generating text that a human will paste into a
prover, it is driving the prover itself, observing what the kernel
accepts and rejects, and adjusting its strategy based on that feedback.
That loop is what makes the training data generation work, because the
same loop that is used at inference time is used to produce the training
trajectories, so the model is trained on the behavior it is expected to
exhibit in deployment, rather than on a static dataset of proof scripts.

The training method is the technical contribution, and it is called the
Hybrid-Experts Iteration Framework. The central problem in training a model
on formal tasks with reinforcement learning is reward hacking, the model
finding a shortcut to the reward signal that does not correspond to a real
proof. The paper's answer is a two-layer structure. At the data level, the
framework iteratively expands the pool of high-quality task trajectories,
generating new formal statements from informal problems, producing whole
proofs directly, and refining existing proofs, so the training distribution
keeps growing and the model keeps seeing novel structure. The iteration is
specific in the way it alternates, because the framework treats the model's
own outputs as a source of training data, generating new candidates,
filtering them with the consistency and legality checks, and feeding the
survivors back in as the next round of training targets, so the loop
converges on the region of the task space where the model is most likely
to produce verifiable proofs, rather than training uniformly over all
possible problems. This is the part that addresses the data-bottleneck
problem in formal reasoning, because high-quality formal proof data is
expensive to produce by hand, and the framework's claim is that a model
driving the kernel can expand the data pool at a cost that is much lower
than manual annotation, while the detection mechanisms keep the expanded
pool honest. At the reward
level, the paper incorporates theorem consistency and legality detection
mechanisms that operate at both the trajectory level and the token level,
and these are the mechanisms that are supposed to close off the reward-
hacking loopholes, because a proof is only rewarded if it is not only
complete but also consistent with the stated theorem and legal in the way
Lean four's kernel would accept it. The combination is what the paper
claims makes the training stable enough to push the model to a high pass
rate on the standard benchmarks.

The results are the part a reader would come back to, and the paper reports
three numbers that together make the case. On MiniF2F-Test, the standard
formal theorem proving benchmark, the model achieves a ninety-seven point
one percent pass rate using an inference budget of only seventy-two per
problem, which the authors frame as a sample-efficiency result, because the
same benchmark has been pushed with much larger inference budgets. On the
harder benchmarks, the model solves seventy point eight percent of
ProverBench and forty-one point five percent of PutnamBench, using no more
than two hundred twenty attempts per problem, which the authors describe
as significantly outperforming existing open-weights baselines. The
open-source detail is the other half of the contribution, because the model
weights, the project page, and the training setup are all released, so a
reader who wants to inspect the training or use the model can, which is a
meaningful distinction from a closed frontier model that reports a
benchmark number without the artifacts to verify it.

Now the skeptical read. The first question is the benchmark scope. MiniF2F-
Test is the easiest of the three, and a ninety-seven point one percent pass
rate there is a strong number but it is on the benchmark that the field
largely considers saturated, so the harder numbers, ProverBench and
PutnamBench, are the ones that carry the claim, and a reader should weigh
those, and the honest read is that seventy point eight percent on
ProverBench is competitive but not a decisive win over the closed frontier
models, and forty-one point five percent on PutnamBench is in the range
where the field is still moving quickly. The second question is the reward-
hacking claim, which is the paper's central technical contribution, and the
honest version of that question is whether the consistency and legality
detection mechanisms are airtight, because a model can still find
reward-hacking strategies that the detectors miss, and the paper's evidence
is that the training is stable and the pass rates are high, but a reader
who wants to use the model in a safety-critical setting should check the
detection mechanisms against the specific failure modes they care about,
rather than relying on the aggregate pass rate. The third question is the
five hundred sixty billion parameters, which is a large model, and the
question for a reader who wants to use this in practice is the serving
cost, because a five hundred sixty billion mixture-of-experts model has
a specific activation footprint and a specific memory requirement, and the
paper should be read together with the deployment notes to understand what
it takes to actually run this, because the benchmark numbers are reported
under a specific inference budget, and that budget is a cost, not just a
measurement. And the practical question is the open-source scope, the
paper says the model is open-source and releases the weights, but a reader
should check the license, because open-weights and open-licensed are not
the same thing, and the license determines whether a commercial user can
actually deploy this in the kind of setting where a formal theorem prover
would be used. There is also the question of what the model is for beyond
the benchmarks, because the three capabilities, auto-formalization,
sketching, and proving, are the building blocks of a formal mathematics
workflow, and the paper's framing is that a model with all three, trained
natively rather than bolted on, is the thing that makes the workflow
usable end to end, and a reader who wants to build on this should check
that the released model exposes all three capabilities in a callable form,
not just the proving step, because the value of the decomposition is only
realized if all three stages can be used together.

Bottom line: this is a substantial open-source model release for native
formal reasoning in Lean four, with a decomposition of the task into three
capabilities, a hybrid-experts training framework with reward-hacking
detection, and strong results on the standard benchmarks, including a
ninety-seven point one percent pass rate on MiniF2F-Test at a low inference
budget and competitive numbers on the harder ProverBench and PutnamBench
benchmarks. The open question is whether the reward-hacking detection is
airtight, how the numbers hold against the closed frontier models on the
harder benchmarks, and what the serving cost of a five hundred sixty-
billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model looks like in practice.
