Clover-35B-A3B-Preview, from the Clover Team at Shanghai AI Laboratory, is a new mixture-of-experts diffusion language model with 35 billion total parameters but only 3 billion activated per token, and it ships with a thinking mode. That last detail matters: it is one of the first open diffusion-language-model releases to treat reasoning as a first-class generation path rather than an afterthought. The report is a technical report, in the sense that it describes a full model and its training pipeline rather than a single architectural idea, and it lands in the middle of a quiet but important shift. Diffusion-based language models, which generate text the way diffusion image models generate pixels, have been appearing for over a year now, mostly as research demos. Clover is positioned as a serious, broadly useful open foundation model, which is a bigger claim.

The architectural heart of the report is how the authors adapt a class of layers called Gated Delta Networks, a form of linear recurrence, to the block-diffusion setting. In this design, the model processes a block of tokens at a time. Inside the block it runs standard bidirectional softmax attention, so tokens can attend to each other in both directions. But it also carries a recurrent memory of everything generated before the block, and it reads that memory out through the same softmax attention that handles the current block. In other words, the long-range history and the current block compete for attention inside a single shared mechanism, rather than being stitched together by a separate handoff. The authors add a learnable block-size calibration and a separate, zero-initialized convolution branch to handle right context, so the model does not have to commit to a fixed block length or treat future tokens as an afterthought.

The reasoning about the architecture is where the report earns its attention. The authors are explicit that the hard part of block diffusion is the handoff between the recurrent memory of past context and the bidirectional attention over the current block. In most hybrid designs those two are separate mechanisms stitched together, and the seam is where information gets lost or where the block has to commit to a fixed size. Here, the memory readout and the current-block tokens enter the same softmax, so the model can dynamically decide, per position, how much to trust the long-range history versus the tokens it is generating right now. The learnable block-size calibration lets the model adapt how much of the block it processes per step, and the zero-initialized right-context convolution branch means the model starts out ignoring future tokens and learns to use them, which keeps training stable. The net effect is that the model behaves closer to a full-attention transformer in what it can see, while still getting the recurrent efficiency of processing a block at a time.

The benchmark results are the part that makes this a serious release rather than an architecture demo. On the agent evaluations, which are the ones that matter for the kind of work the model is built for, Clover reports 73.6 on SWE-bench Verified and 51.7 on Terminal-Bench 2.1, and the report claims a consistent lead across the seven agent benchmarks it evaluated. Those are the numbers that would matter to someone actually putting the model to work on software tasks, and they are the reason the report leans on the agent framing rather than the general-chat framing. On the general side, the report points to strong results on reasoning under constraints, instruction following, and long-context understanding, while being candid that competition math and standalone coding remain areas for improvement. The efficiency story is the second reason the report is worth reading, and it is more specific than the headline speed number suggests. Clover uses a form of self-speculative decoding, where the model drafts multiple tokens in a block and then verifies them, and the report compares it against a strong baseline, an accelerated version of a 35-billion parameter model, on a single high-end GPU. The key result is that Clover sustains high committed-token yields across the workloads it tested, and on agent replay and daily chat it beats the baseline on both the tokens committed per round and the overall throughput. The report is honest about the one workload where it does not win, a math benchmark where the higher cost of drafting offsets the advantage. The headline figure of 680 tokens per second on a single GB300 GPU is the number to remember, but the more important point is that the drafting capability develops alongside the model during training rather than being bolted on afterward, which the authors argue is why it generalizes across workloads better than a separately trained drafter.

There is a final piece that is easy to miss but worth calling out. The report describes a decision-model variant that generates answers to a set of related questions in a single denoising pass, rather than answering them one at a time. On an internal set of five hundred question groups, joint prediction raises the per-question accuracy from 90.37 to 93.88 percent and the all-correct group accuracy from 65.4 to 77.0 percent. That is a meaningful jump on the harder of the two metrics, and it points to a genuine strength of the diffusion formulation, namely that related outputs can be produced together because they share the denoising process. It is a small result on a small internal set, but it is the kind of capability that is hard to get from an autoregressive model and it hints at why the team is investing in this route.

The skeptical read has to start with the obvious caveat about a technical report with a team byline: there are no individual authors to hold accountable, and the evaluation harness, especially the in-house agent runs, is the team's own. The SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench numbers are reported with specific harnesses, and the report itself notes that different harnesses give different scores, so the absolute numbers are less important than the relative claim, which is that Clover leads on the agent benchmarks it chose. That is a fair and defensible claim, but it is a claim about the benchmarks selected, not a claim about the model's overall standing in the open-model field. The bigger open question is whether the block-diffusion architecture, with its shared-softmax handoff and self-speculative decoding, scales and generalizes the way the report suggests at the sizes and data budgets the team has not yet shown. On what is shown, though, Clover is one of the more credible open diffusion language model releases to date, and its combination of strong agentic numbers and genuinely high inference speed is the thing to watch. The reason the architecture deserves attention beyond the benchmark table is that it is the rare foundation-model report that explains, layer by layer, why each design choice is there. The GDN recurrent memory gives the model a bounded, fast state to carry across the sequence, the block diffusion gives it the ability to draft and refine a chunk of tokens in parallel rather than one at a time, and the shared-softmax handoff is the glue that makes the two coexist without the usual instability at the boundary between a recurrent step and a diffusion step. Most model reports present the final stack as a black box and let the numbers speak for themselves, but this one makes the reasoning explicit, and that is what turns it from a product announcement into something a researcher can actually build on. Whether the combination holds at the next scale up is the open question, and the preview framing is the authors being honest that the answer is not in yet. What makes the report stand out among the wave of open-model releases is that the efficiency is not a side effect of the benchmark choice. The speed comes from the architecture itself, from the bounded recurrent state and the parallel block diffusion, which means it should carry to any task the model is run on, not just the ones in the report. That is the property that distinguishes a genuinely fast model from a model that is fast on a particular benchmark, and it is the reason the release is worth tracking beyond the launch-week numbers.
