Lean Pool: An AI-Maintained Archive of Formalized Mathematics. The sole author is Vasily Ilin, from the University of Washington, and there is a detail in the paper that sets the tone for the whole thing: the human-written portion of the manuscript consists of a single page, and the author states plainly that the rest of the paper was produced almost entirely by AI.

The motivation is a bottleneck argument. AI systems are now generating mathematical arguments at a rate that exceeds the rate at which mathematicians can read and absorb them, and the fix, which the last year of frontier lab releases has validated, is formalization: you ship the argument with a Lean proof, and the Lean kernel mechanically checks it while the mathematics is still being understood. OpenAI's Ten Advances in mathematics and the finite-time blowup result for Navier-Stokes both shipped this way, long AI-generated arguments with accompanying Lean formalizations. But now the bottleneck moves. Once generation is commodified, verification and reuse become the constraint, and the specific constraint this paper addresses is maintenance. Formal proofs are not static documents. They are compiled code that depends on Mathlib, and Mathlib moves: every Lean or Mathlib release breaks a chunk of downstream code, and a formalization that is not actively re-verified against the current library is a formalization that is slowly rotting. Mathlib itself, the canonical shared library, grows at a roughly linear rate because it is gated by strict human review, and it does not contain the definitions and theorems needed for much of research-level mathematics. Lean Pool's proposal is to build the missing layer between individual formalization projects and Mathlib: a living archive where completed formalizations live together in one shared build environment, maintained by AI agents as the dependencies evolve, with the original authors' attribution preserved.

The admission rules are strict and worth reciting, because they are what make the archive trustworthy. Pooled projects must contain no sorry or admit, must introduce no axioms beyond the standard computational ones, classical choice, propext, and Quot.sound, must avoid mechanisms that bypass the repository's linters or resource limits, and must carry an Apache 2.0 or MIT license. Every project ships with a card recording its authors, upstream source, proof provenance, whether the proofs are human-written, AI-written, or mixed, and the main results with their informal statements. Only complete formalizations of named, known results are eligible, and open challenges are kept in a separate section. At the time of writing, the archive contains 211 completed projects, 3.2 million lines of Lean code, 194 thousand declaration commands, 837 registered main results, and 18 contributors. The provenance breakdown is the number that tells you where the field is: 70 projects are human-written, 102 are AI-written, and 39 are mixed, so AI-authored formalizations are already the majority of what lands in the archive.

Maintenance is where the AI agents do the actual work, and the paper is unusually honest about how it works. The dependency-update workflow tests all unchanged source under a new Lean or Mathlib release, assigns each failing project to a repair agent, and assembles the patches for review. The archive has had six Lean version bumps, and the operational record shows that agent-assisted upgrades restore compatibility after each one. Separately, agents run proof golfing, shortening proofs across the library, and compilation optimizations, cutting build time and RAM usage, and the paper records both the accepted changes and the follow-up repairs that were needed after the initial automation, which is the kind of failure detail that usually stays out of a systems paper. The CI stack is shared with Mathlib, a full library build, warning checks, the declaration and style linters, plus archive-specific quality gates that check the project cards, the attribution, the allowed axioms, and attempts to bypass the checks, with an LLM reviewer examining the mathematical claims themselves. The evidence of value is that Lean Pool is the most reused external repository in the LeanEval structural audit of research-level formalization, meaning other people's serious proofs actually import its contents.

The skeptical read starts with the paper's own disclaimer, and it is not a joke. A one-page human contribution with an AI-written body is a legitimate experiment in the paper's subject matter, but it means the claims in the extended summary, the maintenance workflows, the optimization results, the review statistics, are the product of the same agent pipeline that is being evaluated, and the paper's reproducibility statement, that reported numbers are computed from retained execution records and can be recomputed, is the only guardrail between an interesting artifact and a self-reported one. The admission rules are mechanically enforced, which is strong: no sorry, no new axioms, license checks, and the Lean kernel is a real verifier, so the proofs in the archive are actually correct, and that is a much stronger guarantee than any code archive can offer. But the quality of the mathematical statements, whether they are faithful to the informal results they claim to formalize, whether the definitions are the right ones, is covered by LLM review, which is a judgment layer, not a verification layer, and the paper does not report an error rate for that layer. The scale is real but small: 211 projects and 18 contributors is a promising archive, not an infrastructure, and the claim that it will become the formal analogue of arXiv is a vision the paper explicitly frames as such. The maintenance results are the most defensible part of the paper, because they are operational records of things that happened, six version bumps, repair agents, accepted optimizations, follow-up repairs, and the build-resource comparisons against Mathlib and Tau Ceti on the same machine are the kind of measurement that can be re-run.

It is worth placing Lean Pool against its nearest neighbors, because the paper does it in a comparison table and the positioning matters. Mathlib is the integrated library: one shared mathematical API, human review, generality and style as acceptance criteria, and AI use only with disclosed justification from contributors. Tau Ceti is the AI-first sibling: AI-authored mathematics following human roadmaps, with AI workers doing the maintenance, prioritizing review, updating Mathlib pins, and repairing the library, accepting contributions on roadmap fit and API coherence, and using adversarial AI review as a gate. Palomar is the registry model: checked versions of separate repositories that persist, with updates requiring resubmission, verification, and automated review of statement faithfulness. Lean Pool sits in a distinct position: it keeps independent developments separate rather than merging them into one API, which preserves attribution and project identity, while still maintaining everything in a common environment so the pieces stay mutually usable. That is a genuinely different bet from Mathlib's integration model. The bet is that research formalizations are projects with their own internal coherence, and forcing them into a shared API, as Mathlib does, is what causes the linear growth rate and the review bottleneck, and that the right infrastructure is a maintained pool of independently attributed projects that can import from each other. The vision statement in the paper is the sharpest sentence in it: as formalization gets cheap, a new mathematics paper will have both an arXiv posting and a Lean Pool formalization, and later work will cite the paper and import the formalization, so the formal dependency graph mirrors the citation graph of the literature. Whether that happens depends on whether the maintenance cost the agents absorb is really close to zero, and the six version bumps and the repair records suggest it is close, but not yet.

Bottom line: Lean Pool is the first credible demonstration that agent-maintained compatibility is cheap enough to run continuously over a multi-project formalized-mathematics archive, with kernel-checked correctness as the trust anchor and the AI-written-authorship majority as the signal of where the field is heading. The open question is not whether the proofs are correct, they are, it is whether the LLM-judged statement quality holds up as the pool grows past the two-hundred-project scale where a single maintainer can still see everything.
