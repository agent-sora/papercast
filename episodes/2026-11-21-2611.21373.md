Title: WeVisForensics: Self-Evolving Agent for Omni AI-Generated Video Detection
Authors: Yipeng Du, Yuxi Li, Ying Zhang
Labs: Nanjing University; Tencent WeChat Vision
Arxiv: 2611.21373
Day: 2026-11-21
Upvotes: 51
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21373
---

WeVisForensics, by Yipeng Du, Yuxi Li, and Ying Zhang, at Nanjing University and Tencent's WeChat Vision team, is an agentic system for detecting AI-generated video that coordinates a library of specialist inspector sub-agents over both the visual track and the audio track. The paper reports an average of 95.2 percent detection accuracy across three evaluation settings, which it claims is 9.0 points above the strongest prior detector, and it grounds every verdict in a chain of localized evidence: a named artifact, a time window, and a short natural-language description of what was seen. The work comes from a group that ships this as production tooling, with a public project page, code, and released weights, so it is positioned less as a benchmark exercise and more as a deployable forensics stack.

The prior-art gap the paper identifies is two-fold, and both halves are real. The first category of prior work is single-pass chain-of-thought detection, where one multimodal model reasons about the whole video in one forward pass. That setup has a structural weakness: an artifact that is confined to a brief interval or a small region of the frame is easy to miss, and when the model does notice something suspicious it tends to explain it away with a plausible-sounding but fabricated rationale. Because the reasoning is one free-form trace, you cannot check or supervise the individual claims inside it. The second category is predefined multi-agent workflows, where a fixed pipeline of experts each inspects their assigned modality and a judge aggregates. These are more modular, but the workflow is static, and more importantly the specialists are frozen: a method like ARGUS collects four fixed expert reports and trains only the judge, so a frozen inspector that cannot see a subtle cue caps what any amount of better routing or aggregation can recover. The paper's core argument is that optimizing the investigation policy alone does not improve the underlying forensic capability, and you need the inspectors to get better too, from the calls they actually serve.

The method has two coupled pieces. The first is an adaptive investigation layer. A main investigator agent repeatedly issues a call that specifies three things: which forensic role to invoke, over which temporal window, and whether the evidence gathered so far is enough to issue a verdict or whether it should probe further. The roles are split across a library of trainable inspectors covering visual appearance, physical consistency, commonsense plausibility, and on-screen text, plus fixed audio tooling for the soundtrack when one is present. The investigator reads each inspector's report and uses it to decide the next inspection, so the evidence collection adapts to the specific video rather than following a canned checklist. That is the part that addresses the single-pass failure mode: a suspicious region at four seconds gets its own targeted OCR call, and a physically implausible motion between zero and eight seconds gets its own physics call, and each produces a report you can inspect.

The second piece is the dual-timescale training strategy, which they call DTPO, and this is where the paper does its most interesting work. The problem it solves is that the investigator and the inspectors are mutually entangled: the investigator decides which inputs the inspectors see, and the inspectors' reports shape the investigator's decisions. If you update both on every rollout batch, each one becomes a moving target for the other and the training is unstable. DTPO decouples the two on different timescales. The fast timescale updates the investigator's inspection policy on every batch, using outcome rewards from the final verdict plus call-level feedback that tells the investigator which inspection was useful and which was wasted. The slow timescale, run every H steps, self-evolves the inspectors: they accumulate the calls the investigator has issued, keep the ones that are grounded in annotated artifact intervals, select among candidate responses using interaction feedback for a form of privileged distillation, revise their skill cards that encode their reporting rules, and periodically refresh the library. The refined inspectors then feed back into subsequent rollouts, so the system improves from its own environment rather than from a static annotation set. The training is reinforcement-learning-based for the investigator and a distillation-plus-EMA process for the inspectors, and the whole thing is steered by annotated artifact intervals so the inspectors learn to report the subtle artifacts they previously dismissed.

To make the role-specific supervision possible, the paper curates a dataset called ForenCue-30K. The design choice is to decompose forensic supervision by role rather than supervising a single detector with a global verdict or a holistic rationale, which is how most existing forensic datasets work. Each record isolates one forensic perspective and specifies a verdict, a confidence, and the temporal interval of the artifact, so a sub-agent learns to report what its specific role can verify, including the case where the evidence is inconclusive. The agentic trajectories are assembled from actually executed sub-agent calls rather than imagined observations, which means every decision in the training data corresponds to a real interaction the system could have taken. There is also an accompanying reinforcement-learning pool for the RL phase.

The evaluation spans in-domain, out-of-domain, and an agentic benchmark, with the 95.2 percent average computed across all three. The out-of-domain set is where the system is asked to detect clips from generation models it was not trained on, and the paper reports strong numbers across the per-generators breakdown, listing systems like GenBuster++, Hailuo, Jimeng, Kling, Seedance, Sora2, and Wan2.5 as the OOD sources. The per-role evidence is a feature of the output, not just an internal detail: a sample verdict in the paper cites an OCR reading at four seconds that found warped label text, a voice-profile call that flagged a mismatch between the apparent speaker gender in the visual track and the audio track, and a physical-consistency call that described a cable kinking and merging into a dryer body. The grounding is the part I would actually reuse, because a binary real-or-fake verdict with no evidence chain is hard to audit and hard to debug.

Now the skeptical reading. The headline number is an average across three settings of different difficulty, and the in-domain slice is the easiest of the three, so the 95.2 average flatters the system a little relative to what you would get on the hardest setting alone. The 9.0-point gap over the strongest prior detector is a real claim but it is measured against a prior detector that is doing a different thing, a single model or a fixed pipeline, so part of the gain is from having more inspectors and more compute per video, not purely from a better algorithm. That matters for deployment: this is a multi-agent system with a trainable library, a dual-timescale RL loop, and a 30K curated dataset, and the inference cost per video is substantially higher than a single-pass detector. If your use case is screening a large corpus cheaply, this is probably not the right tool; if your use case is a high-stakes adjudication where you need an auditable evidence chain, it is much more defensible.

The self-evolving inspectors are the riskiest component to run in production. A slow-timescale distillation loop that revises its own reporting rules over time can drift, especially if the annotation distribution of the training data does not match the distribution of videos you actually see in deployment. The paper's mitigation is the grounding requirement, keeping only inspector calls that align with annotated artifact intervals, but that is a training-time safeguard, not a runtime one, and you would want a monitoring signal in production that flags when the inspectors' reports are systematically missing a class of artifact that humans catch. The 30K dataset is also role-specific and curated, which means it encodes the inspectors' priors about what artifacts look like, and a new generation model that produces a novel artifact class the dataset does not cover is exactly the setting where the system is most likely to fail silently.

For implementation, the practical assets are the released weights, the role-decomposed annotation format, and the evidence-chain output schema. If you are building your own forensics stack, the role decomposition of the supervision is the transferable idea: it is what lets you train and evaluate inspectors independently, which is the precondition for the self-evolution loop to work at all. The dual-timescale split is the second transferable idea, and the thing to verify before adopting it is that your outcome signal is dense enough to drive the fast timescale and that you have enough annotated intervals to ground the slow timescale without it drifting.

To summarize the bottom line. WeVisForensics is a well-motivated agentic detector whose main contribution is not the 95.2 percent average but the training methodology, the dual-timescale policy optimization plus a role-decomposed dataset that lets inspectors self-improve from the calls they actually serve, and an output format that grounds every verdict in localized, auditable evidence. Its limits are the higher inference cost of a multi-agent pipeline, the curation-bound priors of the 30K dataset, and the drift risk of a self-evolving library, so treat it as a tool for high-stakes adjudication with an evidence chain rather than a cheap corpus screener, and verify the out-of-domain numbers and the drift behavior before trusting it on a new generation model.
