Paint-Anything: Unified Any-Color Control for Image Generation and Editing. The first three authors are Ji Xie, Dewei Zhou, and Xinyu Huang, from ByteDance Seed, with Zhennan Chen and Xun Wang completing the author list, spread across ByteDance Seed, Zhejiang University, and Nanjing University.

The paper targets a specific professional requirement that generic text-to-image models do not meet: specifying an object's color with an exact 24-bit hex value, not with a named color like blue or red. A brand designer needs the logo in the exact brand blue. A retailer needs product images in the exact red of a new collection. Prior approaches to color control mostly add something to the model: learned color tokens, task-specific modules, numeric color encoders, or training-free inference-time techniques like sampling guidance and attention manipulation. Those designs are hard to scale across the full color space, hard to port to new architectures, or expensive at inference time, and they tend to treat generation, colorization, and editing as separate problems. The starting insight here is simpler: modern language models can already parse hex strings. The paper shows that a compact 4-billion-parameter model, Qwen3-4B, given a raw hex color code, produces a plausible spoken description of the color, so a hex string can serve as a prompt-native interface for color without any dedicated color encoder.

Paint-Anything keeps the color specification entirely inside the text prompt. Each hex value is wrapped in explicit color tags, like a color tag surrounding the six-character code, so the frozen text encoder sees it as an ordinary token span. One finetuned model then covers both generation and editing with the same interface: for generation, the prompt binds colors to object phrases, and for editing, a source image is encoded as a clean condition latent and the instruction specifies the target hex value. Training uses a standard rectified-flow objective with the text encoder and VAE frozen, and the only model-specific ingredient is the training data and a timestep gate.

The data pipeline is the workhorse. Paint-500K is built from real image-caption pairs through four steps: a vision-language model grounds the caption-relevant objects, SAM3 segmentation isolates object pixels, and then, instead of RGB-space k-means, the pipeline clusters in CIELAB perceptual color space using Mean Shift, which adapts the number of dominant colors to each object's actual distribution and avoids the redundant splits that fixed-K k-means produces under natural lighting. The largest surviving cluster yields the hex label, and the caption is rewritten to bind each label to its noun phrase. Because shadows make real-image colors approximate, the pipeline adds pure-color anchors: solid-color images whose pixels exactly match the paired hex value, used only at high-noise timesteps.

The training recipe is deliberately minimal. The final loss is a weighted sum of three streams, all using the same flow-matching objective: real-image generation samples, paired editing samples, and pure-color anchor samples under the high-noise gate. The gate is the one non-obvious detail. Motivated by the observation that color stabilizes early in pure-color generation trajectories, pure-color samples are trained only at high noise, from a gate timestep up to pure noise, while real-image samples train across the full noise range. The idea is that the anchors provide a clean, low-level mapping from hex string to RGB statistics where the model is still deciding global color, while low-noise training stays on natural images so texture and lighting are never taught from flat fills. An ablation confirms gating matters: ungated anchors underperform. The training set has 400K generation samples, 100K single-object and 300K multi-object, and 100K editing samples, where a pretrained editing model recolors the source image and the original photograph serves as the target, so the targets carry no generative artifacts.

The benchmark results are on FLUX.2-4B and Z-Image Base, both finetuned for 4,000 steps on 4 GPUs with Adam, global batch size 72, and a learning rate of 2 times 10 to the minus 5. The authors introduce ACBench, with 1,000 generation prompts and 500 editing prompts, scored by segmenting the target object with SAM3 and comparing its mean RGB against the requested hex value: a mean absolute channel error of at most 16 gets full score, errors between 16 and 64 are penalized linearly, and 64 or more scores zero. On FLUX.2-4B, the finetuned model lifts ACBench generation from 37.02 to 68.58, an 85.3 percent relative improvement, and editing from 58.90 to 75.57, a 28.3 percent relative improvement. The headline comparison in the table is that this 8-billion-parameter finetuned model beats FLUX.2-dev, a 56-billion-parameter model, on ACBench generation, scoring 68.58 against 51.70. Two things about those numbers. The 85.3 percent relative gain starts from a base score of 37.02, so the absolute improvement is 31.6 points, which is large but should not be read as the model now hitting 85 percent of exact colors. The 28.3 percent editing gain, by contrast, starts from a base that already scored 58.90, so the editing setting was closer to solved before finetuning and the room for improvement was smaller. On the independent CompColor benchmark, under both named-color and hex-translated prompts, the finetuned model achieves the highest average score among the compared methods, and the paper reports supporting results on GenColorBench and a human preference study in the appendices.

The skeptical read. The conceptually cleanest result here is that no new architecture was needed. No color token, no separate encoder, no inference-time optimization: wrapping the hex value in color tags inside the prompt, plus object-level supervision and the high-noise anchors, gets an 8-billion-parameter model past a 56-billion-parameter base on the authors' own benchmark. That is a useful template for any prompt-native control signal, and the ablations, including the color-tag wrapping ablation and the gated-versus-ungated anchor ablation, support each ingredient rather than hiding behind the combined system. The CIELAB-plus-Mean-Shift labeling choice is also the right one over RGB k-means, and the paper shows it directly on a shaded skin patch where fixed-K clustering produces redundant color splits.

But the evaluation has boundaries you should know before relying on the numbers. First, the headline 85.3 percent relative gain is measured on ACBench, which the authors constructed, and the scoring is region-mean RGB with a deliberately forgiving piecewise-linear curve: an error of 16 or less on average across channels is a perfect score, so a model that lands in the right neighborhood of the requested color scores high even when the exact value is off. The independent CompColor and GenColorBench results help, but CompColor's hex protocol is the authors' own translation of named colors, and the GenColorBench comparison partly leans on a baseline whose public checkpoints were unavailable, with the paper falling back to that baseline's published numbers. Second, the training data comes from an internal collection of real image-caption pairs, so the full Paint-500K dataset is not something a third party can rebuild exactly, which limits reproducibility of the exact recipe. Third, the score is about color fidelity, not about the rest of the image: the paper's own qualitative comparisons show the base model producing semantically plausible but numerically wrong colors, and the fix trades some of that general generation behavior for exact color adherence, a trade the numbers on unrelated benchmarks do not fully characterize. And finally, the editing setup uses masks taken from the source image and shared across compared outputs, which is fair for controlled comparison but a looser protocol than segmenting the edited result itself.

Bottom line: this is a tight, reproducible-in-spirit recipe for making exact hex color a prompt-native capability in a flow-matching image model, and the high-noise pure-color anchor trick is the piece most worth stealing for other low-level pixel-space controls. If you adopt it, re-score on your own color-critical test set with a stricter error curve before treating the 85.3 percent headline as the real-world margin.
