Does On-Policy Distillation Really Distill? From Noisy Teacher to Self-Improvement, by Yi Ding and Ruqi Zhang, from the Department of Computer Science at Purdue University. The short version of a long argument: on-policy distillation, one of the most popular token-level post-training recipes of the last year, is not actually distilling. The students get better, but the gains come from suppressing the model's own low-probability tokens, an operation that needs no teacher at all. The authors demonstrate this with controlled ablations, then turn the diagnosis into OPSA, a supervision-free training rule that beats on-policy distillation by 16.77 points on AIME24.

To set up the problem: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, like GRPO and DAPO, samples a group of responses per question and normalizes the verifiable rewards within the group to get advantages. The weakness is sparsity. When several responses in a group get the same correctness outcome, their normalized advantages vanish and the learning signal dies. On-policy distillation fixes that with dense supervision: a stronger teacher scores every token of the student's own rollouts, and the per-token advantage is the log-ratio of the teacher's probability over the student's, a reverse KL estimator. The catch is practical: you need shared vocabularies and white-box access to teacher logits. On-policy self-distillation keeps the paradigm but swaps in a hinted version of the policy itself as teacher. The authors argue all of these share the same fatal assumption: that a teacher scoring prefixes it would never generate itself is still giving meaningful supervision.

That assumption fails measurably. Setup: Qwen3-1.7B student, thinking mode off, with Qwen3-4B, 30B-A3B, and 235B-A22B as teachers. For each of 500 questions from DAPO-17k they sample one correct and one incorrect student response and check the sign of the teacher's advantage on the boxed answer token against the verifiable outcome. A negative advantage on a correct answer, or a positive one on a wrong one, counts as noise. The 4B teacher is already noisy: 20.4 percent of correct trajectories get negative advantages, 40.8 percent of incorrect ones get positive ones, for a 30.6 percent noise rate. It worsens with scale: 34.7 percent for the 30B-A3B teacher and 50.6 percent for the 235B teacher. The largest teacher is effectively broken as a supervisor: 97.8 percent of boxed answer tokens receive negative advantages even when the answer is correct. Their reading: distributional mismatch between student and teacher grows with scale, so student trajectories become increasingly off-policy for the teacher.

Here is the part that should make you stop and think. They run a filtering experiment: train standard on-policy distillation on all trajectories, one variant only on trajectories whose answer tokens carry noisy supervision, and one only on clean ones. All three converge to comparable performance in the same number of steps. Training exclusively on the noisy trajectories learns at the same rate as the standard recipe, which means the teacher's behavior-matching signal is not doing the work. So what actually drives improvement? Two findings. First, which tokens move the loss. The gradient on a sampled token vanishes when the advantage is near zero or when the student is nearly certain of the token. In practice, 29.2 percent of tokens carry exactly zero advantage and 51.7 percent carry an advantage below 10 to the minus 4, and those cluster on the student's high-log-probability tokens, where teacher and student mostly agree. Restrict training to the top log-probability tokens, with the real advantages or with random ones, and AIME24 does not move. Second, which signal matters. The advantages in on-policy distillation are overwhelmingly negative, because the teacher assigns lower probability to the student's sampled tokens. Replacing every advantage with a fixed negative value of minus 0.5, applied to the 20 percent of tokens with the lowest student log probability, gives a steady climb that matches full on-policy distillation. A fixed positive advantage of plus 0.2, by contrast, collapses the policy within 40 steps: response length drops to nearly zero and the model starts emitting garbled tokens. The entire gain of on-policy distillation is thus reproducible with a teacher-free negative signal on the model's own low-probability tokens.

OPSA is the rule that falls out of this diagnosis. It updates only the 20 percent of sampled tokens with the lowest student log probability, always with a negative advantage, and scales the magnitude by token entropy. A low log-probability token is ambiguous: it can be a genuinely uncertain fork where the distribution is spread across many plausible continuations, or a tail sample from a confident distribution. Entropy separates those cases. At a high-entropy position the negative advantage is larger, so the sampled tail token is more strongly suppressed and its mass is redistributed evenly across the head tokens. At a low-entropy position the signal is small, preserving the model's confident predictions. Concretely: the base advantage is minus 3 over 4, the entropy-direction parameter is plus 1 so higher entropy means a larger negative advantage, and the update is restricted to the lowest-20-percent log-probability tokens in each rollout. There is no teacher, no verifiable reward, and no hint anywhere in the loop. Because the advantage comes from the student's own token entropy, there are no teacher forward passes and the overhead is negligible.

The mechanism they describe in four cases is worth stating because it predicts the side effects. At a high-entropy fork, when a tail token was sampled, OPSA suppresses that tail token and pushes its probability onto the head tokens, so the next rollout is less likely to wander down a low-probability branch. At the same fork, when a head token was sampled, the negative advantage redistributes mass among the competing head tokens rather than sharpening onto one, which is the design choice that keeps output diversity intact. At a low-entropy position, the small advantage still nudges a sampled tail token down, while a high-confidence head token is not in the lowest 20 percent by log probability at all, so OPSA skips it and leaves the confident prediction alone. The net effect is to prune unlikely reasoning branches while keeping strong predictions stable and exploration at genuine forks intact.

The headline numbers. On Qwen3-1.7B, the base model scores 13.44 average at 32 samples on AIME24, 9.69 on AIME25, and 5.73 on HMMT25. After OPSA, those become 48.85, 35.31, and 23.33, gains of 35.41, 25.62, and 17.60 points, which the authors report as 263, 264, and 307 percent relative. Pass at 32 more than doubles on every benchmark. Out-of-domain is more modest: MBPP+ goes from 58.24 to 59.44 and GPQA-Diamond from 27.92 to 32.40. Qwen3-4B follows the same pattern with 38.75 points on AIME24, and on the already-strong Qwen3.5-9B the gain shrinks to 11.46, as expected near the ceiling. Against the on-policy baselines on Qwen3-1.7B, OPSA at 35.83 average beats GRPO at 24.79, TTRL at 11.81, on-policy distillation at 22.15, and on-policy self-distillation at 23.58, all without external supervision. The thinking-mode result is interesting: the OPSA model with thinking disabled matches the base model with thinking enabled, and enabling thinking on top of OPSA still helps.

The ablations are the part I would trust most. Response length climbs steadily through training and accuracy tracks it, so a fair question is whether the gain is just longer chains of thought. They test this by counting reflective tokens, phrases like wait, but, and perhaps alternatively, and find the OPSA models produce substantially more of them. The sharper test: they mask out the fork positions whose head-token sets contain those reflective words and exclude them from the update. When the fork tokens are masked, the response-length growth and the accuracy gains largely disappear, and response length collapses around 300 steps. That is a clean causal check pointing the mechanism at fork-token redistribution rather than length per se. They also report a pairwise-Jaccard check that OPSA does not collapse sample diversity, and an ablation over the fraction of trained tokens showing the 20 percent cut is roughly where the signal lives.

Now the skeptical read. First, the noise definition is answer-token-local: they measure teacher supervision noise only on the boxed final-answer token, because per-token reference supervision is generally unavailable. The claim that half of the largest teacher's supervision is noise is really a claim about one token per trajectory, and the broader statement about intermediate reasoning steps is an inference, not a measurement. Second, the fixed-negative-advantage baseline is the real competitor, and it matches on-policy distillation on AIME24 at the 4B-teacher scale. If a constant minus 0.5 on the bottom 20 percent of tokens reproduces the teacher, then much of the token-level distillation literature may be selling a mechanism that is mostly a well-placed negative reward. Third, OPSA is distribution sharpening in disguise, and sharpening has known failure modes: it helps when the base policy is under-exploring and can hurt when it is calibrated. The gains shrink predictably as the base model gets stronger, from 35 points at 1.7B to 11 at 9B. Fourth, the method is trained and tested on math reasoning in the DAPO-17k distribution, and the out-of-domain code and science results, while positive, are small. Whether the entropy-weighted negative signal transfers to open-ended or agentic tasks, where low-probability tokens may be legitimate exploration rather than errors, is open.

If you implement this: it is a small, well-specified change to any on-policy RL loop, and the authors use the slime framework on 8 H100 or H200 GPUs, so the barrier is low. The two knobs to watch are the 20 percent low-log-probability cutoff and the base advantage magnitude. I would re-verify the fixed-negative-advantage baseline on your own model and benchmark before attributing any distillation gains to the teacher, and check pass-at-k, not just the mean, because a sharpening method that trades diversity for mean accuracy will look good on average and bad in the tail.

Bottom line: this is a well-executed diagnostic paper making a strong case that on-policy distillation's gains come from suppressing the student's own low-probability tokens rather than from the teacher, and that OPSA captures most of those gains with no external supervision. Treat the mechanism claims as evidence-based but answer-token-scoped, and re-run the teacher-free baseline yourself before you stop paying for a teacher.
