Today's paper is pi-4: A Foundation Model for 4D Scene Geometry and Motion, from Yifan Wang, Weiguang Zhao, and Ni Yang, with corresponding authors at the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, and co-authors at Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Zhejiang University. This is a foundation-model release, and it is worth an episode on that ground alone, but it is also a genuinely interesting systems result. The model is a unified feed-forward system for 4D scene perception. Give it diverse, uncalibrated RGB observations from the wild, a single image, an unordered collection of photos, or a video, and it recovers dense geometry, camera poses, and metric scale, together with 2D and 3D point tracks and moving-object masks for multi-image inputs. The two things that make this a foundation model rather than another reconstruction head are the input diversity it handles and the size: a shared backbone across all of these capabilities, with about 0.5 billion parameters total, less than half the size of most of the baselines it is compared against.

The architecture is worth walking through because the coupling between geometry and motion is the design decision. A shared DINOv3 image encoder produces features, with a stop-gradient so the backbone is not pulled in two directions. Those features feed two loop decoders. The geometry loop decoder, run for a fixed number of steps, does frame attention and cross-view attention, and has heads for a local point map, a 3D refiner, camera pose, and scale, plus a confidence head. The motion loop decoder, also iterated for a fixed number of steps, does frame and cross-view attention, and has heads for dense 2D tracks and for moving-object masks, with a SAM encoder in the loop. The critical coupling: the geometry branch produces a reconstruction, and that reconstruction conditions the motion branch through spatial adaptive layer normalization, with detached geometry features so the motion gradients do not flow back into geometry. The motion branch predicts image-space correspondences, and those correspondences are lifted into 3D using the reconstructed target-view geometry. So the tracks are not just 2D point flows; they are 3D tracks that inherit the metric structure the geometry branch recovered. That is the difference between a video tracker and a 4D perception model. The loop-decoder structure, an iterative refinement rather than a single feed, is what lets a 0.5B model do work that larger single-shot models do, and it is the same family of design as the loop decoders in modern depth and reconstruction models.

The scale claim is the surprising one, and it is a real architectural result. The model is trained with contexts of at most 64 views, but it produces joint reconstructions and tracks in a single forward pass for scenes with thousands of input images, including cases with over 1,000 images. The mechanism is the loop decoders with cross-view attention operating over a set of views, and the training recipe that samples view counts stochastically, with short view sets of 2 to 24 images receiving most of the probability mass, plus additional mass on single-image inputs and on long view sets of 32, 48, or 64 images. The data recipe is where a lot of the in-the-wild robustness comes from: the training data mixes natural and counterbias camera distributions, where natural sets use modest camera roll and shared intrinsics, and counterbias sets explicitly cover broader roll angles and a mix of lens mappings, including barrel, pincushion, and near-rectilinear distortion, sampled at probabilities of 40, 35, 20, and 5 percent. The augmentation warps both the RGB and the geometric supervision together, so the model learns geometry that is invariant to the lens and roll it was trained on.

The evaluation spans geometry, tracking, and segmentation. On geometry, the paper compares against nine baselines across 16 settings covering monocular depth, video depth, camera pose and trajectory, and multi-view reconstruction, and pi-4 achieves the best overall average rank among the compared methods. On the trajectory evaluation specifically, where all input images of each route are processed in a single forward pass, pi-4 reports the best results on all five metrics, reducing absolute trajectory error by 47.2 percent and chord error by 24.1 percent relative to the strongest baseline in that group. Monocular depth uses per-frame median-scale alignment, and video depth uses sequence-level scale-and-shift alignment, which is the honest way to compare metric scale across methods. On tracking and moving-object segmentation, the model is competitive rather than the clear winner, single-view and multi-view 2D and 3D tracking plus moving-object masks. I am flagging that distinction deliberately: the strong claim is best-average-rank on geometry, and the tracking and segmentation results are competitive. The paper is clear about which is which, and that clarity is part of why the foundation-model framing is defensible.

One ablation is worth calling out because it is a practical detail: the geometry branch reads features from DINOv3 layers 11 and 17 jointly rather than the final layer, and the paper shows that intermediate features give the best local point reconstruction for dense prediction. That is the same finding the UFM work reports for frozen encoders, and it is the kind of choice that quietly determines whether a 0.5B model can compete with larger ones. The measured training-memory footprint for input sets up to 64 views is reported in the paper, which matters for reproduction: the loop decoders with cross-view attention over a growing set of views scale their memory differently than a naive per-view stack, and anyone planning to train a variant at longer context should check that curve before assuming their memory budget holds. The stop-gradient on the frozen backbone is the second reason the joint training is stable: the motion branch can pull on the shared representation without degrading the geometry the geometry branch is still learning, so the two heads improve in parallel rather than fighting over the encoder.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the core architectural claim, that geometry and motion can share a backbone and a single 4D representation at 0.5 billion parameters, because the design is clean, the conditioning mechanism is explicit and cheap, and the parameter count is well below the baselines it beats on geometry. I would trust the data-recipe emphasis, because the counterbias camera distribution is a concrete, checkable answer to why a feed-forward model generalizes to uncalibrated in-the-wild inputs, and it is the kind of detail that separates a robust model from one that only works on clean captures. I would re-verify four things. First, the best-average-rank geometry claim is an aggregate, and I would look at which specific settings carry the rank, because multi-view reconstruction and monocular depth are very different tasks and a strong average can hide a weak cell. Second, the thousand-image single-forward-pass result is an extrapolation well past the 64-view training context, so I would want the degradation curve as view count grows past 64, since that is the regime where cross-view attention memory and the loop-decoder approximation both strain. Third, the 3D tracking quality depends on the geometry reconstruction being right, so the motion branch inherits any geometry error; the paper reports 2D and 3D tracking as competitive, and I would check the 3D track error specifically, not just the 2D flow. And fourth, code and models are released, which is the strongest possible signal, so the practical question is whether the released checkpoint matches the paper's numbers, and I would run the Hugging Face demo on my own uncalibrated captures before building on it.

Bottom line: pi-4 is a compact, well-designed foundation model that couples geometry and motion through a shared 0.5B backbone and a single 4D representation, and its best-average-rank geometry result plus its thousand-image single-pass capability make it a serious baseline for 4D scene perception. The tracking and segmentation numbers are competitive rather than leading, so treat geometry as the strength and verify the released checkpoint against your own uncalibrated data before adopting it.
