A Missing Piece for Trustworthy AI Reviewers. The first three authors are Chenguang Wang, Ming Li, and Chengrui Fan, working at the University of Maryland.

The question is whether an automated manuscript reviewer is robust in a way that matters. The obvious test is to rewrite the paper's prose, change the rhetorical framing, and check whether the reviewer's score stays put. But that test has a degenerate answer. A reviewer that gives every paper the same score is perfectly stable across rewrites, because it never varies at all. The paper calls this false robustness: low rewrite-induced drift that comes not from understanding the science, but from the scores collapsing across papers. So robustness is reframed as a joint requirement. Within a paper, the score must be stable across content-preserving rewrites. Across papers, the reviewer must still discriminate, keeping the differences that separate a strong submission from a weak one. A score that does not move is only acceptable if there is still meaningful spread between different papers.

To measure this they build RobustReview, a full-manuscript benchmark. It starts from 60 anonymized ICLR 2026 submissions with their arXiv LaTeX sources, so the scientific content is fixed and only the presentation changes. Each paper is rewritten under ten rhetorical conditions, and each condition is instantiated independently by two separate LLM systems, GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus, giving 1,260 full manuscripts in total. The conditions are designed to preserve the reported science while moving the rhetoric, so a reviewer that is actually reading the content should not change its score, while a reviewer that is reading the packaging will. They then evaluate 30 reviewer configurations, spanning general-purpose rubric-instructed LLMs, specialized scientific-review models, and agentic review systems, under several review protocols, so the same backbone is tested under different prompting regimes. The metrics split into two groups. Direct within-paper metrics, mean absolute deviation and drift standard deviation, measure rewrite instability. Joint metrics, including ICC, a stability-to-discrimination ratio they call SPR, and a discriminability measure, relate within-paper consistency to cross-paper separation, so a stable reviewer that has collapsed its scores scores poorly on the joint terms. The design point of the joint metrics is that they refuse to reward a score that simply does not move.

The finding the benchmark is designed to expose is configuration-dependent sensitivity. The same underlying model can look robust or fragile depending on how it is prompted. A strict rubric changes both the drift and the spread of scores, and the two effects are not independent, so a reviewer that looks more stable under one protocol may look that way because it has narrowed its range rather than because it reads content more faithfully. So a single stability number, read in isolation, can reward a reviewer that has stopped differentiating. That is the central measurement failure they want to make visible, and it is the reason the paper pushes back on evaluating robustness with drift alone.

Their proposed reviewer, SciCore, attacks the problem from the content side rather than the instruction side. Instead of merely telling the model to ignore rhetoric, it adds a science-core branch. An extractor pulls the scientific core of the manuscript, the claims, methods, and evidence stripped of rhetorical packaging, and the reviewer conditions on that core alongside the full manuscript. The design bet is that if the score is anchored to the scientific content, it should be stable across rewrites while still separating papers whose science genuinely differs. They check the anchor is sound: the extracted cores are stable across rewrites and still paper-specific, with matched original-versus-rewrite cores sitting near 0.97 in similarity while different-paper controls sit far lower, near 0.67. That gap between the matched and control similarities is the property the whole method depends on. The cores have to be close enough to the original that a rewrite does not change them, and far enough from other papers that two different submissions do not collapse into the same core. If either side of that gap failed, the branch would either over-react to rhetoric or under-differentiate across papers, and the joint metrics are exactly the check for which side failed. The final reviewer fuses the full-manuscript branch and the core branch with a fixed weight, so it keeps the presentation information that sometimes matters while being anchored to the content that should not move.

The numbers, on the GPT-5.5 backbone where the final model is evaluated, show the tradeoff being managed rather than eliminated. The standard GPT-5.5 reviewer has a mean absolute deviation of about 0.598 across rewrites, where lower is better, and an ICC of 0.615. Moving to the strict rubric protocol does not cleanly help: the direct drift goes up to 0.766, yet the joint ICC ticks up to 0.632, which is exactly the configuration-dependent tension the benchmark is meant to surface. A core-only branch gets the drift very low at 0.292 with an ICC of 0.751, but its human alignment drops. The dual-branch SciCore fusion lands at a mean absolute deviation of 0.476, the lowest among the full reviewers, with an ICC of 0.775, the highest, and a discriminability of 0.726, also the highest, while keeping human alignment competitive. So the fused reviewer is the one that is simultaneously most stable across rewrites and most separating across papers, which is the joint point the whole benchmark is built to reward.

They also check the result is not an artifact of one backbone. A cross-backbone study reruns the science-core branch under several other backbones, comparing the core-adapted reviewer against the same backbone's standard manuscript review. The direction of the effect is consistent, that anchoring to the extracted core improves the stability-discrimination balance, though the magnitude shifts with the backbone, which is the expected behavior for a method that leans on the underlying model's ability to extract a faithful core. There is also a sensitivity analysis over the fusion weight, the alpha that blends the full-manuscript branch with the science-core branch. Moving alpha changes the balance between human alignment and the joint robustness metrics, which is the tradeoff the dual-branch design is explicitly managing, and the reported configuration sits at an even half-half blend rather than a hard switch to the core. That choice matters: a pure core reviewer would be maximally stable but would throw away the rhetorical and presentation information that sometimes carries legitimate signal, while a pure manuscript reviewer is the opposite, and the fusion is the compromise that lands best on the joint metrics. One more detail worth flagging is that the extractor that pulls the scientific core is itself a model, so the stability of the core is only as good as the extractor's faithfulness. If the extractor over-normalizes, stripping away claims that are genuinely different between two papers, then the matched similarity goes up but the control similarity goes up too, and the discriminability of the fused reviewer silently degrades. The paper reports the gap between the matched and control similarities, but it does not report how sensitive that gap is to the extractor's own prompt, so the stability of the core is a property of one extractor configuration, not a robust invariant, and the fused reviewer inherits the dependency because the anchor that is supposed to stabilize the score is itself a model output.

Skeptically, the benchmark is built from a single venue, ICLR, and the rewrites are generated by two LLM systems, so the rhetorical conditions are shaped by how those systems rewrite rather than by how humans actually vary their prose. The science-core extractor and the final reviewer both ride on the same strong backbone, so part of the stability gain may be the backbone's own coherence rather than the branch as a general recipe. And the joint metrics are themselves a modeling choice, a specific way of penalizing collapse, so a reviewer optimized against a different stability-discrimination balance could rank differently. The honest read is that this is a useful diagnostic for a real and under-measured failure, and a plausible fix, but the numbers are narrow in venue and model family. The paper gives you a sharper definition of what robust review means, and a reviewer that does better on that definition, while staying candid about where the evidence stops.
