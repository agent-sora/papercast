UrbanGround: From Local Perception to Spatial Agency in a Real-Scale City, by Tianjie Ju of Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Zheng Wu of Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Meituan, and Yueqing Sun of Meituan, with further authors from the National University of Singapore, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shanghai University, and the University of Oxford. The question is simple to state and hard to test: a multimodal model can describe a street view, but can it keep using that understanding after it starts walking? Turn a corner and the landmark that justified your last decision disappears, while its location still matters. The authors built a physically constrained replica of the whole of Hong Kong to find out, and the answer is that current agents hold up for one look and fall apart over a route.

First, the environment, because that is the actual artifact. The team took two public datasets from the Lands Department of the Hong Kong government: a three-D visualisation map, a tile-based textured mesh reconstructed from oblique aerial imagery covering the full territory, and a three-D pedestrian network of georeferenced walkable segments. Both are streamed into Unity in a shared geographic frame, tiles load according to the viewing scale, and the tile geometry serves double duty as rendered surface and collision geometry, so buildings and elevation changes physically constrain the agent. Trajectories are recorded in geographic coordinates. On top of that sit a simulation clock with day, dusk, and night lighting, weather including rain that produces particles and an exposure signal distinguishing covered paths from open streets, and a pedestrian population of animated avatars spawned on the registered network. The agent itself is a first-person character controlled by an external multimodal model over a client-server interface: it sees a first-person RGB image, optionally an interactive map with pan, zoom, and orbit, and it acts through a small structured action set: move, sprint, look, jump, open or close the map, and terminate. No privileged state is exposed, no remaining distance, no route API. Episodes cap at one hundred interaction steps, and since each movement lasts at most two seconds that is roughly two hundred seconds of commanded motion, which is exactly the budget human testers used to verify that every task is feasible.

A few engineering details matter if you want to use it. The simulator and the model run as separate processes talking over a client-server interface, so swapping in a different model or agent framework does not touch the Unity implementation. Time is part of the environment state and logged with every observation, which makes trajectories reproducible under a fixed clock, and the clock can be frozen for controlled comparisons or allowed to advance during an episode. The pedestrian population is drawn from the open Microsoft Rocketbox avatar library, spawned on the registered network, with positions and collisions recorded every step, so navigation success can be scored jointly with the safety of the executed trajectory. The map tool deliberately exposes a georeferenced overhead view and the agent's own position, but never a computed or highlighted route. And the team releases the whole thing: the sandbox runs in a browser and as native builds for macOS, Windows, and Linux, with the code and task definitions in a public repository.

The evaluation is organized as a ladder of eight hundred ten manually verified task instances across five levels: local understanding, navigation under explicit instructions, exploration under implicit instructions, multi-task planning, and dynamic interaction, with the city itself changing under the agent in the last level. Ten frontier models were evaluated, spanning the GPT, Claude, Gemini, Doubao, GLM, and Kimi families. Navigation success means finishing within fifteen meters of the destination with task constraints satisfied, and the more interesting metric is pedestrian-network adherence: the fraction of action time actually spent on registered walkable routes.

The results sort into three clean findings. On local tasks, models are competent. Visual recognition accuracy runs into the nineties for the best models, with Gemini three point six Flash at ninety-three point eight and Claude Opus five at ninety-one point three, and active exploration, where the agent must move to find missing evidence before answering, only moderately lowers accuracy, with Claude Opus five at eighty-two point five. But orientation is a shared atomic weakness: asked where things are relative to their heading, several models approach the random baseline for a four-option question, with Gemini three point one Pro at twenty-three point three percent and GPT five point four at thirty-one point seven, against fifty-eight point three for the best model. And there is a quietly damning behavioral metric: even when agents answer correctly, they cheat on foot. Adherence on local tasks sits in the seventies, and the paper's worked example shows GPT five point five correctly identifying the bank next to a pharmacy, then crossing the road directly to finish faster, leaving the pedestrian network entirely.

Second finding: local skill does not compose into navigation. On short-range goal navigation some models do well, seventy-five percent for GPT five point five and Claude Opus four point six. On the same point-to-point task at longer range, nearly every model scores zero, the best being three point eight percent. Overall navigation across all eight subtasks tops out under twenty-three percent. The paper decomposes this carefully: more than half of long-range episodes do end closer to the goal than they started, and multi-stop agents reach ten to twenty percent of their required destinations, so the models make real progress and then lose it. The failure mode is error accumulation without correction: one agent in the example figures repeatedly walking forward into an obstacle instead of revising its route, while a better episode shows the same model correctly traversing a complex footbridge. Instruction-following navigation is worse than distance alone explains, and distance-stratified analysis shows success declining continuously with horizon even within nominally short tasks. In other words, there is no clean short-task long-task split; closed-loop execution degrades with every extra step the plan must survive.

Third finding: the city fights back and agents do not adapt. Under dusk and night lighting, question-answering accuracy drops across the board, for instance Claude Opus five falls from seventy-nine point one to sixty-six point four at dusk, and interestingly dusk hurts more than night, which the authors attribute to lighting interference. But the navigation effect of weather and time is inconsistent across models, so appearance change alone does not explain the failures. The sharper result is the road-closure task: the simulator invalidates a pedestrian segment mid-route, discloses it once, and marks it on the map. Success stays at or near zero for every model, and the safe-progress rate, the fraction of episodes that even respect the closure, ranges only from ten to forty-seven percent, while network adherence stays above ninety-three. Agents keep producing locally compliant walking while their plan is obsolete. Under moving pedestrians, collision rates run seventy-six to ninety percent, so staying on the sidewalk network is not collision-aware control.

The skeptical assessment. This is an evaluation paper, and its claims are about the models it ran, ten of them, at API access, under a one-hundred-step cap, which is a fair but bounded window; longer-horizon agents with explicit map memory or search would need their own study. Success within fifteen meters is a reasonable but generous criterion, and the MLLM-judged or human-verified task instances, while manually checked, come from a small number of Hong Kong districts with distinctive terrain, so transfer of the conclusion to grid-plan cities is asserted, not measured. The adherence and collision metrics are the most novel contributions and the easiest to trust, being computed from simulator state rather than model output. What I would re-verify before building on this: how much of the long-range collapse is context-window engineering rather than spatial reasoning, since the observation bundle includes a text history whose length grows with the episode, and whether the dusk-worse-than-night effect replicates, because it is counterintuitive and unexplained. Bottom line: UrbanGround is a genuinely hard, geographically real benchmark that converts a vague worry about agent fragility into measured numbers, and its verdict is that spatial agency, not perception, is the binding constraint. The actionable takeaway for anyone building agent harnesses is that error correction, not error avoidance, is the missing primitive: every model here could start a route, and none could reliably save one.
