The Teacher Is a Direction, Not a Destination. The first three authors are Hao Li, MeiJia Chen, and Weijie Ren, working between the University of Science and Technology of China, Rutgers University, and Zhejiang University, with independent researchers and Naibo Wang completing the byline.

The setting is on-policy distillation, where a student samples its own trajectories and a teacher scores them token by token. The interesting variant is extrapolation: instead of matching the teacher, you push the student past it. The standard way to do that works in output space. You take the log-probability ratio of the teacher's sampled tokens relative to the base model, treat it as an implicit reward, and extrapolate beyond the teacher along that reward direction. The paper's claim is that this output-space route is structurally broken. The language model head attenuates the teacher's hidden-state change anisotropically. Most of the change encoded in the hidden states reaches the logits at only a fraction of its weight, and the sampled-token log-probability ratios that output-space extrapolation relies on inject noise that extrapolation then amplifies.

The authors back this up with a spectral measurement. On the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B pair, the 512 weakest directions of the centered head carry about 79.8 percent of the teacher-to-base residual's hidden-state energy, but only 30.0 percent of its centered-logit energy. The head is full rank, with singular values spanning from 71.7 down to 1.8, so nothing is collapsed. The attenuation is geometric, not degenerate. A proposition in the paper, stated for the output-space objective, says that conditioned on the prefix, the conditional variance of the extrapolation reward grows with the extrapolation coefficient, which is the formal version of the noise-amplification argument.

The fix, RIDE, moves the extrapolation into representation space. The observation that motivates it: reinforcement learning shifts a model's internal representations relative to its base checkpoint, and that shift has a measurable direction at every layer. RIDE computes, at every layer and every token position, the residual between the RL-trained teacher and its pre-RL checkpoint. It then regresses the student's hidden states toward a target that sits beyond the teacher, displaced along that residual by an extrapolation coefficient lambda. Conditioned on the sampled trajectory, this regression has a clean closed-form interpretation: it is maximizing a linear directional reward defined by the residual, under a quadratic penalty centered at the teacher. So the objective explicitly says: move along the RL direction, but pay a cost for deviating from the teacher. The penalty is what the output-space version lacks.

The training recipe is concrete. Four base-to-teacher pairs: the R1-Distill-1.5B pair, which is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B distilled into JustRL-DeepSeek-1.5B, plus Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.2-3B, and Phi-4-mini, where the RL teachers come from applying the same JustRL recipe to each base. RIDE supervises all layers and the last 2,000 response positions, with lambda at 1.25 and the loss scaled by lambda to the minus two. That scaling matters: it keeps the gradient magnitude stable as you push the target further from the teacher.

Now the numbers. Across all four pairs, RIDE approaches or exceeds the RL-trained teacher on every pair, and it is the only method whose mean does. It beats the output-space extrapolation baseline by 9.1 points on average. The clearest result is the ablation on lambda for the R1-Distill-1.5B pair. For RIDE, final average at 16 rises with lambda: 48.2 at lambda 0.5, 54.3 at 1, peaking at 55.4 at 1.25. The neighboring coefficients at 1.15 and 1.35 give 55.2 and 55.3, so the whole interval from 1.15 to 1.35 improves over plain teacher matching. Every run between 0.75 and 1.35 improves steadily over the training budget, with a format score of 94 to 97 percent. Beyond that, degradation is graceful: lambda 1.5 and 2 finish at 52.3 and 52.4.

Output-space extrapolation does the opposite. It is best at lambda at most 1, scoring 52.9 at 0.75 and at 1, and it is harmed by every lambda above 1. Its extrapolated runs peak early, around step 150, then decline, dropping to 49.9 at lambda 1.25 and 46.2 at lambda 2. The format score collapses from 92 percent at lambda 0.5 to 64 percent at lambda 2, and responses balloon to 8,700 tokens. There is also a regime where output-space extrapolation actively destroys the student: on the three pairs where the RL teacher sits only 2.8 to 6.0 points above its base, extrapolation falls below the untouched student, by as much as 14.1 points on Qwen3-4B. That is exactly the behavior the variance proposition predicts: the smaller the RL-induced signal, the more the noise dominates.

The mechanistic section is the part worth re-reading if you plan to implement this. On the R1-Distill-1.5B pair at lambda 1.25 and training step 500, the authors look at the final layer. The residual retains only 0.59 of the head gain, and the relative L2 error of the head-visible component is 0.5796, with the variance ratio of the two components at 12.55. Those directions that the head attenuates most receive 73.0 percent of the representation-loss gradient but only 48.6 percent of the output-space loss gradient. In other words, the representation-space objective spends its gradient on precisely the directions the output-space objective ignores. The student's hidden states align with the residual at cosine 0.954, and their head-visible component tracks the target at cosine 0.895, with a projection onto the residual of 1.69, above the target coefficient of 1.25. The student overshoots the target direction, which is consistent with the displacement being learned through regression rather than imposed.

A practical note for anyone reproducing this. The target regression is linear in the hidden states, so the per-step cost is a projection of the student hidden state onto the precomputed residual direction at each supervised position, plus a quadratic loss. There is no additional forward pass through the teacher beyond the one standard on-policy distillation already requires, and the pre-RL checkpoint is used only offline to compute the residual. That makes the method cheap to try on top of an existing distillation loop: the only new artifacts are the residual tensors, one per layer, and the choice of which trailing positions to supervise, which the paper fixes at 2,000 tokens. The project page links a code repository, which is worth checking before you reimplement the loss scaling yourself, because the lambda to the minus two factor is the kind of detail that silently changes the effective learning rate.

What I would trust here. The core claim that RL-induced change is best extrapolated in representation space is well supported: the spectral measurement, the variance proposition, and the lambda sweep all point the same way, and the graceful-degradation contrast with output-space extrapolation is a clean falsification of the baseline. The fact that RIDE beats the teacher on every pair, not just in aggregate, is a strong result. What I would re-verify before adopting it. First, all four teachers are produced by one RL recipe, JustRL, on small models in the 1.5B to 4B range. The residual direction may be recipe-specific, and it is an open question whether this survives a different RL objective or a frontier-scale teacher. Second, the evaluation is concentrated in math and reasoning with average at 16, so the breadth of the gains is narrower than the abstract suggests. Third, the method needs the pre-RL checkpoint of the teacher, which is available here because the RL run starts from a known base, but is not always available in practice. And the margin over plain on-policy teacher matching, while positive, is modest: 0.97 to 4.06 points, which is within the noise of a single evaluation seed on some pairs.

Bottom line. RIDE is a well-motivated fix to a real weakness in output-space distillation extrapolation, and the evidence that representation-space extrapolation is the right place to push beyond the teacher is the strongest result in this paper. The open question is whether the RL-induced direction stays well-behaved outside the JustRL recipe and the small-model regime where it was measured.
