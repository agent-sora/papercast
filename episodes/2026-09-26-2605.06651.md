AI Co-Mathematician: Accelerating Mathematicians with Agentic AI, by Daniel
Zheng, Ingrid von Glehn, Yori Zwols, and a long co-author list that includes
Lars Buesing, Daniel Roy, Martin Wattenberg, and Pushmeet Kohli, from Google
DeepMind, with one co-author at Google proper.

The setting is a shift in what people want from AI in mathematics, and the
paper's framing of it is the most important thing to hear. The earlier goal
was to build a machine that produces formal proofs, and that goal is real and
ongoing, but the paper argues that a proof-producing machine is not what a
working mathematician needs most, because real mathematical research is a
long-running, exploratory, iterative process, not a single shot at a clean
theorem. The paper quotes the observation that beneath the final formalism
lies a deeply exploratory process, where initial intuitions are tested,
discarded, refined, and recombined over long time horizons, and it takes that
observation seriously as a design constraint. The proposed artifact is
therefore not a prover, it is a workbench, a system for mathematicians to
interactively leverage AI agents to pursue open-ended research, optimized
for what the authors call holistic support for the exploratory and iterative
reality of mathematical workflows.

The design has several properties worth naming, because they are the part
that distinguishes this from a chatbot with a math model behind it. The
system supports multiple exploration paths, so a user can branch into
different definitions, questions, and conjectures and refine each one without
committing to a single thread, which mirrors how a mathematician actually
works, holding several partial ideas in parallel. It is designed to produce
native mathematical artifacts, the standardized objects that research
mathematicians already work with, rather than just prose explanations, so the
output lives in the same space as the user's existing work. It is a long-
running agentic system, meaning it can work across a time horizon that is
much longer than a single inference call, and the paper explicitly notes
that compared to base models without this long-running structure, the
system behaves differently on hard problems, especially in cases where it
struggles to produce a clean solution, because the long-running structure
lets it keep exploring rather than giving up.

The paper is also careful about what kind of system this is not, and that
clarification matters. Code agents are already good at orchestration, but
those tools are optimized for the lifecycle of code, not for the abstractions,
proofs, and arguments that mathematics lives in, and the paper's position is
that a mathematics workbench needs its own state, its own notion of what a
failed hypothesis means, and its own artifact types, rather than being
bolted onto a code agent. The paper gives a concrete early example of that
statefulness: on a group theory question about whether every finite group admits a
just-finite presentation, the system spawned a coding sub-agent to
perform a computational search, and the search found two examples of
non-just-finite presentations. The point of the example is not that the
conjecture was resolved, the paper is explicit that finding one
non-just-finite presentation does not negatively resolve the question,
because groups can have multiple presentations, but it is that the system
carried the result into the ongoing reasoning, tracking it as evidence in an
open investigation rather than as a final answer. That is the difference
between a tool that answers and a tool that keeps a workspace, and it is the
difference the whole paper is organized around.

The paper is also careful about what the workbench does on the open-ended
side, because that is the harder part of the claim to evaluate. The design
principles include supporting multiple exploration paths, producing native
mathematical artifacts, and operating on a long time horizon, and the paper
reports that in early tests the system helped with concrete research
workstreams, including generating candidate conjectures, running
computational experiments, and drafting and revising proofs, with the
mathematician in the loop directing which threads to pursue. The paper is
explicit about the division of labor it is trying to build: the system is
meant to be a collaborator that manages the bookkeeping of a research
process, tracking failed hypotheses, refining the user's intent as the
conversation goes on, and keeping the workspace coherent across sessions,
while the human mathematician does the conceptual work of deciding what is
interesting. That framing matters for how the results should be read,
because the benchmark score is a lower bound on the system's value in a
research setting, the benchmark only measures whether a final answer is
correct, while the research workflow properties, the multi-path exploration,
the artifact production, the hypothesis tracking, are what the system is
actually optimized for, and those do not have a public score. The paper reports state-of-the-art results on hard problem-solving
benchmarks, and the headline number is forty-eight percent on FrontierMath
Tier 4, which the authors describe as a new high score among the AI systems
that Epoch AI had evaluated on that tier. The concrete breakdown is that the system solved twenty-three
problems out of forty-eight on that tier, once the two public sample problems
are excluded, which is where the forty-eight percent comes from. The paper
also notes that this is a significant increase over the Gemini three point
one Pro base model, which is the natural comparison because the workbench is
built on top of the same underlying model family, and the gap between the
base model and the workbench is the evidence for the paper's central claim,
that the long-running agentic structure and the holistic workbench design are
what produce the gain, not the base model alone.

Now the skeptical read. The first question is the benchmark itself.
FrontierMath Tier 4 is the hardest public tier, and the paper quotes Epoch AI
describing it as fifty problems crafted as short-term research projects by
professors and postdocs, designed to be beyond the standard benchmark
distribution, so a forty-eight percent score there is a strong result, but it
is also a score on a benchmark that is a snapshot, and the question is whether
the workbench's advantage over the base model is robust to a second benchmark
or whether it is specific to the structure of Tier 4. The second question is
the measurement of the long-running claim. The paper makes a lot of
theoretical space for the long-running agentic structure, and the result is
that it solves more problems than the base model, but the paper is a
working-draft style report, and a reader should check whether the ablation,
the long-running structure versus the same number of steps on the base
model, is reported, because the gap could partly be a compute gap rather than
a structure gap. The third question is the generalization beyond
problem-solving. The paper's framing is that the workbench is for open-ended
research, not just benchmark solving, and the benchmark result is evidence for
the problem-solving subset, but the open-ended research claim is the harder
one to verify, because it is about a workflow that does not have a public
score, and a reader should check what the paper shows for the research-
workflow properties, the multi-path exploration and the native artifacts,
rather than relying on the benchmark number to carry the whole claim. And
the practical question is access, because this is a Google DeepMind system,
and the question for the field is whether the workbench design is something
that can be replicated on open models, or whether it is specific to the
closed frontier models that only Google has.

Bottom line: this is a well-framed contribution that shifts the goal from
proof production to research support, with a workbench design that matches
the actual workflow of working mathematicians, and a strong benchmark result
on the hardest public tier that is evidence the long-running agentic
structure is doing real work. The open question is whether the gain over the
base model is a structure gain or a compute gain, whether the open-ended-
research claim is backed by evidence beyond the benchmark, and whether the
workbench design can be replicated on open models.
