ACAP: Asynchronous Coding Agents for Efficient Robot Control, from Zexin He, Rui Li, and Yushi Lan, at S-Lab at Nanyang Technological University, the University of Oxford, and ACE Robotics. The headline claim: if you run one robot as a team of persistent coding agents instead of a single all-purpose agent, you cut task time by about 28 percent on real hardware.

Start with the problem. Coding-agent robot policies extend the Code as Policies idea: the agent inspects the scene, builds a numerical model, and writes and revises the control program during execution. That works, but reasoning takes time, and in the standard setup every decision about every robot component passes through one reasoning process. Diagnosing a failed grasp with the left arm delays an otherwise independent action with the right arm. The authors' argument is that distributing reasoning across robot components should let independent work proceed concurrently. The complication they have to handle is that independent reasoning does not imply independent physical action: the agents share one body, one arm's motion can change the reach or clearance of the other, and one agent's recovery can invalidate the other's plan.

The method has two layers. The first is the asynchronous framework. The robot is split into configured control groups, each bundling actuators, such as an arm with its gripper or a body group comprising the torso and chassis. Each group has a persistent member agent that runs its own observe, reason, code, execute loop at its own pace. Members can use lighter reasoning configurations for local subtasks, while a more capable lead agent reasons about the whole task. The framework supplies four scaffolding mechanisms. Persistent contexts: each member keeps its observations, conversation history, and generated programs across assignments and control transfers within an episode, so it can pick up where it left off. Message buffers: reports and instructions persist until the receiving agent confirms, so agents can work at different speeds. Event-based waits: a synchronization point specifies which actions must wait and what evidence lets them proceed, and the hyper-agent specifies which events are required, any or all, while other work continues. And exclusive control ownership: commands for a control group are only accepted from its current owner, ownership cannot change while a controller call is in flight, and transfers preserve controller targets so responsibility can move without stopping motion or releasing a held object.

The second layer is the hyper-agent. This is the interesting part. Instead of handcrafted coordination rules, a coordinating policy decides, at each of its own decision steps, what to assign, what to revise, what to synchronize, and, crucially, when the lead should take direct control of multiple groups for a tightly coupled action. The paper formalizes this as a policy that selects an action from the interaction history and the current ownership map, where ownership maps each control group to either the lead or its member. An action can delegate an objective with an allowed scope, authorize access to a shared workspace, or arrange direct lead execution of a coupled action. The decision comes from feedback rather than a supplied task graph. The framing in the paper is that the hyper-agent's action space is small and the frontier coding agent is capable enough to make the task-dependent choices itself.

A concrete example helps. In their four-flower insertion task, two member agents prepare and orient their flowers independently, while the hyper-agent coordinates access to the shared vase: only vase entry waits for the access grant, so preparation overlaps with insertion. In towel folding, the lead instead takes over both arms for the shared fold while the members keep doing analysis. The same interface, two different coordination topologies, chosen at run time.

Now the evaluation, and this is where the paper earns its keep. All experiments are on real robots: a Galaxea R1 Pro and four AgileX PiPER-family setups. They define three task categories by physical dependency: Fully Parallel, where control groups execute independent subtasks; Parallel-Sequential, where actions depend on another group's progress or access to a shared workspace; and Collaborative, where groups jointly support, move, or deform an object. Three policy variants share the same observations, tools, and control interface. Direct is the single-agent baseline. Fixed Delegation gives a lead that assigns and synchronizes, but only members can command their assigned groups; ownership never returns to the lead. ACAP is the full method. For the model, the lead and hyper-agent run in a single session of a frontier coding agent with its highest reasoning effort, while members use the same model at a medium reasoning setting.

The task-coverage study runs 72 episodes across 25 task and platform settings. Time cost is elapsed time from policy start to outcome, with a 30-minute floor for non-completion, and they also score graded physical progress on a 0 to 100 scale with human annotators and report the area under the progress curve. Results: across all 25 settings, Direct averages 19.04 minutes, ACAP 13.67, a 28.2 percent reduction. By category, Fully Parallel drops from 20.49 to 13.22 minutes, Parallel-Sequential from 20.27 to 13.86, and Collaborative from 16.25 to 13.75. The physical-progress AUC goes from 62.3 to 72.9, meaning ACAP reaches more of the physical goal earlier, not just faster at the finish.

The coordination question gets its own comparison. On the eight Collaborative settings, ACAP beats Fixed Delegation in mean time cost on every single one, and ACAP also beats Direct on average. The paper's interpretation is that Fixed already has multiple agents, lead planning, and synchronization; the marginal value of ACAP is the option to execute coupled actions directly. Their bottle-opening example is the cleanest: ACAP keeps stabilization and twisting delegated and is the only policy that completes the task, while both baselines fail after the arms cross.

A repeated-trial study with 90 episodes, five trials per task and policy across six tasks, shows lower mean 30-minute-capped time cost than Direct on all six tasks. They also extend the same member interface to mobile manipulation, with a body member driving the chassis and torso alongside two arm members. One thing worth noting: they release a real-world rollout dataset with observations, generated programs, agent interactions, actions, and physical feedback, which is the kind of artifact the embodied-agent community has been missing.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? First, the headline 28.2 percent is a task-balanced mean across 25 settings with a 30-minute floor, and the per-category numbers show the win is concentrated in the parallel categories; on Collaborative tasks the gap is modest, 16.25 versus 13.75, which makes sense because coupled manipulation is exactly where concurrency buys less. Second, the whole result rides on a single frontier coding agent family with its highest reasoning tier for the lead; the cost of running multiple concurrent reasoning sessions is real, and the paper reports time but not dollar cost or token usage, so the efficiency gain needs to be weighed against inference spend before you call it a win in production. Third, the hyper-agent is doing a lot of implicit work; the coordination quality is as model-dependent as anything else here, and the Fixed Delegation comparison, while clean, compares against a baseline where ownership can never return, which is a somewhat weak straw man for the direct-takeover mechanism. And the task set, while diverse, is 25 settings on two robot families; cross-embodiment generalization of the coordination behavior is untested.

Bottom line: this is a well-measured, genuinely real-world study showing that splitting a coding-agent robot policy into persistent per-component agents with a dynamic hyper-agent cuts task time by roughly a quarter and beats fixed delegation on every collaborative setting. The architecture is a practical template for multi-agent robot control, but the gains are model-dependent, the dollar cost is unreported, and the coordination layer is only as good as the frontier agent behind it.
