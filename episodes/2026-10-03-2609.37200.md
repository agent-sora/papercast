---Adaptive Reward Routing, the first three authors are Songlin Yang, Xiaotong Zhao, and Jiacheng Zhang, working at MMLab at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Tencent Video, and the University of Hong Kong.

The problem starts from a simple observation. Modern joint audio-video diffusion models, like the LTX family, must satisfy several things at once: the video has to look good, the audio has to sound good, the two have to agree about what is happening, and they have to stay in sync in time. You cannot capture all four in one supervised objective, so the field has moved to reward-guided reinforcement learning, where each concern is a separate reward. The methods that exist, including GRPO-style approaches and DiffusionNFT, work, but they treat two things as fixed: where in the model the reward-driven updates are allowed to act, and how the competing rewards are weighted. This paper argues both of those quantities are actually dynamic, they change as the model trains, and a fixed setup cannot track them.

It helps to know what model and what training loop they are working inside. The base model is LTX-2, a joint audio-video diffusion transformer with separate audio and video streams sharing one denoising timestep. Each stream runs its own self-attention and its own text cross-attention, and the two streams exchange information through bidirectional cross-attention blocks, audio attending to video and video attending to audio, with the cross-attention outputs gated back into each stream. The training loop is DiffusionNFT, a forward-process form of reinforcement learning for diffusion models. Instead of learning a critic, it builds an implicit positive policy and an implicit negative policy from the velocity predictor, samples a group of rollouts per prompt, and scores each rollout by a group-relative advantage, the rollout's reward minus the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation. A rollout above the group average pushes the positive policy; one below it pushes the negative policy. There is no learned value function anywhere. The multi-reward layer sits on top: rewards are split into video rewards, audio rewards, and cross-modal rewards, and each one gets its own advantage. The baselines for combining them are GDPO, which combines the advantages with fixed predefined weights, preserving your stated preferences but unable to react to conflicts that emerge during training, and MARBLE, which picks simplex weights that minimize the norm of the combined reward gradients, adapting to the local gradient geometry but ignoring which objective you actually said mattered. The paper's claim is that neither is enough: preference and geometry are both needed, and both have to move.

The method has two components, and it is useful to separate them, because they solve two different problems. The first is cross-modal influence-guided routing, which decides where the updates act. The intuition is that during training, some layers of the transformer carry more of the cross-modal interaction than others, and the layers that matter shift as the model learns. The paper uses the bidirectional cross-attention responses between the audio and video branches as a cheap proxy for that influence. It collects those responses over an intermediate-to-late window of the denoising process, normalizes them with a percentile-clipped min-max normalization they call Norm99, and uses the resulting scores to reweight token-level losses and to scale the gradients flowing through the cross-modal layers. The important property is that this requires no additional model interventions, no extra networks, no auxiliary heads, just a readout of attention that is already being computed. In the full pipeline the routing acts at four levels at once: reward reweighting, modality-branch assignment, token-level credit allocation, and layer-wise cross-modal gradient scaling. The second component is preference-preserving modality-aware reweighting, which decides how the rewards coordinate with each other. The setup is that you have predefined weights for each reward, and those weights encode what you care about. A naive scheme lets the reward with the biggest gradient dominate early and then suppress the weaker rewards, so a weak but essential objective, say audio-video synchronization, can starve. Their approach keeps the predefined weights as preference priors and then uses the branch-specific reward-gradient interactions as residual corrections, but only after a warm-up period. So the intended weighting is never thrown away, it is corrected by what the gradients are actually doing, and the correction is computed separately inside the video branch and the audio branch, because a conflict that looks the same from the joint objective can mean different things for each modality.

The evaluation is on JavisBench, a benchmark for joint audio-video generation, and the results are reported as mean and standard deviation over three seeds, on two backbones, LTX-2 and LTX-2.3. The metrics are grouped in four families: visual quality and audio quality, text consistency via image-text and text-audio scores plus CLIP and CLAP, audio-video consistency via a joint image-text-audio score and AVHScore, and synchronization via a DeSync score where lower is better. Let me give you the LTX-2 numbers, because they are the cleanest. The base model, with no reinforcement learning at all, has a visual quality of 1.883 and an audio quality of 5.201, a CLIP of 0.312, a CLAP of 0.358, and a DeSync of 0.604, with the overall JavisScore at 0.604. The strongest RL baseline they run is OmniNFT, and with a tuned variant, OmniNFT star, it gets visual quality to about 3.3, audio quality to 5.6, CLAP to 0.422, and brings DeSync down to 0.378, a JavisScore of 0.378. The proposed method, with the full routing plus reweighting stack, gets visual quality to 3.336 with a standard deviation of 0.009, audio quality to 5.868, CLIP up to 0.314, CLAP to 0.425, the joint consistency score to 0.235, and the best DeSync of 0.341. So the win is a consistent, moderate one: better than the strongest baseline on quality, on consistency, and on synchronization simultaneously, rather than trading one metric for another. On the newer LTX-2.3 backbone the same pattern holds, with visual quality reaching 3.599 and audio quality 5.979.

The ablations are worth spending a moment on, because they attribute the gain to the two components separately. On the routing side, adding token-level weighting to the baseline gives visual quality of 3.008, adding layer scaling gives 3.192, and combining both gives 3.315, so the two routing mechanisms compose. On the reward side, the gray rows of their ablation table isolate the reweighting components from the MARBLE baseline, and the branch-aware correction is what pushes the synchronization and consistency metrics up without dragging quality down. There is also a mechanism analysis, which I found to be the most convincing part of the paper. They take the proxy scores from the routing component and test whether they actually identify functionally important tokens. They block the top-scoring ten percent of target tokens against the random ten percent and the bottom-scoring ten percent, and measure how much the final prediction changes. Blocking the top-scoring group changes the prediction 1.62 times more than blocking the bottom group. The point is that the proxy is not just a heuristic that correlates with the results, it is measuring something real about where the cross-modal computation lives.

A few caveats. The gains over the best baseline are real but modest in absolute terms, and the standard deviations are tiny, which is partly a function of the small number of seeds and the controlled benchmark. JavisBench is a good benchmark, but it is one benchmark, and joint audio-video generation is a field where a method that wins there can lose on a different prompt distribution. The two components also add tuning surface: the denoising window where you read the attention, the percentile clipping, the warm-up length before the residual corrections kick in. The paper reports the settings that worked, but the sensitivity to those choices is not fully mapped. And the method is specific to forward-process RL of diffusion models with explicit cross-attention between modalities, so it does not transfer to a unified-token model where audio and video share one sequence.

To sum up, this is a well-motivated paper that identifies a real gap, fixed routing and fixed weights in multi-reward diffusion RL, and fills it with a clean, cheap mechanism that needs no extra model components, backed by a mechanism analysis that validates the proxy it relies on. The evidence is solid but the margins are modest, and the practical takeaway is that if you are already running multi-reward RL on a joint audio-video model, dynamic routing and preference-preserving reweighting are the two knobs worth adding before you reach for a bigger model.
