The paper is PrismaCoder, from Qiushi Sun, Jingyang Gong, and Yang Liu, all with equal contribution, with the team spanning the University of Hong Kong, Nanjing University, Fudan University, and Huawei's Foundation Model Department. The problem is multimodal code intelligence, the ability to produce programs whose value is judged by what they render: charts, web pages, diagrams, TikZ figures, animations, typeset documents. Proprietary models are far ahead here, and the authors' diagnosis of why open progress is stuck is specific. It is not just the quantity of data, it is the quality signal. A visual-coding sample has to be correct as a program, well constructed as code, and visually right once rendered, and no existing single signal covers all three. Execution returns a pass or fail, which says nothing about visual quality. Code similarity does not map to the rendered output. And task-specific verifiers do not generalize across the different task domains. So the data pipeline is the bottleneck, not the model architecture.

Their answer is PrismaForge, a hierarchical data pipeline centered on a synthesized rubric. Instead of a holistic score or a fixed checklist, they generate a per-instruction rubric that decomposes the quality factors into individual checkable criteria. The rubric combines reusable domain-level checks, things that are true for any chart or any web page, with instruction-specific requirements pulled from the particular task, and the number of criteria is adjusted to the estimated difficulty of the task. The rubric is synthesized hierarchically, from coarse to fine: the first level captures domain-level quality that any sample in a category must satisfy, whether the artifact renders at all, whether it is internally consistent, whether the layout matches the category's conventions, and the second level is instruction-specific, capturing whatever this particular request asked for beyond the defaults, a specific chart type, a particular color scheme, a named element in the figure.

Critically, the rubric then decomposes into two families of checkable criteria, code-level and visual-level, and they are evaluated on different objects. Code-level criteria are checked against the generated program itself: does it run, are the required functions present, is the data pipeline correct. Visual-level criteria are checked against the rendered artifact: does the chart show the right series, do the axis labels match the instruction, does the figure contain the element the user asked for. That separation is what makes the signal usable in reinforcement learning, because a reward that conflates the two would punish a correct program rendered by a flaky screenshot pipeline, or reward a good-looking image produced by code that silently ignored half the instruction. The same rubric then plays two roles: it curates the supervised training corpus, acting as a filter, and it drives reinforcement learning, acting as a reward. From this they build PrismaCoder-2M, which they describe as the largest and most diverse open dataset for multimodal code intelligence, spanning more than twelve categories and ten programming languages, with both text-centric inputs, a description sometimes with code, and vision-centric inputs, an image with an instruction. The seeds come from complementary sources: public visual-coding corpora for common plotting tasks, research figures and open-source repositories for long-tail scientific examples, and web and interactive-demo sources for the interactive end.

On the model side they train and release both PrismaCoder-9B and PrismaCoder-35B on this data. The results are the strongest of the day's open releases. On front-end generation, both models lead the open baselines with judge scores more than twenty points higher than the task-specific open models, and the 9B model in particular posts the largest improvement over its own base model of anything in their table, surpassing most proprietary systems in that column. On DesignBench generation and the WebCode2M CLIP score, the 9B model scores above a proprietary model. On ArtifactsBench, both models exceed all open baselines. The rubric itself gets a direct validation, which is the piece I would weight most: they fine-tune the same base model on two equal-sized subsets of the same data pool, one filtered by a single holistic judge score, the other by the PrismaForge rubric, and the rubric-filtered model wins. They also sample a hundred examples and have human annotators plus three proprietary model judges score them both ways, finding that rubric-based judging achieves substantially higher agreement with human judgments and more consistent induced orderings than direct scoring. One subtlety in that experiment: preserving the relative ordering induced by scores is particularly important for reward-based policy optimization, because inconsistent rankings can provide conflicting training signals, so their agreement measure weights ordering as much as level.

The category coverage is where the diversity claim is either real or not, and the paper's own figure gives the honest answer. The corpus spans twenty-plus scenario categories, from chart generation and chart-to-code conversion to TikZ figures, theorem visualization, flowcharts, interactive artifacts, scientific demos, SVG generation, animation, LaTeX documents, music scores, chemical structures, and web user interface editing and generation. The categories are not evenly weighted. Plotting and web-interface tasks dominate, which is where the seeds were richest, and the long-tail scientific categories contribute a thinner slice. That has a concrete consequence for the benchmark results: the models' strength on the main generation benchmarks tracks where the data is dense, and the columns where they trail proprietary systems, editing in particular, are also the columns where the category is more demand-specific and the rubric criteria harder to synthesize, because an editing instruction's intent lives in the delta between before and after, not in the artifact alone. The two released model sizes give a useful read on data efficiency. The 9B model's jump over its base is the largest relative gain in their tables, which the authors read as evidence that the corpus is high-signal rather than merely high-volume, and that a small model can absorb most of the value. The 35B model extends the gains into the proprietary-competitive range but does not change the pattern: both models' weaknesses are in the same categories, which is a data-coverage signature, not an architecture signature.

The skeptical part. The whole result rests on the rubric judge, and a judge is a model with its own biases, so the question is whether the rubric's per-criteria scores are actually grounded or whether a strong judge is rationalizing a holistic preference into decomposed numbers. The human-agreement experiment addresses this, but on a hundred examples, which is enough to show direction and not enough to rule out the rubric rewarding the judge's aesthetic rather than task correctness. There is also a family-conflict issue they handle honestly: some of the designated judges share a model family with the pipeline, so they verify that the headline gains hold across judge models rather than trusting one favorable judge. The proprietary comparison is the softest claim: leading open models by a wide margin is credible and verifiable, but rivaling leading proprietary systems is true on some columns and false on others, and DesignBench editing is the one web-interface metric where both models fall below every Claude and Gemini variant they test. The dataset is the more durable asset here, two million samples across twelve categories and ten languages, but its composition skews toward the domains their seeds covered, and long-tail visual languages like molecular structures or music scores get far less coverage. And the reinforcement learning reward built on the same rubric inherits its failure modes: if the rubric over-weights a criterion like color fidelity, the RL policy will learn to game that criterion.

The verdict: this is a serious data-side contribution to open multimodal code models, and the reusable idea is the rubric-as-both-filter-and-reward design, which decouples what a sample must satisfy from any single holistic judgment. If you are building open visual-coding models, PrismaCoder-2M is the dataset to start from and the 9B model is a credible baseline to beat; if you are building evaluation, the rubric-versus-direct-judge comparison is a template for how to make your data filtering agree with humans.
