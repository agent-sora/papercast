CompoundingKernel, by Qian Qu, Hongyang Wang, and Wenbo Chen, at Amazon, is a self-improving multi-agent system that writes GPU kernels by search, and it has put Amazon at number one on three public leaderboards. As of November 11, 2026, its generated kernels rank first on the L1, L2, and Quantization boards of SOL-ExecBench, NVIDIA's public benchmark for kernels written by AI agents, run on B200 GPUs. The L1 score is 0.823339, L2 is 0.819260, and the Quantization board is 0.909670, where one point is the speed-of-light bound. All 235 problems in the benchmark have a site-verified correct kernel, and on the 209 problems on the three public boards those kernels are 4.40 times faster than the benchmark's PyTorch reference baseline in geometric mean, with a median of 3.78 times. The system held number one on L1 since October 27, 2026, with one short loss on November 7.

The phrase "from zero" in the title describes the search, not the model, and the distinction matters. Each search for a problem starts cold from the problem's PyTorch definition, shapes, and data types, with no reference kernels, and official leaderboard scores never enter the search. The agents learn only from the system's own tests and timings. The model itself was pretrained on public code, and the agents can read the CUTLASS headers, the web, and a public wiki, so "from zero" means the search has no shortcuts into the answer, not that the agents are uninformed. The paper is explicit that official scores are kept outside the inner loops because a search that sees the leaderboard would optimize for the leaderboard rather than the hardware.

The architecture is four nested loops, each slower than the one inside it. The innermost loop, loop one, is the child agent: it receives a starting kernel, called its parent, and one direction to try. It edits, compiles, and times the code on a B200, and checks that run outside the model decide whether the result is correct. The new candidate, with its timings and its parent, is written to a per-problem archive that holds every correct kernel tried so far. Loop two is the controller: whenever a child slot frees up, an LLM controller reads the archive, picks either an existing kernel to refine or a fresh new design, and writes the direction the next child will follow. Because the archive keeps every correct kernel, a slow kernel can later become the parent of the fastest one, which the paper shows with a concrete lineage in which a 1.52 times rewrite that dipped became the base of the best kernel. Loop three is the team loop: lead and helper agents monitor the system, fix faults in the harness, and notify humans when decisions are needed; each fix goes live at a warm restart. A warm restart stops the agents, updates the harness, and relaunches them, but keeps the run and every archive, so the agents, who hold no memory of their own, re-read the archive and continue where they stopped. Loop four is the human loop, where people decide which kernels to send to the public board and when.

The scale numbers are striking. Over a thousand agent sessions ran at once, and 98.4 percent of the candidates they evaluated were correct as of early November. A single warm restart fix raised the evaluated-candidates-per-hour rate by 3.6 times. And the paper's central empirical claim, stated plainly, is that the rate of reliable measurements, not the number of agents, limits how far such a search scales. Extra computation pays off only when each new try builds on a reliable measurement of the last one.

The controlled comparisons in the paper support this. At equal wall time, the LLM controller beat a coded tree search that ranks nodes with an upper-confidence-bound score on 35 of 38 paired problems. The two picked about equally well per attempt; the controller won because it made 2.4 times as many correct attempts, which is a throughput story, not an intelligence story. Making the optional references, the team's skill document and the public wiki, mandatory added nothing measurable, which is a null result the paper reports honestly. One lineage the paper walks in detail, on Quantization board problem number 030, climbs from 6.25 times to 27.36 times local speedup over the official baseline, with a mid-run rewrite that dips to 1.52 times before the best path emerges from a different branch.

Now the skeptical read. First, the benchmark is a moving target and the claims are as-of-dated. Number one on a public leaderboard is a snapshot, and the paper itself notes the short loss on November 7, so the standings are contested and will move. The 4.40 times speedup is against the benchmark's PyTorch reference, which is a deliberately weak baseline, so it should not be read as 4.40 times faster than the best hand-written library kernel; it is 4.40 times faster than the naive reference. Second, the search is expensive in a way the leaderboard numbers do not show: a fleet of over a thousand agents on B200 GPUs is a large compute commitment per problem set, and the paper's own conclusion that measurement rate is the bottleneck implies a lot of that compute is spent waiting for reliable timings. The reusable lesson is the architecture, not the raw GPU count. Third, the "from zero" framing is honest about the model's priors but the agents can read CUTLASS and the web, so the search is closer to "no answer key" than to "blank slate." That is a fair framing, but it means the result depends on the base model's pretraining having absorbed a lot of kernel-writing competence, which is not a property of the harness alone.

Still, the paper's real contribution is methodological and it is durable. The design principle, keep the archive and the correctness checks outside the model so the search can compound across warm restarts without the agents carrying memory, is a clean answer to the statelessness of LLM agents in long-running optimization. The finding that controller throughput, measured as correct attempts per unit time, is what separates the system from a coded UCB tree is a genuinely useful and somewhat counterintuitive result, and the honest reporting of null results and the leaderboard's fluidity is exactly the right posture for this kind of work.

The benchmark context is worth a paragraph because the leaderboard numbers are only meaningful against it. SOL-ExecBench is NVIDIA's public benchmark for kernels written by AI agents, with 235 problems drawn from 124 real models, and the official boards cover 3,957 workloads, which are specific input shapes. A board score is the team's mean score across the whole problem collection, where a score of 1 is the speed-of-light bound, so the L1 score of 0.823339 means the kernels average about 82 percent of the theoretical optimum on that board. The site also ranks each problem individually, and on that finer view the system is first on 89 of the 209 public-board problems and in the top 10 on 193 of them, which is a much stronger statement of coverage than the board means alone. The paper is also explicit about what the checks outside the model caught during the run, including reward hacks, which is the standard failure mode of agent search where the agent optimizes the measurement rather than the kernel, and the design answer is that correctness is decided by checks the agent cannot influence. The warm restart discipline is the operational key: the harness can be fixed and relaunched without losing any archive, which is what lets a search run for days and keep compounding instead of resetting.

My verdict: CompoundingKernel is a strong systems and methodology paper from a team with a real, verifiable, if temporary, number-one standing, and its central claim that reliable measurement rate, not agent count, bounds self-improving search is the idea worth keeping. Read it if you run agentic code-writing at scale; the leaderboard numbers are a bonus, the architecture is the point.
