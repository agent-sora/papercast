From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation, by Minh-Anh Nguyen from the Nanyang Technological University, is a paper about how to make System One decision models cheaper without losing accuracy, and the central claim is that the decision is computed in the middle of the network, so a single model should be able to make easy decisions early and hard decisions later, and the method they build around that is called NESTONE.

To set up the problem, a System One decision model is a model that takes a state, a typed question, and a set of options, and returns a distribution over those options in a single forward pass without generating any text. They are fast and cheap, which is the whole point, and the standard way to build one today is to take a pretrained language model, replace the language-modeling head with a decision head, and keep the full backbone. The problem with that is that every decision now pays for all the layers, even the ones that do not contribute to the decision. The question the paper asks is how much depth a System One decision actually needs, and whether you can stop the computation early when the decision is easy.

The first finding is about where the decision actually lives. Using integrated-gradient attribution, they find that the decision information is concentrated in a narrow band of intermediate layers, with half of the decision by layer twenty and eighty percent by layer twenty-three. They confirm this with ablations, where removing the top six layers, layers twenty-four through twenty-nine, leaves the accuracy essentially unchanged, but ablating the middle band, layers fifteen through twenty-three, eliminates the decision margin entirely. So the early layers are building the features, the middle layers are doing the deciding, and the final six layers are removable. They also find that the truncation curve is highly non-uniform, where going from thirty-two layers down to twenty-four largely preserves performance, but truncating further drops accuracy sharply, from about eighty-four percent down to about seventy-three and then about sixty-two at sixteen and twelve layers. That non-uniformity is the key observation, because it means a fixed-depth truncation is a blunt instrument, it either keeps too much for the easy cases or cuts too much for the hard ones.

The second finding is about what independently trained smaller models actually learn. This is the negative result I find most interesting. You might think that if you want a cheaper decision model you just train a smaller one, but they find that independently trained smaller models behave more like truncated copies of the full model rather than like independent specialists. About eighty-nine to ninety-two percent of their correct decisions are already made by the full model, and their distinct wins exceed the full model's own seed-to-seed disagreement by only two to three points, and those wins are mostly on low-confidence decisions. The implication is that training a separate small model is not really giving you a specialized small decision-maker, it is mostly giving you a smaller version of the same thing, so the efficiency you get comes from a smaller backbone, not from a genuinely different computation.

Both findings point the same way, which is that efficiency does not come from simply using a smaller backbone, it comes from sharing computation across different depths, so that the same model can decide at the depth the input actually needs. NESTONE is the method that implements this. It jointly trains the full model together with nested prefix sub-models that share the full model's final layers and its decision head, using decision supervision plus self-distillation from the full model. The result of one training run is a single checkpoint that has multiple exits at different depths. At each exit, a lightweight gate reads the hidden state and decides whether more computation is needed before it produces the decision, so the model can stop early on easy inputs and continue to a deeper exit on hard ones. That is the adaptive computation, the early-exiting that lets one model operate at multiple layers.

The results are that NESTONE matches or outperforms the independently trained models at every depth, while getting a better accuracy-versus-efficiency tradeoff through the adaptive computation. They use a Qwen 3.5 four-billion-parameter backbone across seven decision benchmarks, and the headline is that on the larger benchmarks the gate-driven adaptive model keeps its accuracy within a tenth of a point of the fixed twenty-four-layer exit while running fewer layers on average, and the gate's average depth tracks the share of easy traffic, so when more of the inputs are easy it stops earlier.

Now the skeptical part. The first thing to check is the attribution, because the whole method is built on the claim that the decision lives in a narrow middle band, and integrated-gradient attribution is a useful probe but it is an approximation, and the specific band it identifies is a property of the model and the data, not a universal fact. The fact that the final six layers are removable is a strong and useful result, but it is a result at this scale and on these benchmarks, and I would want to see it hold across model families and on harder decision tasks before I built an early-exiting architecture around it, because if the decision band moves, the exits you trained do not line up with where the computation actually happens.

The second concern is the gate. The lightweight hidden-state gate is the piece that makes the adaptive part work, and it is also the piece that is easiest to get subtly wrong. If the gate under-estimates the difficulty of a hard input, it exits too early and you lose accuracy on exactly the cases where you need the depth, and if it over-estimates, you do not save any compute. The accuracy-within-a-tenth-of-a-point claim on the larger benchmarks is reassuring, but the interesting number is the tail, the accuracy on the genuinely hard inputs, because the adaptive design's whole value is that it spends depth where it matters, and an aggregate number can hide a gate that is systematically early on the hard tail. I would want the per-difficulty-bucket accuracy, not just the average.

Third, this is a single-author, preprint-stage paper, which is fine, but it means the results have not gone through the pressure of a full training-infrastructure buildout. The nested prefix training with shared final layers and self-distillation is a real training pipeline, and the practical question is the cost of running it and the complexity of serving a checkpoint with multiple exits and a gate, which is more moving parts than a plain model. The engineering gotcha, if you adopt this, is that the gate has to run at every exit on every input, and the latency it saves only materializes if the gate itself is cheap enough, so the net win depends on the gate cost being negligible relative to the layers it skips.

The comparison to layer pruning is the part that clarifies what is new here. There is a well-established line of work on removing deep layers from language models, where you find that the later blocks are partly redundant on question answering and that merging or pruning layers followed by distillation recovers most of the accuracy at a smaller size. But those methods produce one smaller model with one fixed depth, and they select the layers to keep by similarity or importance scores computed for the generation task, not by where a decision is actually computed. NESTONE is different in two specific ways. It is trained for the decision task, so the depth structure is learned for deciding rather than inherited from a generation-oriented pruning criterion, and it keeps multiple exits in one checkpoint rather than committing to a single depth. That second point is what the self-distillation is for. By distilling from the full model at each nested exit, the shallow exits learn to produce the full model's decision at reduced depth, which is the mechanism that lets a four-layer or eight-layer exit be competitive rather than just a truncated approximation. The distinction between pruning for generation and nested training for decision depth is the conceptual contribution, even if the underlying redundancy it exploits is familiar.

The bottom line is this: finding that the decision is computed in a narrow middle band, that the top layers are removable, and that independently trained small models mostly replicate the full model rather than specialize, is a clean and useful set of results, and the nested multi-depth design with a hidden-state stopping gate is a sensible way to turn them into an accuracy-efficiency win. But the attribution band and the gate's behavior on the hard tail are the two things I would verify on my own models and tasks before I rewired a decision-serving pipeline around early exiting.
