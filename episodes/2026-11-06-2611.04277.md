Harness-S, Learning from Sibling Harnesses for Generalizable Agent Training, is from Kangning Zhang, Wenxiang Jiao, and Bowen Fang, working at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Xiaohongshu, the Zhongguancun Institute of Artificial Intelligence, Southeast University, and Carnegie Mellon University.

Every agent you have run is really two things: the model weights, and the harness around them. The harness is the prompt, the tool interfaces, the memory mechanism, the context management, the execution rules. The paper starts from a claim that is easy to state and hard to act on: the harness is not a neutral wrapper. Hold the weights fixed and the harness still changes the information the policy sees and how its decisions become actions. ReAct interleaves reasoning and acting; SWE-agent shows the agent-computer interface itself shapes task success. A lot of recent work automates the optimization of prompts, workflows, and harness code from execution feedback. The question this paper asks is whether a specialized harness can improve the policy itself, so that the benefit persists when you throw the special harness away and go back to the original one.

The setup is harness evolution from a single base, called H0, within one execution system. The variants that share the execution foundation of a complete system but modify how it supports the policy are called sibling harnesses. The paper identifies three properties of how siblings behave, and these are the load-bearing observations. Locality: a harness edit typically repairs a subset of failures while introducing regressions on tasks the base already solved, so each variant develops a distinct strength. Complementarity: siblings recover one another's errors; the oracle union of a set of siblings covers more tasks than any individual variant, or than repeated sampling of the base at the same rollout count. Nonstationarity: a harness's utility changes as the executing policy learns. An advantage over the base at one checkpoint can become a disadvantage at a later one, and the ranking of variants can reverse as training proceeds.

These three properties expose two limits of the obvious approaches. First, you cannot just accumulate local repairs into a single universal harness. Repairs may impose incompatible execution requirements, and even compatible edits interfere with one another, so a single accumulated design loses the strengths of the parts it merged. Second, evolving the harness and the policy together is expensive and it limits reuse: you keep regenerating and re-validating variants as earlier repairs stop helping the changing policy, and the gains may not transfer when the policy returns to the base.

So the paper's move is to not merge. Instead of building one better harness, Harness-S selectively rescues the failures of the base execution system using sibling harnesses, and then transfers the resulting capabilities into a single policy that stays effective under the original harness. Directional harness evolution develops specialized siblings along distinct repair directions, forming a reusable pool that stays fixed during policy training. Complementary harness rescue training only invokes the pool when all initial attempts under the base harness have failed. The same policy generates fresh rollouts under selected auxiliary harnesses, and those trajectories replace the failed group for reinforcement learning. An adaptive router selects the top-k variants using harness behavior profiles and voting across multiple large models, and a policy-aware re-anchoring step refreshes that evidence as the policy learns. The key discipline is that the siblings are a training-time scaffolding, not the deployed system. At test time you are back at H0.

The results are on AppWorld, SWE-Gym, and DBBench, with the model being Qwen3.5-4B. The central comparison is a policy trained with the rescue pipeline versus a policy trained policy-only under the base harness, and the point of the experiment is that the trained policy substantially outperforms the policy-only training when it is returned to the original base harness. That is the transfer claim, and it is the part that distinguishes this from just routing at inference. On AppWorld, the full training plus test comparison shows the adaptive top-8 variant with profile re-anchoring reaching 67.56 pass at 3 and 56.87 average at 3, against 60.31 and 48.98 for the base GRPO policy-only. The rescue-success breakdown by training stage is where the nonstationarity shows up: the fraction of rescue-triggering tasks with at least one successful rollout fluctuates as the policy learns, and the adaptive profile variant beats the fixed-profile top-8 and the rule-based router throughout.

There is a behavioral analysis that is worth more than the leaderboard numbers. Under the base harness, the paper has a large model label complete evaluation trajectories and report the fraction exhibiting particular behaviors. Schema lookup before first API use goes from 22.9 percent at base, to 38.2 under plain GRPO, to 67.6 under Harness-S. Explicit pagination loops go from 13.8 to 38.3. Repeated execute-code, a bad behavior, drops from 15.4 at base to 30.1 under GRPO and back down to 9.8 under Harness-S. On SWE-Gym, switching to assertions after a blocked test goes from 15.4 percent at base to 48.5 under Harness-S. These are concrete, inspectable behaviors, and they are the evidence that the rescue training is moving the policy's own habits rather than just hiding behind the auxiliary harness at eval time. The paper's claim is that these behaviors transfer, and the held-out case analyses support it.

A skeptical read has a few things I would verify. The model is a 4-billion-parameter open model, so the transfer result is in the regime where the policy has headroom to learn new habits; I would want to see whether the same pipeline helps a much larger, better-calibrated policy, where the base is already strong and the rescue signal is rarer. The rescue mechanism only fires when all base attempts fail, so the training signal is concentrated on the hard tail, and the variance of those rescue-success rates across training stages suggests the signal is noisy. The adaptive router uses voting across multiple large models plus behavior profiles, which is a meaningful inference and compute dependency during training that the paper does not fully cost out. And the nonstationarity result is a real warning: the profile re-anchoring is doing real work, so a naive deployment that caches the router profiles once will degrade as the policy moves. The reuse-at-inference gain, reusing the router for rank-1 inference at the same rollout count, is a secondary benefit, not the main result, and I would not bank on it being large.

The router is the component I would spend the most time understanding before reproducing, because it is where the three observed properties are actually handled. The harness behavior profiles are summaries of what each sibling tends to do, and the router ranks the pool by those profiles plus voting across multiple large models. The top-k selection means you are not committing to a single best sibling, you are spreading a fixed rescue rollout budget across several candidates, and the paper shows that a broader top-8 with one attempt each outperforms a narrower selection with repeated attempts on the same siblings. That is a direct empirical consequence of the complementarity observation: the siblings fail on different tasks, so breadth of coverage beats depth on any one of them. The re-anchoring step is what addresses nonstationarity, refreshing the profiles as the policy's own behavior shifts, so the router is not ranking siblings on evidence gathered from an older version of the policy. Without it, the fixed-profile variant still works but lags the adaptive one across training stages, which is the paper's own evidence that the nonstationarity is real and not an artifact.

The engineering gotcha is the pool's fixedness versus the policy's motion. The sibling pool is deliberately frozen during policy training, which is what makes it reusable and cheap, but it also means the pool is a snapshot of repair directions from an earlier policy. As the policy improves, some siblings' repair directions stop matching the current failure modes, and that is exactly what the re-anchoring step is patching over. If you reproduce this, the re-anchoring cadence is a knob you will be tuning, and getting it wrong in either direction either wastes rescue rollouts or lets the router route on stale evidence.

Bottom line: Harness-S is a clean reframing of harness optimization, trading a single evolved harness for a fixed pool of siblings used only to rescue base-harness failures, with the real deliverable being a policy that carries the learned behaviors back to the original harness. The transfer evidence is the interesting part, but it is demonstrated on a small open model with a noisy, re-anchoring-dependent training signal, so validate the behavior transfer on a frontier-scale policy before you treat it as a general recipe.
