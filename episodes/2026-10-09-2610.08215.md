"Learn2Play Bench: how well do LLM agents learn from experience in unfamiliar environments?" by Yibo Li, Jinhang Qiu, and Zhi Zheng, from the National University of Singapore. This is a benchmark, and it is one of the better-motivated benchmarks I have seen in a while, because it attacks a real confound that a lot of agent-evaluation work papers over. When you tell an agent to improve over repeated attempts on a familiar task, you cannot tell whether it actually learned something new or whether it just recovered knowledge it already had from pretraining. This paper removes that ambiguity by design.

The setup is a set of newly designed text-based games with hidden rules that are deliberately novel or counterintuitive. The rules are not given to the agent. It has to discover them by acting, reading the textual feedback, and carrying useful experience into later episodes, without any weight updates. The paper gives a concrete example that makes the point immediately: in one of the games, stealing a colleague's lunch helps the player get promoted, while working hard does not. An agent that has internalized what real workplaces are like will be actively misled by its priors, so it genuinely has to learn the rule from the feedback rather than from anything in its pretraining. The games have deterministic outcomes and automatic scoring, which is what makes the learning curves reproducible and comparable.

There are twenty game templates, each of which can generate multiple instances that differ in layout, characters, or hidden-rule parameters. The benchmark splits along two dimensions. Ten games are fixed, repeating the same instance across episodes. Ten games are re-shuffled, where the visible situation changes between episodes but the underlying rule stays the same. That re-shuffled half is the transfer test, because it asks whether an agent can apply what it learned when the surface details change. They also identified five challenge games, CATNIP, DREAMGARDEN, MOLA TEA, REDEYE, and ROADSIDE OBSERVATORY, through pilot evaluations, and those get a ten-episode scoring window while the remaining normal games get five.

The evaluation protocol is a black-box command interface. The agent issues actions and observes textual feedback, but it cannot read the game source or call any oracle solver. Each game is run for a fixed number of episodes, and there are three independent trials per game, each starting with no memory of the previous trials. Within a trial, what carries across episodes depends entirely on the harness, which is exactly the variable they want to study. They score with four metrics on normalized scores. Max and Mean measure the achieved performance, the peak and the average across episodes. Learning Gain compares the average of the first and last few episodes, the net improvement. And Learning Slope fits a line to the normalized score over all episodes and reports its slope, the overall rate of change. Challenge games are weighted three times normal games so that the two groups each contribute half of the aggregate score.

They compare eleven backbone models under a common coding-agent harness called OpenCode, which gives shell and file tools. The models range from Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT 6 Astra at the top down through Claude Opus 5, Qwen 3.8 Max, the Gemini 3.5 and 3.7 Flash models, GPT 5.6 Sol, GPT 5 Mini, DeepSeek V4 Pro, and Kimi K3. For the self-evolving method comparison they hold the backbone fixed and vary only the memory mechanism, testing naive, which retains nothing across episodes; memory, which cumulatively appends the complete unmodified interaction history; Reflexion, which reflects each episode into concise lessons; EvoTest, which evolves a guiding prompt and a state-extraction program; ReasoningBank, which distills episodes into reusable reasoning lessons; and Agent Workflow Memory, which induces multi-step workflows from successful histories.

Now the findings, because these are the part I would actually take away. Finding one, and this is the surprise: retaining the complete raw interaction history beats summarizing it. The plain memory method, which just keeps the unmodified logs, leads all four metrics on the Kimi K3 backbone, reaching a Max of 63.3, a Mean of 48.0, a Learning Gain of 16.9, and a Learning Slope of 4.69. It beats Reflexion at 61.6 Max, ReasoningBank at 59.9, Agent Workflow Memory at 56.1, and the no-experience naive baseline at 54.7. The reasoning is not obvious, but it makes sense. Reflection and rule extraction can turn an incomplete understanding into a rule that later episodes then keep following, and if that early rule was wrong you are stuck with it. The raw history keeps the original evidence available, so an agent can revise an interpretation when a later observation contradicts it. The paper gives a specific case from a game called GEMFORGE where an EvoTest run, after one failed combination, writes a rule that tells it to avoid several other combinations, including one that would have succeeded. The compression threw away information that the agent needed.

Finding two is the human comparison, and it is humbling. The top-performing human players beat the best agent. The human top player reaches a Max of 84.3, a Learning Gain of 21.6, and a Learning Slope of 7.33. The best agent, Claude Opus 5.5, reaches 80.1 Max with a Learning Slope of 5.22. Humans explore more varied action sequences and repeat actions less, and they recover more often after setbacks. That is a concrete, quantified human-agent gap on a task designed to measure exactly the learning ability we claim to be building toward.

Finding three is the harness effect, and it is the most practically useful. With the backbone held fixed, changing the harness can substantially improve both performance and learning while reducing estimated inference cost, through fewer model calls and less repeated context. The same point shows up in the method comparison: the flexible OpenCode harness, where the model decides when to review earlier feedback, run calculations, or check a plan, beats the more rigid self-evolving methods that follow preset steps. Finding four is that agents learn more slowly in the re-shuffled, changing environments even when the rule is the same, and the drop in learning slope is larger for weaker backbones under the same harness. Finding five is a specific weakness: agents can learn the rule but still fail to turn it into a strategy. In a game called HAUNTED INN, an agent learns how to assign guests to safe rooms but does not plan ahead, so a later guest ends up with nowhere safe to stay.

Let me be direct about what I would trust and what I would re-verify. What I would trust is the benchmark's core design, because the novelty-and-counterintuitiveness constraint genuinely closes the pretraining-knowledge loophole, the deterministic scoring makes the numbers comparable, and the fixed-versus-reshuffled split is a clean way to separate learning from transfer. The finding that raw memory beats summarization is specific, well-evidenced, and I would expect it to hold in a lot of settings, because it is a direct consequence of lossy compression discarding revisable evidence. What I would re-verify before I leaned on the absolute numbers is the scale and the harness. Twenty templates is a small surface, and a lot rides on the quality of the manual rule design; a handful of poorly specified games could distort the aggregate. The backbone comparison is all done under one harness, OpenCode, so the cross-model ranking is entangled with that harness's behavior. And the human study is small, so the top-k human groups are a rough ceiling, not a precise one. The engineering gotcha if you adopt this for your own evaluation is the cost and the window: three trials per game, up to ten episodes each, across twenty games and eleven backbones is a large number of agent runs, and the normalized-score aggregation means you have to trust their normalization and weighting if you want to compare your own model fairly.

Here is the bottom line. Learn2Play Bench gives you a controlled, reproducible way to measure whether an agent actually learns from experience rather than recalling what it already knows, and its most useful result is that keeping raw interaction history often beats the fancier reflection and rule-extraction methods people reach for. If you are building or evaluating self-improving agents, this is a credible testbed to run them on, and the human-agent gap it quantifies is a useful reminder of how far test-time learning still has to go.
