The paper is Cache Engineering for Agents, from Tianyu Fu, Ruoxi Wang, and Zihan Min, all with equal contribution, at Tsinghua University, with Yu Wang as the corresponding author and one collaborator at the Chinese University of Hong Kong. The problem is the key-value cache in long-running LLM agents. As an agent's trajectory grows over hundreds of tool-calling rounds, the KV cache becomes the dominant cost: the paper reports that KV cache reads alone account for over eighty-two percent of total API cost in their measurement. And there is an accuracy side to the problem too, because letting the attended context or its positional range grow past what the model handles reliably degrades task performance. The existing fix is context engineering, trimming or summarizing old messages, but that has a nasty property: editing the text prefix invalidates every cached state after the edit point, so you pay for a full re-prefill of everything you kept. Harnesses therefore wait until a context threshold trips, then throw away most of the history at once, which is the moment they lose the useful middle of the trajectory.

Their proposal is to stop editing the text and start editing the cache directly. The agent keeps the full message history as-is, but gains two new operations on the KV state itself. Drop removes selected cache entries from future attention, so the text stays in the conversation but the expensive states are excluded from computation. Repos adjusts the positional encodings of the retained keys, which matters because after you drop a span of the middle, the kept suffix's position indices are no longer contiguous, and models are sensitive to that. Together with the ordinary append operation, the model and harness interleave these operations throughout execution to shape the active cache. The paper formalizes the operation set and then identifies a serving-system consequence they call history dependence: with standard prefix caching, the token prefix uniquely determines the cache state, which is what makes prefix trees and shared KV reuse work. With drop and repos, two trajectories can have identical token prefixes and identical retained positions but different cache states, because the operations they applied along the way differ. So prefix matching, the foundation of every major inference server's cache reuse, is no longer sufficient for correctness. Concretely, the paper shows two trajectories that keep the same retained keys and positions but differ in what was dropped in between, producing different retained states, and a server that keys its cache on the token prefix alone would serve the wrong state to one of them. The fix in AKV is to key cache entries on the operation history, not just the tokens, which is a small change to the cache key and a large change to what the key is allowed to assume. The engine's interface is deliberately stateless from the client's point of view, so existing serving APIs keep working, but internally it tracks which states are live, which have been evicted to host memory or storage, and how to restore them when a trajectory returns to a branch, which is the piece that makes the drop-and-continue pattern actually usable instead of a research curiosity.

To make that work in practice they build AKV, a serving engine that presents a stateless interface compatible with existing serving APIs while internally tracking stateful cache reuse, eviction, and restoration. The evaluation has three parts. First, a tool-response rolling-drop policy, meaning you preferentially drop the KV states for tool responses rather than reasoning text, improves average benchmark scores by one point seven over threshold-triggered summarization while cutting average cost by twenty-seven point seven percent. Second, with AKV in the loop, the same policy reaches up to one point two times the output throughput and a one point five times speedup in p-ninety-five latency. Third, they run recursive self-improvement on the cache policy itself, letting an agent search over drop and repos schedules; the machine-found policy beats their expert-designed one, with a further roughly seven percent cost reduction on top, and on a one hundred task subset the self-improved policy reaches the highest pass rate for both models tested. They also ablate which cache to drop: dropping reasoning cache does worse than dropping tool-response cache, and repos specifically helps one benchmark by letting exploration continue past the positional limit that would otherwise halt execution. One subtlety worth internalizing: dropped KV does not erase influence the way text deletion would. Because retained states were computed with attention over the dropped region, the kept cache still carries a compressed shadow of what was dropped, which is exactly the failure mode the paper's nested-block illustration points at, and the reason their ablations matter.

The serving-side numbers deserve their own accounting, because they are where the practical case lives. At their respective peak-throughput settings, AKV runs at a higher concurrency than the standard baseline, and at that operating point it achieves a one point three six times speedup in mean request latency and a one point five one times speedup at p-ninety-five, with input and output throughput at one point five three and one point one eight times the standard engine. The mechanism is not mysterious: when the standard engine re-prefills after a threshold-triggered summary, those re-prefill requests occupy GPU time that could otherwise be decoding, so fewer concurrent requests fit in the same window, and tail latency climbs. AKV avoids the re-prefill entirely, so the same hardware decodes more requests in parallel. There is also an end-to-end comparison on a one hundred task subset of a software engineering benchmark, run at each method's peak concurrency, where AKV reaches the highest final pass rate in the least total GPU time, which is the single most decision-relevant number in the paper, because it couples accuracy and wall-clock cost in one measurement. The reason the rolling-drop policy beats a one-shot summary on accuracy is visible in their ablations: abrupt context reductions cause information loss that shows up as dropped pass rates, whereas removing older tool-response cache incrementally keeps the model's working context continuous, so the agent never experiences a hard cut in its own history.

The skeptical part. The one point seven score gain is small, and it is the number that justifies the whole serving-engine rewrite, so look hard at its variance across benchmarks before you believe it is systematic; the cost reduction is the more credible claim, because dropping tool responses is close to free once the states are materialized. The history-dependence problem is real and under-appreciated in the serving literature, but it also means every existing prefix-caching optimization has to be re-derived for this scheme, and AKV is a research engine, not a production one: the stateless-front, stateful-back design has to survive the moment your cluster scales, fails over, or shares KV across replicas, none of which the paper exercises. The recursive self-improvement result, where the machine-beats-expert policy saves another seven percent, is the most interesting claim and the least controlled, because the search space for cache schedules is small enough that a decent grid search might close most of the gap, and the paper does not isolate how much of that gain is generalizable versus overfit to the two benchmarks used. And the whole premise rests on the model tolerating non-contiguous positional ranges after repos, which is an empirical property of the specific models tested, not a guarantee for the next one.

The verdict: this is a genuinely new operational primitive for agent inference, and the drop-plus-repos formalization plus the history-dependence analysis are the durable contributions even if AKV never ships. If you run long-horizon agents today, the tool-response rolling-drop policy is implementable on top of current stack with modest pain and the cost savings are the part to believe; if you are building serving systems, the history-dependence result should change how you think about prefix caching assumptions.
