SimpleOPD: Simple Tokenizer-Agnostic On-Policy Distillation for Long-Context Reasoning, by Haonan He, Haodi Lei, and Yun Luo, with the SU-01 Team at Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. The problem is specific and practical. On-policy distillation is now a standard post-training tool: the student generates its own trajectories, and a teacher provides dense token-level supervision on exactly the states the student visits. But nearly all published work assumes teacher and student share a vocabulary. Try distilling from a long-context reasoning teacher that writes hundred-thousand-token proofs into a short-context student from a different model family, and three things break: the tokenizers do not align, the teacher prefers responses longer than the student's context budget, and training destabilizes into length explosion, truncation, and repetition loops. This paper reports what actually breaks when you attempt this transfer and two small fixes that make it work. The results are strong enough to matter; the mechanism is simple enough to ship.

The cross-tokenizer alignment first, because it is the load-bearing idea. Rather than mapping one vocabulary onto another, SimpleOPD performs distillation in shared text space. The student generates a response under its own tokenizer and chat template. That response string is appended to the context reconstructed under the teacher's own chat template, and the teacher tokenizes it natively. Then a linear two-pointer scan walks both tokenizations of the identical response string: whenever the accumulated text prefixes match and the current tokens contribute the exact same text span, the pair is aligned and inherits supervision; partially overlapping spans are skipped entirely, because you cannot uniquely assign one teacher log-probability across multiple student tokens or merge several teacher tokens into one student position. Unmatched student positions fall back to the student's own log-probability, so the objective degrades gracefully to plain policy gradient where alignment fails. When tokenizers are identical every position aligns and the objective reduces exactly to reverse-KL distillation. The reported lexical overlap ratio, the fraction of student tokens receiving teacher supervision, starts high and rises further during training, sitting in the zero point nine zero to zero point nine eight band for Qwen3.5, Intern-S2, and GLM students alike. Partial alignment loses little signal in practice.

Now the instability findings, which are the part most papers would have hidden in an appendix. Naive on-policy distillation from SU-01, a thirty-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts reasoning model with olympiad gold-medal-level proof capability that sustains over one hundred thousand tokens of chain of thought, into Intern-S2-Preview produced modest score gains accompanied by visible degeneration: truncation rate climbing toward twenty percent, repetition rising, average response length growing continuously. The failure mode has a clean diagnosis. Teacher supervision keeps rewarding longer thinking, so the student gradually stops emitting termination tokens like the end-of-thinking marker and end-of-sequence token, responses run past the context limit, and trajectories arrive truncated. A case study documents one model producing nine hundred seventy-two exact self-check repeats after reaching a correct answer on AIME25, and another collapsing into a single-token loop.

Two mitigations follow. First, special-token masking: exclude structural termination tokens, the close-think and end-of-text markers, from the distillation advantage, since format control need not match the teacher's distribution. This helps but does not fix it alone; truncation kept climbing late in training. Second, a student-reference KL loss with coefficient zero point five that anchors the student to its own initial policy. Combined, truncation drops to nearly zero while performance climbs. For Intern-S2-Preview, ProofBench@4 goes from twenty-one point seven zero to thirty-eight point five zero with reference KL alone and AnswerBench@8 rises to seventy-nine point one zero, AIME25@8 to ninety-five point eight zero. Larger teacher-student gaps needed stronger regularization: GLM-4.7-Flash and Gemma-4 used coefficient one point zero, and an ablation over coefficients zero point five, one point zero, and one point two found one point zero best overall, with weak regularization under-constraining and strong regularization blocking absorption of teacher behavior.

Training setup, briefly: one hundred rollout iterations, constant learning rate 0.000001, rollout batch sixty-four, four samples per prompt, PPO clipping at zero point two, four policy updates per rollout, distillation length thirty-two thousand tokens for Qwen-family students and six thousand for GLM and Gemma, using the Slime framework with SGLang evaluation rollouts. Training data is roughly forty-five hundred mathematical proof problems: sixty-three from the Open Proof Corpus, two thousand nine hundred forty-eight scraped from Art of Problem Solving, nine hundred from online competition books, and six hundred seventeen from a Chinese olympiad forum plus Evan Chen's materials. Evaluation uses ProofBench and AnswerBench from IMO-Bench plus AIME25 and AMOBench, decoding at temperature one point zero and top-p zero point nine five with responses allowed up to one hundred sixty thousand tokens, DeepSeek-V4-Flash judging ProofBench averaged over four runs, rule-based verification first on the verifiable sets with a fallback model judge, and checkpoint selection by the average of AIME@4 and AnswerBench@1.

Results. Same-tokenizer students improve substantially: Qwen3-4B gains twelve point three zero points on ProofBench, seventeen on AnswerBench, and nineteen point five eight on AIME25. Scaled to Qwen3-30B-A3B, ProofBench jumps twenty-two point six seven points, from thirteen point eight zero to thirty-six point four seven. Cross-tokenizer works too: Qwen3.5-35B-A3B reaches forty-two point three nine on ProofBench, up fifteen point six one. The flagship number is Intern-S2-Preview: twenty-one point seven zero to forty-four point five zero under the cost-efficient judge, essentially matching the teacher's forty-five point zero zero, and from thirty-four point zero to fifty-five point two under Gemini-2.5-Pro judging in the original SU-01 setting, surpassing Gemini-2.5-Pro and GPT-5 while approaching DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale. Cross-family transfer holds but attenuates with tokenizer distance: GLM-4.7-Flash improves on both benchmarks, thirty point eight to thirty-nine point seven on ProofBench, while SentencePiece-based Gemma-4 gains on ProofBench, twenty-five point five to thirty-four point two, and slips slightly on AnswerBench. Against competing OPD variants, EOPD and G-OPD, SimpleOPD wins three of four benchmarks on Intern-S2, with the proof benchmark gap widest.

Three more analyses deserve note. Distilling from a different and larger teacher, DeepSeek-V4-Flash at one hundred fifty-eight billion parameters into the thirty-billion student at just six thousand token length, still yields eighteen point zero one points on ProofBench to thirty-nine point seven one, plus AIME25 gains to ninety-seven point five zero, suggesting teacher strength substitutes partly for distillation length. Length matters: raising distillation length from six thousand to thirty-two thousand tokens improved every benchmark for stable configurations, lifting ProofBench from forty point zero seven to forty-two point three nine for the Qwen3.5 student and from thirty-eight point eight zero to forty-four point five zero for Intern-S2, evidence that long-form proof behavior needs long sequences. Data composition matters too: adding the teacher's verifiable math data to proof data dropped ProofBench from forty-four point five zero to thirty-eight point five zero while buying only one point on AnswerBench, so keep the mixture pure when proof transfer is the goal. Out-of-domain generalization appears despite math-only training: HiPhO physics improves from thirty-eight point six to forty-one point one, beating the teacher's thirty-five, HLE ticks up, and FrontierScience Research more than doubles from one point seven to five point zero, though from a low base. The KL-coefficient sweep on GLM quantifies the regularization trade-off precisely: ProofBench goes thirty-seven point one five at coefficient zero point five, thirty-nine point seven one at one point zero, thirty-seven point nine six at one point two, while AIME25 peaks at ninety-four point one seven away from the middle setting.

Skeptical notes. ProofBench judging is LLM-based and non-verifiable, so those headline numbers inherit judge variance despite the four-run averaging; treat judge-relative comparisons as softer than the AIME25 numbers, which are rule-verified. The student-reference KL coefficient was tuned per family, so expect hyperparameter search per student. Gemma's partial regression shows tokenizer distance still costs something even after alignment. And the termination-token masking is task-specific plumbing tied to particular chat markers; porting requires care. Bottom line: this is the clearest public demonstration that long-context reasoning transfers across tokenizer boundaries with dense on-policy supervision, provided you protect the student from inheriting the teacher's length habits. Implementers should start from the alignment scanner plus masked termination tokens and reference KL, then verify stability by watching truncation rate, not just benchmark scores.
