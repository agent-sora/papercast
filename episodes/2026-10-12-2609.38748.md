Hear the World in Stereo: Learning Dynamic Spatial Correspondence for Immersive Joint Video-Audio Generation, from Hanmo Chen, Chengcheng Liu, and Tianxiao Chen at Xidian University and the vivo BlueImage Lab. Recent joint video-audio generation models have gotten strong at semantic correspondence and temporal synchronization, the audio matches what is on screen and it is in the right beat. But that is not enough for the applications that actually want this, which are augmented and virtual reality and interactive gaming. Those need stereo audio, a genuine sense of three-dimensional space, and stereo audio has a specific requirement that the current models do not address. The perceived location of a sound has to evolve consistently with the motion of the visual source that produces it. If a car drives across the screen, the sound should sweep across the listener's stereo field with it. The authors call this property dynamic spatial correspondence, and it is largely underexplored in existing methods. Their contribution is a framework, StereoBind, that explicitly binds visual source motion to stereo sound generation, plus the first dataset and benchmark built for this task.
The mechanism is grounded in how humans actually localize sound. Horizontal sound localization relies on interaural time differences and interaural level differences, the disparities in arrival time and intensity between the two ears, and as a source moves, those binaural cues should evolve with it. So the framework starts from a visual motion track, a frame-wise two-dimensional trajectory of where the sound source is on screen, and uses it to condition stereo generation. StereoBind builds on the LTX-2.3 backbone, which has separate video and audio branches, and it uses three complementary components: Visual Motion Binding transfers source-aware motion context from the video branch into the audio stream, the Spatial Track Encoder captures the absolute source position, and a Residual Track RoPE models the relative motion over time. The implementation details are the kind of thing that would stop a reimplementation from being a black box. A frozen motion-track IC-LoRA goes into the video branch, and trainable LoRA, rank thirty-two, goes into the attention and feed-forward of the audio branch. The motion track sequence is projected and passed through a temporal Transformer encoder followed by a layer norm to get frame-level motion context, then resampled to match the audio branch's coarser temporal resolution. In training each sample is one hundred twenty-one frames at a resolution of six hundred forty by three hundred eighty four, and the dataset construction uses Qwen-3.5-35B-A3B in the data-synthesis pipeline and Qwen3-Omni-30B in the spatialization pipeline.
The data side is a four-stage spatialization pipeline that converts an existing audio-video clip into a spatially supervised stereo example: it identifies the sound source, grounds candidate positions, verifies the audio-visual alignment, and derives a smoothed motion track, applying Gaussian filtering to the horizontal coordinate and a cubic polynomial fit to the vertical one before using it as spatial supervision. From this they build StereoWorld-29K, a training set of about twenty-nine thousand spatialized examples, and StereoWorldBench, an evaluation suite with metrics for stereo spatial fidelity, including an interaural level difference Wasserstein distance, a sound event localization accuracy, a stereo magnitude response error, and two auralization metrics for angle and calibration.
The results are measured against both joint video-audio generation baselines and video-to-spatial-audio models. The comparison set includes Ovi, MiniMax-H3, LTX-2.5, and LTX-2.3 on the joint generation side, and PrismAudio and See2Sound on the spatialization side. On their own StereoWorldBench, StereoBind takes the best result on the stereo spatial fidelity metrics, with an interaural level difference Wasserstein distance of two point seven five four, a sound event localization accuracy of zero point seven five seven, a stereo magnitude response error of fourteen point eight six, and auralization angle and calibration scores of zero point four six five and zero point three one four. For reference, the closest joint generation baseline, Ovi, sits at a Wasserstein distance of three point seven four four, a localization accuracy of zero point five seven one, and a magnitude response error of sixty five point five eight, so the gap on the spatial metrics is large, and it comes without sacrificing generation quality: StereoBind also reports the best motion smoothness and imaging quality in the comparison. The ablations confirm which part of the design does the work. Removing the residual track position encoding, or removing the spatial track conditioning entirely, degrades the spatial fidelity metrics, with the no-spatial-track variant showing the largest drops, which is the expected result but the one worth stating: the motion track is what carries the spatial signal, and the position encoding is what keeps it temporally aligned. The visual and audio quality metrics, subject consistency, motion smoothness, imaging quality, production quality, and audio-visual sync from standard suites, are used to verify that the spatial conditioning does not come at the cost of the underlying generation. There is a design detail that explains why the spatial conditioning does not degrade the underlying generation, and it is worth stating. The spatial track signal is injected as an additional cross-attention path into the audio branch, alongside the existing video-to-audio attention, rather than replacing it. That means the audio branch still gets its semantic content, the what of the sound, from the video context exactly as before, and the motion track adds only the where, the position in the stereo field. The two signals are separable in the architecture, which is why the visual and audio quality metrics, subject consistency, motion smoothness, imaging quality, and the audio-visual synchronization scores, stay at the top of the table even while the stereo spatial metrics improve. The auralization-based metrics in the benchmark, the angle and calibration scores, are computed by re-deriving a spatialized signal and comparing it to the reference, so they measure whether the perceived direction of the sound matches the tracked source position, which is a closer proxy to the listening experience than a raw interaural distance metric. The dataset scale, roughly twenty-nine thousand spatialized examples, is large enough that the model is not overfitting to a handful of spatialization styles, which matters because stereo cues are subtle and easy to get subtly wrong across a range of source motions and scene layouts. The choice to build the dataset from real video, rather than synthetic scenes, also matters for the benchmark's realism: the source motions in real footage include the kinds of abrupt direction changes, occlusions, and depth motion that a purely synthetic spatializer would never have to handle, so the model is trained and tested on a harder distribution of spatial behavior than the toy setting would suggest.
The skeptical read. First, the benchmark is the authors' own, built by their own spatialization pipeline, so the reference stereo audio that the metrics compare against is produced by the same method family as the model being evaluated, and a model that imitates the pipeline's spatialization style would score well even if the perceptual spatialization were off. The auralization and interaural metrics are good proxies, but they are not a listening test. Second, the motion track is an external supervisory signal, derived from a tracking and smoothing pipeline, so the system inherits the errors of that tracker, and a wrong track gives the model the right machinery pointed at the wrong place. Third, the claim of being the first track-conditioned stereo video-audio generation framework is strong, but the comparison set on the spatialization side is only two models, so the depth of the prior-art gap is hard to fully assess. Bottom line: StereoBind is a well-grounded first step on a real gap, the explicit binding of visual source motion to stereo generation, and the dataset and benchmark are the part that will outlive any single model. I would want an independent spatialization reference and a human listening study before treating the stereo fidelity numbers as more than a strong self-referential win.
