HypoEvolve: Genetic Algorithms Enable Multi-Agent LLMs to Discover Scientific Hypotheses. The first three authors are Jieyuan Liu, Mengzhou Hu, and Jefferson Chen, from the University of California San Diego, with the remaining authors spanning UC San Diego, Texas A and M University, Carnegie Mellon University, and the Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence.

The paper addresses a question that has become urgent as scientific agents get better: how does the design of collaboration between agents affect the quality of the hypotheses they develop? Recent systems combine scientific agents with evolutionary search, cycling through critique, comparison, and revision. But when such a system performs well, it is impossible to tell how much of the performance comes from the agents' scientific capabilities and how much comes from the rules that direct their collaboration. The paper's contribution is a framework that separates those two things. It makes collaboration an experimental variable: the scientific roles, the prompts, and the evaluation criteria are held fixed, and only the search rules, the rules for combining, revising, and retaining hypotheses, are varied. Hypothesis quality is the measured outcome.

The framework is called HypoEvolve, and it is a generational genetic algorithm in which specialized large language model agents provide both the scientific variation and the comparative fitness. The population is a set of hypotheses, and each generation proceeds through a fixed sequence. A generation agent produces offspring from the current population. Crossover operates at a rate of 0.6, and it is formulated as reasoning over claims and rationales: agents can combine mechanistic arguments across two parent hypotheses, which is a semantic operation, not a string splice. Mutation operates at a rate of 0.15, and it is formulated as reconsidering the assumptions behind an explanation, including drug substitution and out-of-the-box reinterpretation. Parents and offspring are then evaluated together, using pairwise judgments of evidence and testability, scored with a Bradley-Terry model. Selection is by tournament with k equals 2. The best mu hypotheses are retained, so new proposals compete directly against the ideas they build on, and the population size is fixed across generations. Every hypothesis records its parentage and the operators that produced it, which makes each hypothesis's development inspectable across generations.

The evaluation is through a drug repurposing task, chosen because prospective experiments are too costly to run as a live evaluation. Each proposed hypothesis is a candidate drug for a cancer type with a mechanistic rationale, and the rationale implies a concrete biological claim: that the drug's targets are relevant to the specified cancer. Across 34 cancer types, the claim is assessed with two external measures, DepMap selectivity and Open Targets association, and both measures are reserved for use after the search, so the search cannot see the evaluation signal. Under a shared task and retrieval protocol, HypoEvolve achieves the highest mean scores among six baselines on both measures: DepMap selectivity of 0.171 and Open Targets association of 0.426, compared with 0.115 and 0.329 for Tree of Thoughts, the strongest baseline. The advantage over single-pass generation generalizes to held-out cancer types, and with the scientific operations and the hypothesis count held fixed, fitness-guided parent selection improves both the mean and the minimum score of the population.

The design choice worth examining is why a genetic algorithm rather than a simpler search. A single-pass generation, the obvious baseline, has no mechanism to combine the partial insights of different hypotheses, and it discards the population after one round. Tree of Thoughts gives you branching and backtracking, but the branching is over reasoning steps, not over hypotheses, and it has no explicit retention rule that forces new proposals to compete with the old ones. The genetic formulation contributes three specific things: the population, which keeps diverse candidate hypotheses alive across generations; the semantic crossover and mutation, which give the search a way to produce genuinely new hypotheses rather than resampled variants; and the fixed-size retention rule, which connects the comparative judgment directly to the direction of subsequent search, because which hypotheses survive determines what the next generation is built from. The retention rule is the piece that is easiest to overlook and hardest to replicate, because it is what converts the pairwise judgments into search pressure. A system that evaluates hypotheses but keeps them all, or keeps them by recency rather than by comparative fitness, is doing ranking, not search, and the paper's controlled result, that fitness-guided selection improves both the mean and the minimum score with everything else held fixed, is what isolates that specific mechanism as the source of the gain.

What would I trust? The experimental design, which is the paper's real contribution. Holding the scientific roles, prompts, and evaluation fixed while varying only the collaboration rules is exactly the controlled comparison the field needs, and it is what makes the result, that fitness-guided selection improves hypothesis quality, actually attributable to the collaboration design rather than to better prompting or a stronger base model. The evaluation protocol is also sound: using external, reserved measures, DepMap selectivity and Open Targets association, that the search cannot see, is the right way to prevent the system from overfitting to its own evaluation, and the 34-cancer-type drug repurposing task gives the hypotheses a concrete, checkable biological claim instead of a vague plausibility score. The parentage tracking is a small but valuable addition, because it makes the search process auditable, and an auditable search is what you want when the output is a scientific claim you might actually pursue.

What would I re-verify before building on it? First, the absolute scores. DepMap selectivity of 0.171 and Open Targets association of 0.426 are the winning numbers, but they are on a scale where the strongest baseline gets 0.115 and 0.329, so the entire field, including HypoEvolve, is producing hypotheses with a modest external-evidence rate. I would want to understand the base rate, what score a random drug-cancer pair gets on these two measures, because a 0.171 selectivity that is only slightly above the base rate is a very different object from one that is many times the base rate, and the paper's internal comparison, against Tree of Thoughts and the other baselines, does not answer that question. Second, the transfer to held-out cancer types is the claim that matters most for real use, and I would want to know how many held-out types there are and the variance of the advantage across them, because an average advantage over a small number of types is fragile. Third, the LLM agents doing the crossover and mutation are the scientific engine, and the paper holds their roles fixed, but I would want to see how sensitive the results are to which base models fill those roles, because if the genetic scaffold only helps with strong base models, the collaboration-design result is conditional in a way that matters for deployment.

One more point on the framework's generality. The paper positions HypoEvolve as a foundation for building autonomous research teams, and the value of that is that the collaboration rules are now a separable, testable component. You can run the same scientific agents under different retention rules and measure the difference, which is something the existing multi-agent systems, where the search logic and the scientific roles are entangled, do not easily allow. That separability is what the framework buys you, independent of whether drug repurposing is the right test domain.

Bottom line: HypoEvolve is a controlled framework for studying how agent collaboration affects hypothesis quality, and its headline result is that fitness-guided parent selection in a generational genetic search beats six baselines, including Tree of Thoughts, on both external evidence measures across 34 cancer types. The design is the contribution, and it is clean; the open question is whether the winning absolute scores are meaningfully above the base rate, because that is what determines whether the discovered hypotheses are actionable rather than merely better than the alternatives.
