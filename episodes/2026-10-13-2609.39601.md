The paper is "GroundingPI: A Grounding Foundation Model towards Physical Intelligence with Visual Primitives," by Qize Yu, Lianrui Fan, and Boyu Chen, from XPeng Inc., Peking University, and the University of Hong Kong, with collaborators across Berkeley, Princeton, NUS, Tsinghua, and HKUST. This is a new 4-billion-parameter foundation model, and it is the second-highest upvoted paper of the day at 54. The thesis is blunt: general-purpose vision-language backbones are not built for the kind of precise, cluttered, tiny-object grounding that closed-loop robotics and driving need, so build a perception-native foundation model instead of bolting perception onto a general VLM.

The architecture is deliberately unglamorous, which is part of the point. GroundingPI stacks a MoonViT-V2 visual encoder, the one from Kimi K3, through a projector with 2-by-2 spatial merging and a two-layer MLP, into a Qwen3-4B autoregressive decoder. The output interface is a shared vocabulary of text, structural markers, and 1,000 quantized coordinate tokens, so points, boxes, referring expressions, OCR, GUI elements, layout, and visual prompts are all just structured token sequences. When a queried category is absent, the model emits a None payload instead of hallucinating a box. That unification is what lets 34 benchmarks spanning 11 perceptual capabilities share one model and one training pipeline.

Training is staged, and the appendix gives the full recipe, which is worth the standing-recipe attention because every stage is specified. Pretrain 1 has three stages: a vision-language projector alignment stage that freezes the visual encoder and language model and updates only the projector on image captions at 8,192-token context; a joint multimodal pretraining stage that unfreezes everything, with the language model at a peak learning rate of ten to the minus five and the visual encoder and projector at ten to the minus six; and a general visual and video understanding stage that extends the context to 32,768 tokens. Pretrain 2, the coordinate-alignment supervised fine-tune, then updates all modules on eight spatial task groups — detection, GUI grounding, referring-expression grounding, referring-expression pointing, OCR, document layout, dense pointing and counting, and visual prompting — at 8,192 tokens, with quantized coordinate tokens supervised by the same cross-entropy objective as everything else and no separate IoU regression loss. The aggregate supervised exposure across all four stages is 721 billion tokens, of which 500 billion is Pretrain 1 and 221 billion is Pretrain 2. GRPO reinforcement post-training then follows the supervised checkpoint, with custom rewards for grounding quality and OCR. Supervision comes from public datasets plus what they call dedicated data engines: an iterative annotation loop where a teacher ensemble generates candidates, fields are fused, tasks are validated, and labels that fail coverage or consistency checks are revised or retired, with versioned supervision so bad iterations do not contaminate the training set. The six data families span detection, GUI, referring, OCR, layout, and pointing, and the leave-one-out analysis that follows treats them as the unit of analysis, which is the right choice for a paper making a perception-as-foundation claim.

The headline numbers: against 44 baselines across the 34 grounding benchmarks, GroundingPI averages 73.68 percent, which the paper reports as a new state of the art and above the larger GPT-6 Astra at 71.54 percent. The capabilities covered span 11 families: common and long-tailed object detection, dense and tiny-object detection, referring detection, object pointing, robot and spatial pointing, OCR, GUI grounding, layout grounding, and visual prompting. The paper is explicit that the comparison with GPT-6 Astra is a chosen probe: they use it to explore the potential and limits of improving basic perception, not as a like-for-like contest.

The downstream story is where the physical-intelligence framing earns its keep, and it is done carefully. On RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboCasa-GR1, the authors hold the action head, action data, and training budget fixed and swap only the visual backbone, so the comparison isolates perception from policy. GroundingPI as a frozen backbone beats every mainstream backbone they evaluate, and on RoboCasa-GR1 it trained on half the demonstrations outperforms the baselines trained on three quarters of them. On nuScenes, as the visual backbone for trajectory prediction, it reaches an average open-loop L2 error of 0.296 meters. The controlled-backbone design is the right way to test the claim that perception, not action data, is the bottleneck, and the fact that halving the demo count still wins is the single most transferable number in the paper.

The ablations are the more interesting part for anyone implementing this. A leave-one-out analysis over the six pretraining data families shows that dense grounding and OCR are the two components whose removal most hurts downstream manipulation and driving, with the driving error jumping from 0.296 to 0.391 when the dense-grounding family is dropped, so the perceptual pretraining is doing real work rather than just inflating the benchmark average. The scale analysis in the appendix complements this: sweeping the grounding pretraining volume from 88.4 to 221 billion tokens, the 34-benchmark grounding score rises steadily and robotic manipulation improves on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution splits, so scaling the perception pretrain buys action capability. The data-composition sweep decomposes the mixture into basic and dense grounding, referring, pointing, and auxiliary families, and the leave-one-out numbers show the dropped family degrades exactly the downstream task it was meant to serve, which is the signature of a genuinely multi-task representation rather than a benchmark-fitted one.

The systems details are worth a line each. Supervised training runs on 128 GPUs in BF16 with ZeRO-1, FlashAttention, and activation recomputation, and the language backbone is a 36-layer dense transformer with hidden width 2560 and a 262,144-token positional capacity — large context is part of the design, not an afterthought, because dense grounding and layout tasks routinely exceed a few thousand tokens when packed. The dynamic visual patch budget of at most 4096 patches per image is the mechanism that lets the same model handle a small object at high resolution and a full driving frame without a separate model per regime, and it is one of the few places in the paper where the 4B budget is visibly doing real architectural work rather than just shrinking the parameter count.

The skeptical read. First, the state-of-the-art claim is self-contained: 34 benchmarks, 11 capabilities, and a single average. Averages over 11 heterogeneous capabilities hide per-capability variation, and the paper's own qualitative appendix shows dense and tiny-object scenes remain the hard cases, so the 73.68 is carrying a lot of weight, and the per-capability tables are where the honest reading actually lives. Second, the GPT-6 Astra comparison is apples to oranges in the way these comparisons always are: a proprietary general-purpose model evaluated on a benchmark battery designed by a team optimizing their own model's strong points. Third, and this matters for the physical-intelligence framing, the driving result is open-loop L2 error, not closed-loop driving performance, and the manipulation results are simulation benchmarks with a fixed action DiT. The claim that "perception is the bottleneck for action" is plausible and the controlled-backbone design is the right way to test it, but the evidence here is simulation transfer, not robot-in-the-wild. And 4B parameters with a Qwen3-4B decoder means the semantic ceiling is that of a small model; the paper is honest that this is a System-1 perception layer meant to complement larger reasoning models, but anyone hoping it replaces the VLM in a VLA should read that sentence twice.

Bottom line: a clean, well-controlled demonstration that dedicated perceptual pretraining on quantized visual primitives pays off for downstream action transfer, with the kind of fixed-budget backbone comparisons the field needs more of, and with a training recipe specified in enough detail to attempt a real reproduction. The open questions are how much of the 73.68 survives outside their own benchmark suite, and whether the simulation transfer gap to real hardware holds when the action head and the demos are less carefully controlled.
