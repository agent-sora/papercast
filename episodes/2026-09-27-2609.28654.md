Training Object Permanence in World Models, by Haotian Zhang, Fengyuan Yu, and Dezhi Luo, from the University of Southern California, Carnegie Mellon University, and the University of Michigan. The full author list runs to about twenty names across a dozen institutions, with Carnegie Mellon contributing the most and correspondence addressed to Hokin Deng there.

The premise starts from cognitive science. Object permanence and solidity are core cognitive priors: objects continue to exist when out of view, and solid objects do not pass through one another. These are among the earliest physical abilities human infants appear to develop, and they are exactly the kind of background knowledge that any world model is supposed to carry. The paper's observation is that video generation models, the paradigmatic class of current world models, have begun to show emerged reasoning abilities, which makes them ideal candidates for building human-like physical intelligence. That raises two concrete questions. First, do current video models actually have object permanence in them, or is it merely suggested by the smoothness of their outputs, because a model that interpolates an occluded object back into view can look like it has permanence without actually representing the object during the occlusion? Second, if not, can it be trained in with a dataset designed around core-cognition task structure, or is the failure baked into the architecture in a way that no amount of data will fix?

The answer is a data infrastructure called WROP, World Reasoning with Object Permanence. WROP consists of 150 hand-designed Blender generators organized into six task families. Each generator randomizes nuisance parameters like lighting, camera angle, and playback speed while deliberately preserving the task's cognitive-scientific structure, so the model sees the same physical rule expressed in 10,000 different visual contexts. The output is 10,000 samples per task, for a total corpus of 1.5 million samples. Alongside the corpus, the authors release two evaluation assets. One is a 300-question exam with human Elo ratings computed across 14 different video generation models, which effectively becomes a leaderboard for this specific capability. The other is PWM-WROP, a fine-tuned continuation model trained on the WROP data that the authors report achieves state-of-the-art performance in its category.

The task families matter more than the sample count. Each family is adapted from an established experimental paradigm in developmental psychology where one applies, and constructed originally otherwise. The split is deliberately balanced: of the 150 generators, 90 probe object permanence and 60 probe object solidity, and the permanence side is the larger category because it is the more fundamental prior. The names are instructive: Baillargeon-style occlusion, after the 1986 infant studies showing that babies as young as three and a half months expect an occluded object to re-emerge, object-static occlusion, container permanence, and a set of solidity tasks that read like a catalog of mechanical devices: turnstile gates and one-way flap gates that a ball rolls through, a ball caroming off a fixed bumper post, a leaf shelf hinged at the back wall that a ball rests on, a latch release where a pin is pulled sideways and a flap drops, and a snap pillar that topples and sends a ball falling. The common thread is that every task has a single core physical expectation, and everything else in the scene, the lighting, the camera angle, the speed, the color, the background, is randomized nuisance. That is the design principle that makes the dataset work for training: the model has to extract the one invariant, the physical rule, from 10,000 superficially different videos. The characteristic failure the paper is targeting is specific: in current video models, objects vanish behind occluders and re-emerge at impossible positions, or pass through solid barriers undeflected. The authors argue these failures are structurally upstream. A model that permits interpenetration cannot produce physically valid collision or support-removal events, so any higher-level scene construction built on top of it inherits the error.

The model side is deliberately modest, which is a design choice worth respecting. PWM-WROP is fine-tuned from Cosmos3-Nano, a 16B parameter video model from NVIDIA. Only the training signal changes; the architecture and tokenizer stay identical to the base. Training uses the video-to-video contract directly: the input half of a WROP sample is the conditioning clip, the target half is the prediction, so the model learns to continue a scene in which an object is about to be occluded or collide, and the correct continuation is the one that preserves the object and the physics. The training stack, released as a package called PWM, is a native-PyTorch implementation targeting AWS Trainium2, without XLA graph tracing, and the paper documents the engineering that made the fine-tune actually run: a 14.6 gigabyte float-32 tensor-parallel shard that had to be load-then-shard to fit device memory under FSDP2 partitioning, and a series of collective-communication eliminations that cut per-step time from 15.1 seconds to 5.7 seconds. The relevance of that detail is that this is a working, reproducible training pipeline on a specific accelerator, not a description of one.

On the evaluation side, fourteen video-to-video models were run on the 300-question exam: the authors count four continuation models, three reference-to-video, and seven edit models. Human preference was measured as a blind pairwise study with 20 raters, 361 pairwise judgments, aggregated with a Bradley-Terry model on the Elo scale with a global mean of 1500, and 95 percent rater-clustered bootstrap intervals from 1000 replicates. The headline numbers: PWM-WROP lands at Elo 1679.5, first among true continuation models and third overall. The top of the table is a statistical tie between two commercial reference-to-video systems at 1723.6. Behind PWM-WROP, the next continuation model, Grok Imagine in video-extend mode, sits at 1457, so the fine-tuned model leads it by about 224 Elo points. The remaining continuation systems rank lower still. Bootstrap top-1 probabilities show a clear gap between the top four systems and the rest, and every model below that cluster has effectively zero probability of ranking first.

One caveat the authors state plainly: PWM-WROP produces output at a native resolution of 320 by 192, while the commercial reference-to-video systems it sits behind output at 720p and 1080p. So the ranking is a capability comparison, not a resolution-matched one, and the Elo gap to the top two is within bootstrap noise.

Skeptical assessment, then. What I would trust: the corpus construction. Hand-designed generators that randomize nuisance dimensions while fixing the cognitive structure is the right way to build this kind of data, and 1.5 million samples is enough that the training signal cannot be a memorization artifact. The Elo protocol is also better than average for this space: blind, pairwise, rater-clustered bootstrap, and the honest reporting that the top two systems tie statistically. The finding that concept-specific synthetic fine-tuning moves a 16B model 224 Elo points ahead of the next continuation system is the practically useful result, because it says targeted training on physical-prior data works without a new architecture.

What I would re-verify: the claim that these capabilities are the sort that transfer. A 320-by-192 fine-tune on 150 synthetic Blender tasks is a narrow intervention, and the paper's own qualitative analysis is task-level, showing which generators drive the family-level results rather than showing that the permanence behavior generalizes to open-world video. If you are building a world model for robotics or simulation, the actionable piece is the exam and the corpus, not the model weights. The Trainium2 stack is interesting mainly to people on that hardware.

Bottom line: WROP is a clean, well-constructed benchmark-and-corpus contribution that establishes object permanence and solidity as measurable, trainable axes for video world models, with a modest fine-tuned model as an existence proof rather than a product. The exam and the 1.5 million sample corpus are the assets to keep; the 16B model is evidence that the data works, at low resolution, behind the top commercial systems.
