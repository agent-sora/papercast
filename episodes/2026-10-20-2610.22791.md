The paper is Self-Evolving Coding Agents via Harness-Policy Optimization, from Zhepei Wei, Wenlin Yao, and Baolin Peng, at Microsoft Research, with co-authors at the University of Virginia and Carnegie Mellon University. The core observation is that a coding agent's performance is set by two coupled parts: the policy model, and the harness, the external scaffold that decides what the model sees, how its actions run, what feedback it gets, and when an episode ends. Those two parts reinforce each other, so optimizing only one of them is provably suboptimal. A harness tuned for one checkpoint will not target the failure modes of an updated policy, and a policy trained under a fixed harness can only learn from the trajectories that harness elicits. The paper's answer is a method called Self-HPO, a recursive loop that alternates harness optimization and policy optimization, and it uses the evolving policy itself as the proposer of new harnesses, so no separate external model is needed to drive the harness side.

The loop has two steps that alternate. The H-step holds the policy fixed and uses it to analyze the agent's own failed trajectories and propose revised harnesses: new prompts, new tool wiring, new feedback formats, new termination rules. It evaluates candidate harnesses on a search set and selects the best one. The P-step then freezes that selected harness and updates the policy through reinforcement learning, using verified rewards from the executable verifier on the coding tasks. The tasks that the policy never managed to resolve during the P-step become the search set for the next H-step. That last detail is the one that keeps the whole thing targeted: the harness is always searching over the problems the current policy is actually failing, so as the policy's weaknesses move, the harness search moves with them.

The base policy is Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, a Mixture-of-Experts model with 35 billion total parameters and 3 billion active, and the seed harness is mini-SWE-agent, the small single-file scaffold. The objective they optimize is the expected resolve rate, the probability that the executable verifier accepts the agent's patch, averaged over the training tasks. The reinforcement learning is the standard verifiable-reward setup, which they call RLVR, where the reward is the binary pass or fail from running the tests. So the P-step is not doing anything exotic, it is reinforcement learning under a harness that changes each round. The novelty is in the coupling and in who proposes the harness.

A useful way to think about the division of labor: the H-step exposes the policy's weaknesses and compensates for them from the outside, while the P-step internalizes the behaviors the improved harness elicits into the weights. After a P-step, the failures that the next harness should target have shifted, because the policy now fails in different places. That is why the search set is not static. The paper makes a point of contrasting this with the two existing lines of work. Harness optimization literature holds the policy fixed and searches over prompts, workflows, or agent code. Policy optimization literature holds the harness fixed and updates the model from successful trajectories or verifiable rewards. A few very recent works do optimize both, but they rely on an external harness proposer and inherit the moving-target problem this paper addresses.

There is also a second instantiation for comparison. In the self-improving setup, the policy proposes its own harness candidates. In the external setup, a stronger model, Opus 4.8, is used as a fixed harness proposer while the same policy is trained. That comparison isolates how much of the harness gain comes from having a strong proposer versus letting the agent do it itself.

The results are on three software-engineing benchmarks: SWE-Bench Verified, SWE-Bench Multilingual, and SWE-Bench Pro. The baselines are policy-only, which is plain RLVR with a fixed harness, and harness-only, which is a harness-search method they reference as Self-Harness with a frozen policy. On SWE-Bench Verified, the base agent resolves 61.0 percent of tasks. Policy-only and harness-only each improve on that, but Self-HPO reaches 69.5 percent, and it is the only method whose resolve rate climbs at every optimization step. Averaged across all three benchmarks, Self-HPO lifts the resolve rate from 57.8 to 64.8 percent, and the gains carry to the out-of-distribution benchmarks, which matters because the search set is built from in-distribution failures. If you swap in the stronger external proposer, Opus 4.8, the SWE-Bench Verified resolve rate goes further, to 71.6 percent.

The ablations are the part I would weight most. Two of them in particular. First, evolving the harness search set with the hard tasks identified during policy learning beats reusing a fixed search set, which confirms that keeping the harness aligned to the moving failure modes is what produces the extra gain. Second, they show that both optimized components keep their gains even when re-paired with the base version of the other. The optimized harness still helps the base policy, and the optimized policy still helps the base harness, and when you combine the optimized harness with the optimized policy, the joint gain exceeds the sum of the two individual gains. That is a superadditivity result, and it is the strongest evidence that the two components are genuinely co-adapting rather than each just getting better in isolation. The paper also reports that Self-HPO improves both pass at 1, the single-sample reliability, and pass at k, the capability frontier, so it is making the agent both more reliable and capable, not trading one for the other.

It is worth pausing on what the harness actually is in this setting, because the term covers a lot. Here it is the concrete set of scaffolding around the policy: the system prompt, the tool definitions, the formatting of observations, the feedback the agent receives after each step, and the termination rules that decide when an episode ends. Changing the harness is not a weight update. It is editing a configuration and a set of prompts, which is why the H-step can run with a frozen policy and why a proposed harness is cheap to evaluate on the search set. The cost of the loop is dominated by the P-step, the reinforcement learning, not by the harness search. That asymmetry is part of why the self-proposing variant is interesting: the expensive part stays the same, and the cheap part gets better because the policy that knows the failure modes is the one proposing the fix.

What I would trust here is the superadditivity ablation and the out-of-distribution transfer. Those two are the pieces that distinguish a real co-evolution result from a harness-search result with extra steps. The step-by-step monotonic improvement is also clean, and it is the kind of curve that is hard to fake with a single lucky harness. What I would re-verify before adopting this: the whole pipeline is a recursive reinforcement learning loop, which means it is expensive, and the paper reports final numbers but the cost of running multiple H-step and P-step rounds over a 35-billion-parameter model is substantial, and it is not obvious where the compute is spent. The external-proposer comparison is useful but asymmetric, Opus 4.8 is a much stronger model than the policy, so the 71.6 versus 69.5 gap mixes harness quality with proposer strength rather than isolating the self-proposal mechanism. And the search set is defined by tasks never resolved during a P-step, which is a reasonable proxy for the current failure modes, but it means the harness is only ever optimized against the failures the agent surfaces on that particular round, so it can inherit a bias toward the tasks that happen to be in the current batch. A skeptical read: this is a clean, well-ablated demonstration that coupled harness-and-policy optimization beats either one alone, and the self-proposing variant is a genuine step toward an agent that improves its own scaffold. But it is a single base model, a single seed harness, and three in-domain benchmarks, so the general claim that this loop scales is, for now, supported by one instance.
