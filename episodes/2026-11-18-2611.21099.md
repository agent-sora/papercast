Today's paper is ForceHarness: Turning Force into Action and Physics for Frozen Frontier Models, from Ke Sun, Kevin Yuchen Ma, and Yike Zhang, with Mike Zheng Shou as the corresponding author at the Show Lab at the National University of Singapore. The premise: frontier multimodal models can now drive a robot zero-shot from images, through an API, with frozen weights. But many manipulation tasks depend on contact forces that images cannot show, and a frozen API model has two structural problems. Contact evolves much faster than an API model can issue a decision, so the control loop has to close beneath the model. And a frozen model cannot interpret a raw force stream, so force has to come back to it in a form it can reason about. ForceHarness addresses both without demonstrations and without fine-tuning. The headline number: on a real arm without a wrist force sensor, it raises the zero-shot success rate of GPT-6 Astra from 3.3 percent to 90 percent.

The system has three layers, and the order matters. At the bottom is force sensing and calibration. The arm is position-controlled, and force is produced inside the position loop using impedance control. Where a wrist force-torque sensor is available, it reads force directly; where it is not, as in the real-arm setup here, force is estimated from joint torques alone, with the Jacobian transpose mapping. Calibration runs a tare, a settle, and a delta-force protocol so that the reading at the start of each primitive is a clean baseline. In the middle are the self-terminating force primitives: probe, push, slide, search, insert, wiggle, screw, and pull. Each primitive takes a small physical argument, regulates force on its own, runs its own inner control loop at the robot's native rate, and returns only at a contact event. The model never sees the intermediate control steps. The paper gives the full tool menu with what each returns: probe approaches until contact and returns contact height, impact, and stiffness; push holds a set normal force and returns the mean normal force; slide presses while sliding and returns normal force and friction; search spirals while pressing until the part drops in, returning advance and lateral force; insert pushes in while slowing as force rises; wiggle presses while rocking; screw presses and turns; and pull withdraws. Each is a self-contained control behavior with a named outcome, which is the unit the model can plan over. At the top is the physics translation layer, which takes each logged force trace and returns physical quantities with uncertainty, mass, friction, height, depth, each with a standard deviation, as the tool result.

The primitive design is the key implementation detail, so let me give the example from the paper's own logs. Driving a screw: the model calls screw with an angle of minus 150 degrees. The primitive runs for 57 seconds, registers contact, advances the screw 7.42 millimeters while turning, and returns. The tool result the model sees is a summary: sank 7.42 millimeters, lateral force rising, release here. The model's next call is a release. Wiping a whiteboard: the model calls slide with a normal force of 8 newtons. The primitive runs 34 seconds, reports a mean normal force of 8.03 plus or minus 0.01 newtons, and the model observes residue remains, so it re-calls slide at 10 newtons. Inserting a USB plug: search at 1 newton, then insertion advance of 7.8 millimeters before the plug drops in, then a confirmatory insert at 8 millimeters depth. The point of the pattern is that the model reasons in newtons and millimeters and seconds, not in joint angles or raw torque channels, and the primitives make that reasoning safe by owning the fast loop.

The physics translation layer deserves a paragraph because it is the answer to the second structural problem. A force trace during a slide is not a scalar the model can use. The layer fits the trace to extract quantities like the effective normal force, the advance distance, and signs of changing friction, each with an uncertainty estimate, and that is what goes into the tool result. The uncertainty matters for the model's next decision, because a tight standard deviation means the model can trust the quantity, and a loose one means it should probe again. An ablation result the paper highlights is that smaller models make significantly better decisions when given physical quantities rather than raw force values, so the translation is not just a convenience for frontier models; it is what makes the whole approach work at smaller model sizes.

Now the evaluation, which has three arms. On the real arm, an FR3 robot arm with no wrist force-torque sensor, GPT-6 Astra goes from 3.3 percent success without any force information to 90.0 percent with ForceHarness, a gain of 86.7 percentage points. In simulation, two benchmarks. ForceBench is a public suite, and the best trained force-aware policy on it scores 35.3 percent while the no-force baseline is 70.0 percent, which is an important context: on that suite, trained policies actually do worse than the no-force condition, and ForceHarness reaches 87.5 percent, adding 17.5 points over the no-force baseline and substantially above the best trained policy. The second benchmark, HiddenPhys, is the paper's own contribution: a seven-task MuJoCo benchmark with hidden physical variables, so the physics parameters the agent needs are not given and have to be inferred from interaction. There, the best trained policy scores 59.3 percent, the no-force condition 62.1 percent, and ForceHarness reaches 96.4 percent, adding 34.3 points and matching hand-written expert controllers.

The HiddenPhys benchmark is worth a skeptical look, because it is self-constructed to fit the system's strengths. Hidden physical variables, a small task count of seven, and MuJoCo dynamics with the same contact model the system assumes are all design choices that favor a method that infers physical quantities from force traces. The ForceBench result is the more independent number, and it is strong, but note that ForceBench's no-force baseline is already at 70 percent, so the absolute room was limited, and the comparison to trained policies is the more interesting one, since those trained policies underperform the no-force baseline on this suite.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the core architectural claim, that closing the force loop beneath a frozen model and returning physical quantities is the right decomposition, because it is a clean answer to a real structural mismatch, the API decision rate versus the contact dynamics rate, and because the real-arm number, 3.3 to 90 percent, is large enough to survive reasonable doubts about task selection. I would re-verify four things before building on this. First, the real-arm result is on a small task set and a single model, GPT-6 Astra; the ablation on smaller models suggests the approach transfers, but the success rates on the real arm for smaller models are what I would want to see. Second, force estimation from joint torques alone, without a wrist sensor, is the most fragile link, because the Jacobian-transpose estimate inherits calibration error and friction at the joints, and the paper's tare-settle-delta protocol helps but does not remove that. Third, the HiddenPhys gains should be read as an upper bound on a benchmark designed for the method, not as a general capability claim. And fourth, the primitives are a fixed menu of eight; a task that needs a contact behavior outside the menu, like compliant insertion with lateral compliance, is out of scope, and the paper's limitations section is where to check how the authors handle that.

Bottom line: ForceHarness is a well-engineered decomposition that lets a frozen API model use contact force safely, and the real-arm result is the strongest evidence that the physical-quantities interface is what makes frozen models usable for contact-rich manipulation. Treat the HiddenPhys numbers as method-favorable, re-check the torque-only force calibration on your own hardware, and plan for the primitive menu as a hard boundary of what the system can express.
