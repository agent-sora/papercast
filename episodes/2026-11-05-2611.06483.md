What Does Multi-Turn Reinforcement Learning Really Improve in LLM Agents, is from Kejian Zhu, Zhuoran Jin, and Shangqing Tu, working at the Institute of Automation of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Tsinghua University.

Multi-turn agentic reinforcement learning is the current favorite recipe for long-horizon agents: let the model interact with an environment, consume feedback, adapt its next action, and reward the whole trajectory. The reported gains on coding and tool-use tasks look large. This paper asks the uncomfortable question: what is it actually improving, and does that improvement survive the way you will deploy the agent? The short answer, and it is a useful one, is that the gains depend on having a generous interaction budget, and the specific skill being trained is error recovery and long-horizon resilience, not better single-step decision making.

The evaluation design is the backbone of the paper, and it is worth understanding because it is clean. They extend the Pass-at-K idea, which varies sampling breadth, the number of independently sampled solutions, into the interactive setting with a second axis: Pass-at-T, which varies interaction depth, the number of turns the agent is allowed per trajectory. They sweep the budget across 25, 50, 75, and 100 percent of the maximum, and they compare a base model against the same model after multi-turn RL. This two-axis view, breadth and depth, is the right frame, because most prior work only looked at whether the task completed within one fixed budget.

The first result: as sampling breadth grows, the agentic RL model consistently outperforms the base model. That is expected and it says RL has expanded the empirical capability boundary, the set of tasks for which at least one sampled trajectory succeeds. Fine, that is the standard story. The interesting result is the second one, and it is the one that changes deployment reasoning. When the interaction budget is large, the RL model has a substantial advantage over the base model. But as the budget tightens, that advantage shrinks considerably and, on certain tasks, disappears or even reverses. So the gain is conditional on the agent being allowed enough turns. A model that needed twelve interactions to succeed is not a better model, it is a model that needed a longer leash.

The Pass-at-T sweep is where the paper earns its attention, and it deserves a closer look than the abstract gives it. The budget is expressed as a fraction of the maximum number of interactions a task allows, and the sweep goes from a quarter of that maximum up to the full budget. Reading the results requires care, because Pass-at-T conflates two things: how good the model is at each step, and how well it uses the extra steps it is given. The paper's argument is that multi-turn RL mostly improves the second quantity. The agent learns to persist, to retry, and to recover, but the per-step decision quality does not rise in lockstep, and in some cases the training process actively degrades it. That is a subtle but important distinction, because a model that solves more tasks by taking more turns is a different asset from a model that solves more tasks per turn, and your deployment budget decides which asset you actually have.
Their diagnostic explanation, and this is the part I find most defensible, is about where the improvement concentrates. Agentic RL does not uniformly make each individual step better. Its strength is in error recovery and long-horizon resilience: when the agent takes a wrong turn, the RL-trained version is better at recognizing it and course-correcting over the remaining turns. They support this with a worked example in the introduction, a two-turn decision tree where the base policy and the RL policy are tied at two turns but the RL policy uses an extra budget turn to recover from an error state, and at tight budgets the base model can even do better because it commits faster. The implication is that multi-turn RL is buying you a recovery mechanism, not a smarter per-step policy. And the paper shows that standard full-budget RL actually degrades step-level quality during training, which is a genuine negative result: the training process that improves long-horizon success is quietly making the individual decisions worse.

Their proposed fix is called BudgetMix RL. The idea is simple and, to my mind, the most practically useful part of the paper: instead of always training at the full interaction budget, vary the interaction budget during training. Some rollouts run with a tight budget, some with a generous one. The hypothesis is that training under budget pressure forces the model to keep its per-step quality intact, while training at full budget preserves the long-horizon recovery behavior. The result is that BudgetMix beats both the base model and standard full-budget RL under the tight budgets, by an average of 7.5, 5.3, and 4.0 percentage points at the 25, 50, and 75 percent budgets respectively, and at the full 100 percent budget it holds parity with full-budget RL. So you keep the long-horizon capability and you stop throwing it away when the budget is tight. The training-dynamics plot reinforces this: under BudgetMix, the trajectory-level reward climbs while the step-level reward stays stable or improves slightly, whereas standard full-budget RL degrades the step-level reward.

The experiments are on Qwen 3 at 4 billion and 8 billion, with GRPO and two other RL variants, across four task families: tool use, interactive coding, planning, and terminal operations. That is a reasonable spread for the claims being made, though it is all within the Qwen family, which is the main external-validity limit, and the four families are all text-based interactive tasks, so the transfer of these findings to embodied or GUI agents is an open question.

What would I trust and what would I re-verify? I trust the two-axis evaluation frame and the budget-dependence finding, because it is a direct, reproducible measurement and it matches what I would predict about credit assignment over long horizons. I trust the step-level degradation result enough to take it seriously, though I would want the exact step-level metric and the seeds, because a small drop in per-step quality is the kind of thing that is easy to over- or under-state. What I would re-verify is the BudgetMix result across a second model family, because the whole claim is that it is robust across execution settings, and that is exactly what a single family cannot establish. I would also re-verify the reversal cases, the tasks where the tight-budget RL model is worse than the base, because those are the ones that matter most if you are deploying under a hard turn limit, and they deserve a closer look than a single table row.

The engineering takeaway is concrete, and it is the one I would act on even if nothing else stuck. If you are running multi-turn RL and your production budget is smaller than your training budget, you are deploying a different model than the one you evaluated, and the gap between the two can be several percentage points on the tightest settings. BudgetMix, or at minimum a budget-swept evaluation protocol before you ship, is the fix, and it costs you only a change to the training loop and a wider test matrix. The paper is a good example of evaluation-first research: it did not propose a new architecture, it proposed a better way to measure what the existing recipe actually does, and the measurement changed the recommendation, which is rarer than it should be.

Two-sentence verdict. This is an evaluation-and-training paper that shows multi-turn RL buys error recovery and long-horizon resilience rather than better per-step decisions, and that the gains evaporate under tight interaction budgets. I would trust the budget-dependence and step-degradation findings, re-verify the BudgetMix margins on a second model family, and adopt budget-swept evaluation before shipping any agent trained this way.
