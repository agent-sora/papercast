Title: WAM-Policy: World Action Models with On-Policy Distillation for Quadrupedal Loco-Manipulation
Authors: Junlin Long, Xu Deng, Yiran Wang
Labs: Australian Centre for Robotics, University of Sydney; UNSW Sydney; Maincode
Arxiv: 2611.24011
Day: 2026-11-20
Upvotes: 13
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24011
---

WAM-Policy, by Junlin Long, Xu Deng, and Yiran Wang, at the Australian Centre for Robotics at the University of Sydney, UNSW Sydney, and Maincode, is a method for turning a world action model, a model pre-trained for robot-arm manipulation, into a language-conditioned policy for arm-free quadrupedal loco-manipulation, where a quadruped walks on three legs and manipulates objects with a front leg. The specific gap is that no existing policy for this task follows language instructions, and world action models, which are emerging as effective language-conditioned manipulation policies, are pre-trained for arms and sample too slowly to use directly on a legged robot. The contribution is a two-stage method that adapts the arm-centric model to quadrupeds and then compresses it into a fast student, with the whole thing evaluated on a benchmark of ten simulated tasks built from 1,000 demonstrations that were synthesized without any teleoperation.

The prior-art situation is worth stating because it explains why this is a real problem and not a fine-tune. A quadruped doing loco-manipulation has to coordinate locomotion and manipulation simultaneously, which is a fundamentally different control problem from a fixed-base arm, where the base is stable and the only thing to control is the end effector. World action models are built on the assumption of an arm-like action space, so the first thing you have to do is change the morphology, the body the policy is controlling, and that change is where most of the difficulty is. The second difficulty is speed: world action models sample slowly, which is acceptable for a tabletop arm that does not need to react in real time, but a quadruped that is walking and manipulating at the same time has a much tighter real-time budget, so the policy has to be both morphologically correct and fast.

There is one more framing detail that makes the whole thing tractable, and it is the reason the two stages are structured the way they are. A world action model is a high-NFE model, meaning it needs many network function evaluations to sample one action, which is where the slowness comes from. The target in both stages is a low-NFE student that runs on a fraction of that budget, specifically a 1/2-NFE student that consumes one video frame and emits two action steps. Every stage therefore ends with the same operation, distilling a high-NFE teacher into that 1/2-NFE student on-policy. Stage one replaces only the visual and action interfaces of the world action model, swapping the arm-centric ones for the quadruped ones, and fine-tunes it on quadruped demonstrations. That is a deliberately small change, it keeps the pre-trained dynamics knowledge intact and only re-wires the body, which is what makes the cross-morphology adaptation feasible at all instead of retraining from scratch.

The method has two stages, and the naming is doing real work. The first stage is Cross-Morphology Policy Adaptation and Acceleration. It adapts the arm-centric world action model to the quadruped body, and then distills the transferred policy into a student that runs with one video frame and two action steps, using on-policy self-distillation, which the paper abbreviates as OPSD. The idea of on-policy self-distillation here is that the student learns from the teacher's behavior on the trajectories the student itself generates, rather than on a fixed dataset, so the student does not suffer from the distribution mismatch that comes from imitating offline data. The result is a student that keeps most of the teacher's success while sampling 14 times faster, which is the acceleration the quadruped needs. The second stage is Task-Specific Improvement and Merging. It acknowledges that the OPSD student is bounded by its teacher, which only imitated demonstrations and therefore cannot exceed the quality of the demonstrations it was given. So the second stage tunes the teacher with reinforcement learning, which lets it improve beyond the demonstration distribution, and then merges the resulting slow specialist policies into one few-step student by multi-teacher on-policy distillation, abbreviated as M-OPD. The two-stage structure is the key design: stage one gets you a fast policy that is as good as the demonstrations, and stage two uses RL to push past the demonstrations and then compresses the improved specialists back into a fast student.

The results are on QLM-Bench, ten simulated tasks with 1,000 demonstrations synthesized without teleoperation. The transferred policy, after stage one, reaches 77.0 percent success, and the OPSD student keeps 76.7 percent of that while sampling 14 times faster, so the distillation costs almost nothing in success. Then task-specific RL and M-OPD lift it to 85.0 percent, which is the gain from the reinforcement learning stage, an 8 point improvement over the pure imitation student. They also deploy the OPSD student on a real Unitree Go2 Air robot, which is the real-robot validation, and the point of that is to show the policy is not locked to the simulator. The no-teleoperation detail on the demonstrations is worth noting, because it means the 1,000 demonstrations were generated, likely by a simulator or a scripted policy, rather than collected by a human teleoperating the robot, which is a meaningful reduction in data cost for a legged manipulation setup.

The skeptical reading. First, 77.0 to 85.0 percent on a simulated benchmark with 10 tasks is a strong number in the domain, but it is ten tasks, and loco-manipulation on a real quadruped is a much broader space than ten simulated configurations, so the real-robot deployment of the OPSD student, not the RL-merged one, is the more honest signal of what transfers, and the paper reports the RL gains primarily in simulation. Second, the 14 times faster sampling claim is a compression result, and the 0.3 point drop from 77.0 to 76.7 is reassuring, but it is measured on the same ten tasks, so the speedup-success trade is characterized on a narrow task set and you would want to check it on tasks where the slow teacher's extra sampling steps are doing real work, like long-horizon or contact-rich manipulation. Third, the two-stage method has a lot of moving parts, cross-morphology adaptation, on-policy self-distillation, task-specific RL, and multi-teacher merging, and the ablation question that matters for adoption is how much of the 85.0 comes from the RL versus from the merging, because if most of it is the RL, then the value of the distillation machinery is primarily the speedup, and if most of it is the merging, then the multi-teacher compression is the real contribution. The paper's framing suggests the latter, but the clean decomposition is what you would want to verify.

For implementation, the reusable pieces are the on-policy self-distillation for accelerating a slow teacher into a real-time student, and the multi-teacher merging for combining specialized RL policies into one deployable model. If you are working on legged manipulation, the OPSD pattern, distilling on the student's own rollouts with a one-frame, two-action-step budget, is the thing to try first, because it is the cheapest way to get a fast policy from a strong but slow teacher. The no-teleoperation demonstration synthesis is the second asset to inspect, because it is what makes the data pipeline affordable, and it is the detail most likely to be non-obvious when you try to reproduce it. The cross-morphology adaptation is the hard part to transfer to a new body, so if your target robot is not a Unitree Go2, that is where your engineering time will go.

To summarize the bottom line. WAM-Policy is a clean two-stage method that turns an arm-pre-trained world action model into a fast, language-conditioned quadrupedal loco-manipulation policy, with on-policy distillation preserving success at 14 times the speed and task-specific RL plus multi-teacher merging adding an 8 point gain. Its limits are the narrow simulated benchmark, the real-robot validation covering only the OPSD student, and the moving-parts complexity that makes the RL-versus-merging attribution the key thing to verify. If you are building legged manipulation, the on-policy self-distillation for real-time compression is the technique to adopt.
