## Thinking in a Low-Resource Language: What SFT Builds, What RL Fixes

First, a paper that should make every engineer paranoid about their evaluation harness: "Thinking in a Low-Resource Language," from Alibaba, OpenAI, and NVIDIA. The setup: take three frontier mixture-of-experts models with three-and-a-half to four billion active parameters, and fine-tune them to reason in Greek. On accuracy, almost nothing happens. And here is the killer line from the authors: the benchmark itself is noise at this scale, because changing only the random seed shifts the score by seven-point-seven points. That is larger than every single data and recipe effect they measured. If you are tuning models by a point or two of accuracy, you are tuning noise.

But that null result is exactly the point, because the real changes live where accuracy cannot see. The base models never think in Greek: zero out of a thousand reasoning traces, even when the question itself is in Greek. They answer correctly while reasoning entirely in English. What the small-scale training actually does is change the language the model chooses to think in, not whether it gets the answer right. That is a representation shift that standard metrics completely miss.

The practical takeaway is uncomfortable and useful at once. First, token-level accuracy is the wrong tool when you care about emergent behavior like internal reasoning language. Second, because the effect is hidden, you need trace-level inspection, not just benchmark scores. And third, the authors draw a real distinction between what supervised fine-tuning builds and what reinforcement learning fixes. SFT gives the model the surface behavior of thinking in the target language. RL is what actually shapes and stabilizes when and how it does so. If any of your eval cards report only accuracy, assume there is a seven-point seed floor you are not seeing.

## Hierarchical Self-Improvement: Evolvable Agent Harnesses

Next, "Hierarchical Self-Improvement," from Tailin Zhou. The core observation: when we improve an LLM agent, we almost always edit the prompts, the tools, or the workflow — but the executable scaffold that wraps the model, the harness, is treated as a fixed artifact the moment we deploy it. This paper argues the harness itself should be task-specific and continuously evolvable.

The design is elegant. Each task family maintains its own harness, which gets hot-swapped across iterations through a fixed task-injection seam, and rewritten using feedback from the environment. Crucially, the model itself stays frozen. A single frozen LLM operates the loop; what changes between iterations is the harness. So the learnable surface is not the weights, it is the executable state around a fixed model.

For a staff engineer this is a really appealing architecture, because it separates the expensive thing — the model — from the cheap thing — the orchestration code around it. You can evolve the harness at inference time without touching weights, without a training run, without the regulatory and ops burden of redeploying a model. The task-injection seam is the key API: it is the fixed contract that lets you swap one harness for another without rewiring the world. If you build agent systems, think hard about where that seam is in your own stack, because that is what turns a rigid deployment into something you can improve without breaking your model.

## Inject, Align, Recover: Retrieval-Free Document Knowledge

Third, "Inject, Align, Recover," a three-stage post-training framework for what the authors call document knowledge internalization. The problem: an LLM frequently fails to answer questions about a bounded document collection when you do not retrieve the source documents at inference time. You might think retrieval is the answer, but retrieval is a runtime dependency: an index, an embedder, latency, and a surface area for failure. This paper asks whether you can convert a fixed corpus into usable parametric knowledge instead — so the model just knows it, no lookup needed.

IAR separates the job into three stages that classical continued pretraining smears together. Stage one, Inject, converts the source documents into continuation-style data and trains them in. Stage two, Align, tunes the model toward the QA behavior you actually care about on that knowledge. Stage three, Recover, restores the model's general abilities, which almost always degrade when you bolt on a corpus.

That decomposition is the whole insight. Continued pretraining treats injection, alignment, and recovery as one undifferentiated step, so you cannot trade them off. Separating them lets you control how hard you inject, how aggressively you align, and how much you pay to recover. For anyone stuffing a knowledge base into a model, this is the frame you should use: separate the injection from the alignment from the recovery, because those three objectives fight each other if you optimize them as one.

## FlowEvo: Self-Evolving Agents

Fourth, "FlowEvo," a training-free framework where workflows and skills co-evolve at inference time. The problem statement is sharp. An LLM agent can construct workflows on the fly to handle a complex task, but a procedure discovered in one episode is usually thrown away as soon as that episode ends. Meanwhile, the skill libraries people do build tend to be assembled offline and never grow from what the agent actually does.

FlowEvo closes that loop. When an agent successfully completes a workflow, FlowEvo compiles it into a callable skill and stores it in a persistent bank. Later, when a related task arrives, the agent retrieves the relevant skills and uses them either by executing them directly, or by feeding them in as context. So the bank grows from real successful episodes, and the workflows the agent builds become reusable skills, which in turn shape future workflows. That is the co-evolution.

For a senior engineer the appeal is that it is training-free — no fine-tuning, no weight updates, just a growing library plus retrieval. That is the same philosophy as the harness paper: evolve the state, not the weights. The interesting engineering problem is the compilation step, turning a messy episodic workflow into something reliably callable, and the retrieval policy that decides between execute versus inject-as-context. If you have an agent that repeats similar tasks, FlowEvo is the pattern you want to borrow.

## TinyCast: Probabilistic Zero-Shot Forecasting

Fifth, "TinyCast," and this one is a personal favorite. It is an attention-free, zero-shot forecaster with a staggering one-hundred-forty-six-thousand-five-hundred-and-five parameters. The premise is stated almost as a provocation: at this size, the periodic structure of a context is worth computing rather than learning.

Here is the architecture. A zero-parameter spectral detector computes the dominant periods in the input context. The context is then folded on the phase of those periods. A dilated convolutional encoder, plus a block-autoregressive quantile decoder, models whatever is left. The spectral step does the heavy lifting that a big attention stack would otherwise have to learn.

On GIFT-Eval, it is smaller than every zero-shot entry whose parameter count can even be established, and it defines the size-accuracy frontier on probabilistic accuracy. For forecasting workloads, "zero-shot" means you do not need to retrain for each new series, "probabilistic" means you get a predictive distribution rather than a single number, and 146K parameters means it runs essentially anywhere, on a phone, in an edge box, inside a trading loop. If you have time-series data and you are currently fine-tuning a model several orders of magnitude larger, this paper is a direct challenge to your architecture. Compute the periodicity, fold on the phase, and let a tiny dilated network handle the residual.

## Chain-of-Experience for Continual LLM Improvement

Finally, "Chain-of-Experience." The opening claim is the interesting one: humans continuously learn from experience, but conventional LLM evaluation ignores the model's ability to improve through interaction at inference time. The authors introduce Chain-of-Experience, where a model accumulates experiential traces through repeated interactions with itself or with the environment, forming a continual improvement loop that goes beyond any single zero-shot inference.

So instead of asking, can the model answer this cold, you ask, can the model get better at this over the course of a session? The feedback can come from the model's own self-reflection, or from external environmental signals. That traces are accumulated and carried forward across interactions is what turns a one-shot eval into a test of learning.

For anyone deploying agents in the real world, this reframes how you should evaluate. Two identical models with identical weights can perform very differently depending on whether they are allowed to chain experience. So your eval should measure not just cold accuracy but the improvement rate within a session. It also argues for systems that keep context and working memory warm across turns, because the model's ability to improve depends entirely on having those traces to reason over.
