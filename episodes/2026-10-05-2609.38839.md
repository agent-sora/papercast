Title: FrameMorrow: Future-guided Frame Selection with Prospective Tokens for Long-Horizon Video Generation
Authors: Bo Yin, Xiaobin Hu, Jiaqi Zhao
Labs: National University of Singapore; Harbin Institute of Technology Shenzhen
Arxiv: 2609.38839
Day: 2026-10-05
Upvotes: 20
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38839
---
FrameMorrow is from Bo Yin, Xiaobin Hu, and Jiaqi Zhao at the National University of Singapore and the Harbin Institute of Technology Shenzhen. It is a frame selector for long-horizon video generation that decides which past frames to keep by asking what will matter next, rather than what matters right now. The paper frames it as a plug-and-play module that can sit in front of a wide range of video generators, including closed-source models, with little extra inference cost.

The problem is specific to long videos. As a generator keeps extending a sequence, the amount of past content it has produced grows, and holding all of it becomes both expensive and redundant. Early details fall out of the model's input window, and when those details become relevant again, the subject has drifted, an object's state is inconsistent, or a revisited scene no longer matches how it looked before. The obvious fix, keep everything, is infeasible at scale. The standard approach to the problem is to select past frames by their relevance to the current frame, and that is the gap the paper targets. Relevance to the present is not the same as usefulness for the future. Information that looks important right now may be irrelevant a second later, while something that looks trivial now may be exactly what you need three seconds from now. The claim is that you should be selecting history by its relevance to future information needs.

The key insight is that you do not need to generate the whole future to know what it will need. A compact representation of what becomes important next is enough to guide the selection. FrameMorrow builds a small set of prospective tokens, a short latent code that summarizes the upcoming information need, and it uses those tokens to score and rank candidate past frames. Concretely, the selector predicts the prospective tokens for the next segment, then measures each candidate historical frame against them with a temperature-scaled similarity, and keeps the top frames. Because the output is a set of explicit frames rather than model-internal states, the module does not need to know anything about the generator's internals, which is why it can be attached to closed-source models. The selector itself is trained with a future-grounded ranking objective, where a teacher model, which has access to the actual future continuation, provides the target ranking, and a student learns to reproduce that ranking using only the prospective tokens. That is the training trick: the teacher sees the future the student will never get, and distills that foresight into a compact signal.

The implementation is deliberately lightweight. The prospective representation is a small number of tokens, and the ranking is a simple similarity computation over candidate frames, so the added cost per step is a small similarity pass rather than a full forward through a second model. The paper reports this holds across the different backbones they test, which is the point of the plug-and-play design.
The evaluation is broad in the way the paper wants it to be. They test across five benchmarks and eleven generative models, spanning three settings: long-video generation, interactive generation, and action-conditioned world models. That spread is what the plug-and-play claim rests on. If the selector only worked inside one open generator's training loop, the closed-source story would fall apart, so the multi-model coverage is load-bearing.

The main quantitative table is on VBench-Long, measuring 60-second generation over all 128 MovieGenBench prompts. The numbers track several baseline configurations: a self-forcing baseline, a position-encoding variant, a deep-forcing variant, and a retrieval-augmented long-live variant, and then FrameMorrow on top. In the reported table, the FrameMorrow configuration reaches an average quality score around 68.47 with an average rank of 1.33, against a retrieval-augmented baseline around 64.97 and the self-forcing baseline around 62.22. The consistent pattern across the table is that FrameMorrow improves the long-range consistency and visual quality scores while keeping the action-alignment metric up, which is the trade-off a naive frame selection would get wrong, because dropping frames to save cost usually hurts the consistency of the action the video is supposed to depict.

The honest part of the results is that the individual metric deltas are not huge. We are talking about single-digit-point improvements on the composite quality scores and improvements in the average rank, not order-of-magnitude jumps. The value of the paper is less in the size of any single number and more in the consistency of the direction across eleven different generators and three different generation regimes. A method that moves the needle in the same direction across that much diversity is a stronger claim than a big number in one setting, because it says the selection principle, not a particular architecture, is doing the work.

The skeptical read. The multi-model evaluation is the paper's real strength, and it is exactly what you want to see for a module that claims to be architecture-agnostic. The concerns are the magnitude and the measurement. The per-metric gains are modest, so if you are choosing a frame-selection method for a production long-video pipeline, a single-digit-point quality improvement has to be weighed against the added inference cost and the engineering of a second selector model in the loop, and the paper is relatively light on the exact cost in latency and memory of running the prospective-token predictor at every step. The teacher-to-student distillation is clever but introduces a quality ceiling set by the teacher, and the paper does not deeply ablate how much of the gain comes from the future-grounded teacher ranking versus just having a better frame-ranking model in general. The VBench-Long table is a single benchmark family for the headline numbers, and the other four benchmarks are reported at a higher level, so the fine structure of the gains across them is harder to verify from the main text.

Verdict: FrameMorrow is a clean, well-motivated contribution with a genuinely useful reframing of the frame-selection problem, select for the future rather than the present, and the breadth of the evaluation across eleven generators and three regimes is the most convincing part. The main caveats are the modest per-metric gains, the light treatment of the per-step inference cost, and the single-benchmark headline, so treat the selection principle as well supported and the specific magnitude of the quality lift as the number you would want to reproduce on your own pipeline before adopting it.
The training setup is worth pausing on, because it is the part that makes the module practical rather than just a nice idea. The teacher that provides the future-grounded ranking has access to the actual future continuation, which the student never sees at inference time. The student is trained to reproduce the teacher's frame ranking using only the prospective tokens it can compute on the fly. That distillation is what converts hindsight, which is available only in a setting where you already have the future, into foresight, which is what the module needs at generation time. It is a clean way to get the benefit of knowing what matters next without actually having to generate the next frames first, and it is the reason the selector can run cheaply at inference. The temperature-scaled similarity that ranks the candidate frames is the same computation in training and inference, so there is no train-inference mismatch in the ranking step itself, which is a property that a lot of retrieval-style methods quietly lose.
There is a reason the selector works across closed-source models, and it is worth being explicit about it. Because the output of the module is a set of frame indices rather than a modified internal activation, it sits entirely upstream of the generator's forward pass. You hand the generator a curated subset of its own past frames, and the generator treats them exactly as it would treat any reference frames, with no knowledge that they were selected by a foresight-driven ranking. That is what makes the integration genuinely plug-and-play, you do not need gradients, you do not need to modify the model, and you do not need access to the model's weights, which is precisely the situation with a closed-source API model. The cost of that generality is that the selector can only influence the generator through the frame set it is given, so its lever is the selection, and the quality of the final video is bounded by how well the generator uses the frames it is handed, which is outside the module's control.
