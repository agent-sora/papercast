Fast Weight Attention for Continual Learning, by Yifan Zhang of ByteDance Seed and Princeton University, Steve Ta and Jasper Zhang, with further authors from ByteDance Seed, Princeton, Tsinghua, UCLA, and Hyperbolic Labs, including Andrew Chi-Chih Yao among the corresponding authors. The framing is worth pausing on, because it reframes an architecture debate as a learning problem. Standard attention externalizes memory as a growing key-value cache: nothing is ever compressed, nothing is ever forgotten, and quadratic cost follows. State-space models and fast-weight architectures instead compress the whole context into a fixed-size recurrent state, which means the state update is literally an online learning rule, a little gradient step executed every token, inside the forward pass. Seen that way, the real design question for these architectures is not what shape the state has but what objective that internal learning rule is actually optimizing, and when it gets to see its training pairs. That question has been implicit in every linear-attention and Delta-network paper so far; this one makes it explicit and finds that the field has been using the wrong pairing.

The central observation is a one-position misalignment. Under read-after-write autoregressive semantics, when the model is predicting token t, the state it reads was last written at t minus one; the value that becomes available at step t should therefore be bound to the prefix feature that was available when it was predicted, the key from the previous position, not the key from the current one. Most prior recurrences bind the same-step pair, current key with current value. That pairing is still causal, but it optimizes a different internal objective: the memory is trained on information that was not yet available at prediction time. The paper's family, named Falcon, writes the shifted pair instead, and derives all its variants as normalized first-order updates on two local objectives: ridge regression onto the newly revealed target, and a negative inner-product objective whose writes are purely additive. The numbers name the dynamics: Falcon-one applies a scalar normalized step, the classical NLMS recursion with a shrinkage term; Falcon-two promotes the step size to a per-channel vector, one plasticity rate per value dimension, with a dual form derived so per-column adaptivity stays tractable on GPUs; Falcon-three replaces the single example with a sliding window of recent causal pairs, a bounded-rehearsal mini-batch step whose normalization matches the window's curvature. The A variants are the inner-product counterparts. All of them come with recurrent, masked-parallel, and chunk-parallel forms compatible with existing state-space-duality training kernels, plus a numerically stable treatment of the decay term, and a short lemma guarantees each normalized step descends the instantaneous objective.

The honest headline: this is an analysis and framework paper with modest-scale experiments, not a frontier-model result. Language models of one hundred twenty-four to one hundred thirty million parameters were trained on FineWeb-Edu with a matched fifty-billion-token budget against a LLaMA-style transformer baseline and the strong recurrent field, RetNet, Mamba-two, DeltaNet, and Gated DeltaNet. On validation perplexity the regression variant Falcon-one-point-three is strongest overall at seventeen point one zero against seventeen point three two for Gated DeltaNet, the best baseline, with the inner-product Falcon-one-A at seventeen point four zero; on downstream averages the Falcon variants take the best zero-shot and one-shot recurrent numbers, around forty-nine point three to forty-nine point five across eight tasks, against forty-eight point eight for the best baseline, and roughly at parity with the full transformer. The paper's own words are that the takeaway is not a uniform win but that the aligned, normalized updates preserve competitive language-model quality. The sharper result is the controlled arithmetic stress test, variable-length multi-digit addition, where the model must store digits and propagate carries: there, the sliding inner-product variant Falcon-three-A reaches eighty-seven point two percent out-of-distribution accuracy extrapolating to thirty-three to forty-eight digits, with Falcon-one-A at eighty-five point nine, beating the transformer and the recurrent baselines, which is exactly what you would expect if correct temporal alignment of the write rule matters most when storage and retrieval dominate.

What the framework buys, beyond the specific numbers, is a clean separation of four design axes that the field usually entangles: temporal alignment, when the pair is written; plasticity, how fast the state moves, controlled by a dimensionless gain bounded below two for stability; forgetting, the explicit shrinkage term; and rehearsal, whether updates carry one example or a bounded window. Reading existing architectures through that grid is illuminating: linear attention and Mamba-two writes turn out to be gradient descent on an inner-product objective with unnormalized steps, DeltaNet is one gradient step on same-step squared error, and gated variants add the shrinkage term, so the whole zoo becomes a family of online optimizers differing in objective, alignment, and normalization, and the Falcon rules are the objective-aligned, curvature-normalized corners of that family.

Two engineering details matter to implementers. First, step-size normalization is objective-matched: the regression rules divide by the write feature's squared norm plus the ridge term, which makes the update invariant to key scale, while the inner-product variants divide by a write-energy statistic that acts as a magnitude stabilizer rather than a curvature requirement, and using the wrong normalizer for your objective is exactly the kind of silent mismatch this framework is designed to expose. Second, everything stays compatible with chunk-parallel training in the state-space-duality style, so none of the rules require giving up modern training kernels; the sliding-window variant additionally needs a fixed-width tail of the last few causal pairs carried across chunk boundaries for exact continuation, a small bookkeeping cost called out in the appendix. The per-channel rules are also given a vectorized dual form so per-column adaptivity does not serialize the GPU.

The skeptical read. First, scale: everything is at one hundred thirty million parameters and fifty billion tokens; the gap between normalization schemes that matters at this scale can vanish or invert at billions, and no experiment here tests that. Second, the language-modeling wins are thin, a few hundredths of a perplexity point over Gated DeltaNet, well within single-run noise, and no seeds or variance are reported, so the safe claim is parity plus better extrapolation, not dominance. Third, the shifted alignment claim, while carefully argued, is validated indirectly: the strongest evidence is the arithmetic task where alignment should matter most, not an ablation that flips alignment within an otherwise identical architecture, which would be the cleanest test and is not reported. Fourth, four of the six derived variants are defined but never benchmarked, including both per-column rules, so the family's most interesting members, per-channel plasticity being the one most likely to matter at scale, remain empirically uncharacterized. Fifth, the continual-learning framing is aspirational: the experiments cover next-token prediction and arithmetic, not genuine online adaptation drift, and no interference or forgetting benchmarks appear. What I would trust: the objective-alignment analysis itself, which is careful, correct-looking, and immediately usable by anyone implementing linear attention or Delta-style rules, since it is one index shift and a normalized step away. The boundary handling is also thought through: the first position uses a zero write feature and a zero step size, so no decay is applied to a state that has not yet received data, a small detail that quietly prevents a real class of initialization bugs. What I would re-verify: the alignment effect at realistic scale, and the per-column variants under the dual-form implementation. Bottom line: the paper's value is the lens, that a fixed-size recurrent state is an online learner and its update rule is a learning rule you can reason about with optimization tools, and its concrete gift is a family of normalized, correctly aligned update rules that hold their own on language modeling while clearly winning on tasks where memory, not modeling, is the bottleneck. For anyone betting on subquadratic architectures, the design conversation is shifting from what the state looks like to what it is being trained to do, token by token, and this paper supplies the vocabulary.
