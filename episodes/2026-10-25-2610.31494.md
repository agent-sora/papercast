AgentVQA: A Unified Benchmark for Agentic Visual Understanding, by Advait Gupta, Sachin Konan, and Tom Yin, from the University of Maryland, College Park, and Princeton University.

Vision-language models have become remarkably broad in their demonstrated abilities, but the paper's opening observation is that we do not actually understand how well they perform in agentic contexts, where the model is one component of a control loop acting on the world. Online evaluation in simulators and real environments is the gold standard for measuring interactive agents, but it is expensive, hard to parallelize, and hard to reproduce across labs. Offline benchmarks exist, but they are fragmented: each domain, web agents, robotics, egocentric video, games, spatial planning, has its own evaluation setup with its own question formats, and there is no single instrument that lets you compare a model across all of them. AgentVQA is that instrument: an offline multiple-choice visual question answering benchmark that unifies five agentic domains in one evaluation harness.

The construction pipeline is the part a practitioner would scrutinize. They start from 23 existing source datasets spanning the five domains. From those they manually select 1000 challenging, real-world agent examples and adapt them for reliable offline evaluation. The selection is heavily filtered: roughly 22 percent of the initial candidate pool survives automatic filters for correctness, difficulty, diversity, and relevance to agentic decision-making, and human annotators then review, edit, and repair the survivors, rejecting approximately 60 percent of what reaches that stage. The result is a benchmark where every item is verified, but the verification cost is real, and the 1000-item ceiling is a deliberate trade between quality and scale. The five domains are web agents, where the model must localize UI elements in screenshots; egocentric videos, where the model must interpret first-person dynamic scenes; robotics, where the model must reason about physical manipulation states; games, where the model must read game state and infer strategic implications; and spatial planning, where the model must reason about navigation and spatial relations.

The headline result is that across 22 evaluated models, the best achieves only 58.5 percent accuracy. That is comfortably above random chance on four-choice questions, but it also tells you the agentic visual understanding problem is far from solved, even by the strongest closed systems. The domain breakdown is where the structure of the field becomes visible. The strongest model, Gemini 3.7 Flash, leads on Spatial Planning at 62.5 percent and Robotics at 52.5 percent, but the hardest domain is Web Agents, where only three of the 22 models exceed 40 percent and the best manages 47 percent on the thinking-high variant. Games shows the largest spread, from 23 to 70 percent across models, which the paper reads as evidence that strategic reasoning is a capability with high variance rather than a capability uniformly shared by frontier models. Qwen3.7 Flash leads Egocentric Videos at 72.5 percent, which is notably the strongest single-domain number in the whole table. The domain difficulty ordering is itself a finding: the benchmark is not uniformly hard, and a model's ranking depends heavily on where its visual strengths sit. Web Agents, the domain closest to the actual deployment scenario for most commercial agents, is also the one where even the best models fail most often, which is the uncomfortable alignment between what the field deploys and what the benchmark shows it cannot do.

The cross-domain rank instability is a second finding, and arguably the more interesting one. The strongest model differs by domain. Gemini 3.7 Flash leads overall at 58.5 percent, but Qwen3.8 27B, the strongest open model, trails it by 7.6 points at 50.9 percent, and Kimi K3 at 48.3 percent, Qwen3.5 35B at 47.9 percent, and Qwen3.5 397B at 47.8 percent all outperform Claude Opus 4.8 at 44.0 percent and Sonnet 5 at 43.4 percent. A 397B open model outscoring a frontier closed model on a visual benchmark is a data point the open-weights community will want to see replicated, though the paper does not analyze the variance around those gaps. GPT-5.6-sol gains 16.8 points when its thinking budget is enabled, the largest reasoning-budget effect in the table, which suggests the benchmark has a substantial hard-tail that extended deliberation can crack for some models but not others.

The context-dependence ablation is a useful design finding. On the multi-step task suites, removing conversation history from the input decreases average performance by 9.5 percent on one suite and 10 percent on the other. The paper's conclusion is that historical context access is critical for multi-step agentic tasks, which is a warning to anyone designing offline evaluations: if you strip the trajectory history to make the benchmark cleaner, you are measuring a different capability than the one the agent has in deployment. The error taxonomy rounds out the analysis. They classify Gemini 3.1 Pro's incorrect predictions, and 78 percent of the errors are grounding errors, failures to locate the correct interface element. In Games, 71 percent of errors are visual miscomprehension, the model fails to read the game state rather than failing to apply the rules. Robotics and Spatial Planning are dominated by spatial confusion and viewpoint gaps at 65 and 62 percent respectively. Egocentric video errors are more distributed, with motion misperception at 39 percent, perception gaps at 29, and spatial confusion at 15. The taxonomy is useful because it maps each domain's dominant failure to a fixable component. Grounding errors point at the perception stack and the way the interface is presented to the model. Visual miscomprehension points at video understanding and frame selection. Spatial confusion and viewpoint gaps point at 3D reasoning, which is the capability least represented in current pretraining data. A model selection or architecture change addresses one of these, not all of them, and the benchmark tells you which one your deployment actually suffers from.

The skeptical read: the benchmark's value is in the unification, and the weakness is in the sample size. One thousand items across five domains is 200 per domain on average, and with multiple models per domain, per-model confidence intervals will be wide. The model rankings tracking online evaluations is claimed but the correlation analysis deserves a second look before you make procurement decisions off this table. The multiple-choice format compresses open-ended agentic behavior into four options, which is what makes it cheap and reproducible, but it also means a model that would have failed the actual task for the wrong reason can pick the right letter by pattern-matching the distractor style. The construction pipeline partially defends against this, since annotators repair and verify every retained example and reject roughly 60 percent of what reaches human review, but the four-option frame itself caps the ceiling: a model that knows the answer but cannot commit to a single choice scores zero, and there is no partial credit for near-correct spatial localization. The 1000-item scale is also a deliberate constraint on what you can conclude. Cross-domain comparisons are meaningful at this scale, but any per-model, per-domain number that matters for procurement should be treated as directional until you have run your own task distribution. The 78 percent grounding-error rate is the number to take into a design review: if your agent's visual failures are mostly localization failures, your fix is probably in the perception stack, not the reasoning stack, and no amount of extended thinking will recover a model that cannot find the button. The benchmark is a reasonable offline proxy for the online evaluations it was designed to complement, and the honest way to use it is as a fast screen that you confirm with simulator runs before trusting. The five-domain coverage also means a single model score never tells the whole story: the leaderboard should be read as a vector of domain strengths, and the deployment decision should key on the one domain your agent actually operates in, not the average.
