Distilling LLM Feedback for Lean Theorem Proving, by Gaetan Narozniak, Gérard
Biau, Rémi Munos, and co-authors including Ahmad Rammal and Pierre Marion,
from FAIR at Meta, Inria, and ENS at the PSL Research University.

This is a post-training paper, and the problem it starts from is the one that
limits almost every reinforcement-learning pipeline for formal mathematics.
The standard recipe is supervised fine-tuning on expert solutions followed by
reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards, and the most common RL algorithm
is GRPO, which samples several candidate proofs for the same statement, scores
them, and nudges the policy toward the ones that type-check. That recipe has a
well-documented failure mode, and the paper is explicit about it: GRPO suffers
from sparse rewards, because most attempts on a hard theorem are simply wrong
and give no gradient signal, it limits exploration, because the policy quickly
stops trying novel lemma combinations, and it collapses, meaning the policy
entropy drops until the model generates the same handful of high-reward
trajectories it already knew how to produce before training started. The paper
cites the recent evidence that GRPO does not actually amplify the diversity of
model answers during training, and in a formal-mathematics setting, where the
whole point is to explore a huge library of lemmas, that collapse is exactly the
wrong behavior to have.

The proposal is Feedback Distillation, and the mechanism is worth stating
precisely. The model is trained, at the token level, to match its own
distribution conditioned on privileged feedback produced by a separate language
model. So the procedure is: the student model attempts a proof, an external
model, in their example Claude, looks at the attempt and produces feedback,
which might be a compiler error, a note that a lemma is being misapplied, or a
suggestion of a different tactic, and then the student is trained to produce the
tokens it would have produced had it had that feedback available to it in the
first place. The key property is that this is token-level supervision, not a
single scalar reward, so the gradient signal is dense rather than sparse, and it
can carry specific content, the external model's knowledge about which lemma to
use, which type coercion is missing, which tactic sequence is wrong, rather than
just a yes or no. That is the difference between a reward and a correction, and
it is what lets the method inject external knowledge into the training loop
rather than just selecting among what the student already knows.

The evaluation is on Lean 4 theorem proving, and there is a concrete example
they use to illustrate the mechanism, drawn from the MiniF2F dataset, with
Qwen3-8B as the student. The specific case is a statement involving a real
number, and the student model assigns substantial probability to writing the
unicode symbol for pi directly, about fifty-six percent of its mass on that
token, whereas the teacher model, which has been shown the compiler feedback,
strongly prefers Lean's canonical form, assigning ninety-eight point one percent
to the token for the Real type, versus only one point eight percent to the
unicode pi and zero point one percent to the double-struck real symbol. That is
a clean illustration of what feedback distillation is doing: the external
model's feedback, which in this case is the compiler telling the student that
the unicode symbol does not type-check in this context, is being distilled into
the student's token distribution so that next time the student writes the
canonical form without needing the feedback.

The headline quantitative result is on a test split of the LeanWorkbook dataset,
using Qwen3.5-9B as the base model. GRPO alone achieves fifty-nine percent
pass@1. Feedback Distillation combined with GRPO, where GRPO is initialized
from a Feedback Distillation checkpoint, achieves seventy-five percent. That is
a sixteen-point absolute gap, and the framing is that the two methods are
complementary: Feedback Distillation improves the policy's diversity and
entropy, which gives GRPO more to work with, and GRPO then refines the
improved policy. The paper also reports that Feedback Distillation maintains
higher policy entropy than GRPO and scales better with pass@k, which is the
diversity claim made concrete: a higher-entropy policy produces more distinct
valid proofs across k samples, which is the thing you want when the search
space is a large lemma library.

Now the skeptical read. The method requires an external feedback model at
training time, and the quality of the training signal is bounded by the quality
of that model's feedback on the specific theorem class being trained. If the
external model does not understand the particular area of mathematics, the
feedback it provides will be wrong or unhelpful, and the distillation will be
distilling noise. The paper uses Claude as the feedback model, which is
reasonable, but the ablation over feedback-model quality is the thing a
reproducer would need to see, because the method's value is entirely in the
feedback channel. The paper does in fact explore the self-feedback variant,
where the feedback model is a frozen copy of the model itself rather than an
external system, and the result is instructive: even in that setting, combining
GRPO with self-feedback distillation still beats GRPO alone, but by a smaller
margin than when a stronger external model provides the feedback. That
comparison is the cleanest evidence for the paper's central claim, because it
isolates the source of information: the model can extract some useful signal
from analyzing its own generations, but the external model's competence is what
closes most of the remaining gap, which points specifically at the feedback
model's quality as the variable that matters.

The tool-use dimension is the third thread of the paper, and it is the one that
connects this work to the broader direction of agents that interact with
compilers as tools rather than models that simply emit proof text. When the
model is given a tool interface, so that it can run the Lean compiler and read
the error output before deciding its next tactic, the training becomes more
stable and the accuracy is higher, and the effect holds for both the
Qwen3-8B and Qwen3.5-9B configurations. The paper also reports an interesting training-dynamics detail about the
exponential moving average that keeps the teacher tracking the student: a more
aggressive EMA, meaning a lower interpolation coefficient, accelerates early
learning but induces greater instability and ultimately limits peak
performance, while a fully frozen teacher, the other extreme, is the baseline
the EMA was introduced to avoid. The EMA coefficient is therefore a hyperparameter
worth tuning rather than a free lunch, and the paper uses a value of zero point
nine for the main runs. The combination of
tool use and the EMA schedule is what makes the RL stage practical, because
without the compiler in the loop, the model is learning from a weaker signal,
and with an aggressive EMA, the diversity the feedback distillation was meant to
preserve gets eroded again. The LeanWorkbook result is on a specific dataset and a
specific base model, and the transfer to other theorem-proving distributions,
particularly ones that go well beyond the level of LeanWorkbook, is an open
question. The entropy claim is the most theoretically interesting, because it
is the claim that the method is actually doing something GRPO is not, but
entropy is a proxy for diversity, and a higher-entropy policy could in principle
be producing more distinct but all-wrong proofs, which would be a diversity
gain without a correctness gain. The pass@k scaling curve is the right test for
that, and the paper reports it, so that is the figure a reader should look at
before trusting the diversity claim. And the initialization trick, starting
GRPO from a Feedback Distillation checkpoint, is a specific recipe whose
sensitivity to the checkpoint's training length and the base model's size is
the kind of thing that can vary substantially across configurations.

Bottom line: this is a clean and well-motivated post-training method that
addresses a real failure mode of GRPO in formal mathematics, with a concrete
token-level mechanism, a clear illustration of what the feedback carries, and a
strong headline result on LeanWorkbook. The open question is how the method
scales to harder theorem distributions and whether the entropy gain translates
into a robust correctness gain across the full range of Lean 4 problem types,
rather than just the ones LeanWorkbook covers.
