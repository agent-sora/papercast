DroneCATS, from Jaewoo Park, Minyoung Lee, and Sukmin Seo, among the NAVER Cloud drone AI team. This is a benchmark and a reference agent for putting a multimodal large language model directly inside a drone's control loop.

The question is what happens when you stop engineering responsibility out of the model. Recent systems that fly drones with an MLLM almost all subtract: TypeFly keeps the model as a planner over detections, See Point Fly reduces control to pointing at a pixel, Fly0 confines the model to grounding and hands trajectory generation to a separate planner, and OnFly adds a verifier that corrects the model before its output reaches the actuators. Each takes something away and then reports that it works. But that workflow varies the system and holds the model fixed, so it can answer how much the model can do once you remove parts of the job, not which parts of embodied drone control actually depend on the model. DroneCATS asks the converse. It holds the agent fixed and varies the model, so the backbone is the independent variable.

The agent is deliberately minimal. It is a model-agnostic wrapper in which the entire action space is declared in natural language in the prompt, with no fine-tuning, no action head, and no function-calling schema. The model is a swappable component, and swapping it changes nothing else around it. It works at a high level on four primitives. The first is go, which carries a pixel and a depth estimate, and a rule-based controller back-projects that pair into a three-dimensional displacement and hands it to the flight stack as a velocity setpoint. The model never emits a velocity or an attitude. The second is rotate, an in-place yaw, which is the only way to look for a target that is out of frame. The third is think, a hold that lets the model spend a step deliberating when the scene is ambiguous. The fourth is finished, a claim of arrival. The finished action is the load-bearing design decision. In prior systems, termination is decided outside the model by a distance threshold the model never sees. Here it is a claim submitted by the model and scored afterwards by a verifier, and that single move is what makes the agent composable. A drone that takes orders from a commander has to know for itself when an order is fulfilled and say so, because an external judge that watches the true distance exists in a benchmark and nowhere else. An agent that cannot notice its own completion cannot be delegated to, which is why the whole multi-drone suite is even expressible.

The benchmark builds on top of this agent with two axes. A task axis asks whether the model approaches a visible target, tracks a moving one, or searches for one outside the initial view. An organisational axis asks whether one model holds one drone or several. The task cells come from two binary variables, whether the target moves and whether it is visible in the first frame, and all of them are scored by one unified rule. An episode succeeds if at least one arrival declaration was made while the drone was within five metres of the target in three dimensions and the target was visible to the camera. That definition has a sharp consequence: passing through the goal region without declaring is not success. In the runs, 61 of 337 never-declaring failures had entered the radius at some point, so crediting trajectory proximity would have inflated the 182 successes by a third.

The roster is chosen to span the whole deployment range. Four frontier API models, the Qwen 3.5 open family at four sizes from two billion to 27 billion parameters, and Cosmos 3 Edge at two billion, an embodiment-specialised physical-AI reasoner. The Qwen ladder exists so scale can be varied within a single model family, and the two-billion end matters because it is the scale a drone can actually carry. Serving the Cosmos model at a 32k context takes 10.5 gigabytes and about 0.44 seconds per decode, which the authors note is a ceiling no aircraft carries, so the real onboard budget is tighter still. The single-drone suite runs 80 episodes across two Unreal maps in AirSim, and the commanding suite adds 20 episodes at four drones.

The findings are a paradox. The structurally simplest cell, flying to a target visible from the first frame, is not solved: the best model, Gemini 3.7 Flash, succeeds in 13 of 20 episodes, or 65 percent. Withholding the target from the first frame drops that model to 40 percent, and no model exceeds 40 percent there. But the failure is not where the success rates suggest. The paper decomposes approaching into three nested quantities: how often the drone came within the radius, how often it declared arrival, and how often it did both. For frontier models the three track each other, which is the pattern you expect when navigation is the constraint. For the small open models they diverge, and Qwen 3.5 at nine billion is the sharpest case. It passes within the radius in 90 percent of episodes, more often than any frontier model, yet converts only 35 percent, declaring on average at 0.63 of the start distance. It reaches the target and then misuses the action that would end the episode. The two-billion model fails the other way: it declares in 25 percent of episodes at 1.28 of the start distance, announcing arrival without having closed any of it, and never succeeds. Cosmos 3 Edge flies, entering the radius in 25 percent of episodes, but never declares once. The spatial perception holds up; the action protocol does not.

The commanding setting amplifies the divide. Here one context flies four drones toward look-alike candidates that can only be told apart from close range, and the fleet verdict pools the declarations. Gemini 3.7 Flash succeeds in 80 percent of commanding episodes, while GPT-5, within one episode of the best single-drone approacher, succeeds in only 20 percent, and Claude Opus 5 in none. The failure mode is specific to this setting: small models treat the four views as one. Qwen 3.5 at nine billion emits an identical point for all four drones in 70 percent of cases, and the 27 billion model does it in 58 percent, one answer pasted into four different views, so at most one of the four commands can be grounded. The one-drone ordering does not predict the commanding ordering, which is the point the benchmark is built to expose.

The value of this work is diagnostic rather than a leaderboard. The cleanest contribution is methodological: by making the model the only variable and anchoring success on the model's own declaration, it isolates a failure that prior systems, which decide termination outside the model, could not even measure. That is a real gap in the literature, and the decomposition of navigation from protocol is exactly the kind of result that changes what you build next. The caveats are honest about scale. The commanding suite is 20 episodes, so the success rate moves in five-point steps, and the authors say so. The depth the model emits is its own uncalibrated estimate, not a sensor reading, so go commands ride on a step-size guess. And everything runs in AirSim on Unreal maps, which is fine for a benchmark but means the declaration-discipline finding has not been stress-tested against real motion, wind, or sensor latency.

The core finding, that small models reach the target and then fail to correctly emit the terminating action, is well supported by the within-family Qwen ladder and the oracle-versus-success gap, and it generalises the lesson that grounding looks easy only because it is the part these models already do. As a benchmark it is rigorous and reproducible, but as a measure of deployable edge autonomy it is still a step removed from the aircraft, and the 20-episode commanding cell will not survive a re-run untouched.
