Look Only When Needed, abbreviated LOWN, from Donghyeon Cho, Joowon Kim, and Seungho Shin at KAIST, with Eunho Yang also at KAIST, is a decoding paper for diffusion vision-language models, and its premise is a measurement that most people would guess but few would have actually taken. Diffusion language models generate text by repeatedly unmasking tokens over a series of denoising steps, and a diffusion vision-language model conditions every one of those steps on the image. On the benchmarks they study, visual tokens make up 89 to 97 percent of the input sequence, which means the model is spending most of its decoding compute re-reading the same, unchanging image, over and over, at every step. LOWN measures how often that re-reading actually changes the output, and then skips it when it does not.

The measurement is a paired comparison. Along the model's standard decoding trajectory, the authors run the full forward pass, with the image, and a text-only forward pass, with the visual tokens removed, at every step, and they record both sets of top-one predictions without letting the text-only pass affect the trajectory. Two findings come out of it. First, at 83.4 percent of the positions the model actually unmasks, the full and the text-only forward give the same top-one token. The image is rarely changing the decision. Second, the share of steps on which the image changes at least one unmasked decision varies enormously across tasks. It is 7.9 percent on MMBench and 43.8 percent on DocVQA, 22.8 percent on average. Within a trajectory, the image-dependent steps cluster early and then die out, on eight of the ten benchmarks. A fixed schedule, skip the image every other step, or after the first third, cannot capture this, which is the whole motivation for making the decision per step and per input.

The method turns the measurement into a test. Whenever LOWN runs a full forward pass, it keeps the predictions and confidences on the still-masked positions, and it calls that the anchor. At each subsequent step it runs the cheap text-only forward, orders the masked positions by decreasing anchor confidence, and scans down the list. A token is certified when the text-only prediction agrees with the anchor. Agreement does two jobs at once. It approximates the condition that the image is not needed for this token, and it checks that the anchor is still valid now that the response state has changed since the anchor was computed. The scan stops at the first disagreement, and the certified prefix is what the step can use. If that prefix has at least the number of tokens the baseline would unmask, the step skips the full forward entirely and unmasks the entire certified prefix, which is how LOWN can advance more than the baseline in a single step. If the prefix is too short, the step runs a full forward, refreshes the anchor, and unmasks tokens in disagreement-first order, so the positions where the text-only prediction and the full prediction differ get decided with the image. No training, no learned threshold, no task-specific constant. The acceptance length is set by the input and the step.

The error analysis is the part that makes this more than a clever skip. The certificate is checked against an anchor that ages as the response changes, and the paper measures the cost. A fresh full forward overturns 4.1 to 11.0 percent of the tokens that were certified against an anchor one step old, and in LOWN's own runs, a fresh full forward overturns 2.2 to 4.7 percent of the tokens unmasked on text-only steps. And because every full forward replaces the entire anchor, the worst-case staleness is bounded by the structure of the loop rather than drifting. That is why the method can be aggressive, it knows exactly what the failure rate of the skip is, and it is small.

The results are on ten benchmarks spanning multiple-choice, document and chart understanding, math, and open-ended captioning, on LLaDA-V as the primary backbone. LOWN retains 99.6 percent of the baseline score at 35.3 percent of the decoding FLOPs on average, and it scores highest on eight of the ten benchmarks against accelerated methods given at least its FLOPs budget. The per-benchmark relative cost ranges from about 0.3 to 1.0 times the baseline, which tracks the image share of each task's input: the captioning-heavy benchmarks sit at the low end and the document ones at the high end, exactly as the step-level analysis predicted. The composition result is the one I would actually deploy on. LOWN is orthogonal to visual token pruning, which cuts how many visual tokens a forward processes, because LOWN cuts how often a forward processes them at all. The paper composes LOWN with two public pruners, ZOO-Prune and RedVTP, and the combined system cuts the partner's FLOPs by 1.8 to 2.5 times, and with a key-value cache the pair decodes 5.6 to 7.0 times faster than the unmodified model. The same certificate rule transfers to two other diffusion VLMs, LaViDa and VidLaDA, so it is not an artifact of one architecture.

Where the skepticism lives. The whole method is a property of a specific decoding regime, masked diffusion language models that re-read the full multimodal sequence every step. If the base decoding changes, for instance if someone makes the image representation cheaper or moves to a different unmasking schedule, the measured redundancy, 83.4 percent, could move with it, and the paper does not test LOWN against a moving baseline. The 99.6 percent retention is an average over ten tasks, and on the document-heavy end, where the image share of the input is largest and the image-dependent steps reach 43.8 percent, the method is necessarily doing more full forwards, so the speedup is smaller exactly where the task is hardest. And the certificate, for all its clean analysis, is a heuristic agreement test, not a bound, and the paper reports its empirical overturn rate rather than proving it cannot be exceeded on adversarial inputs.

The cost model is worth stating because it is honest. A text-only forward costs 3 to 10 percent of a full forward, so a step that skips the image is cheap but not free, and the relative FLOPs break down into the fraction of steps that run full forwards plus the text-only overhead. The method's speedup is real, but it is bounded by how much of the sequence is image, and the paper reports the per-benchmark numbers, which is the right way to present it.

The practical takeaway is straightforward. If you are serving a diffusion VLM, and specifically LLaDA-V or one of its derivatives, this is a drop-in decoding change with no training and no threshold to tune, and it composes with the pruning and caching tricks you are probably already using. The certificate also gives you a free diagnostic: the fraction of steps where the image actually matters is a per-task number you did not have before, and it is exactly the number you would want when deciding which workloads are worth a bigger, image-heavy model and which are not. If you are not in that regime, it is still a useful measurement technique. The paired full-versus-text-only comparison is a cheap way to find out how much of your multimodal inference is redundant, and the answer might surprise you.

My verdict is that this is a clean, well-motivated inference paper whose central claim, that most decoding steps in a diffusion VLM do not need the image, is established with a real measurement rather than an assumption, and whose method, the certificate, is simple enough to implement in an afternoon. The results are strong within their regime, the composition story is the most practically useful part, and the main caveat is that everything is scoped to one decoding paradigm.
