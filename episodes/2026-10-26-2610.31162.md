HiSTEVE 1.0: Harnessing Human-Interface VLA Agents for Open-World Games, by Zecong Tang, Zian Guan, and Yuncheng Guo, from the University of Hong Kong, Zhejiang University, Fudan University, and LIGHTSPEED.

The goal is a Minecraft agent that plays like a human player: first-person vision in, native keyboard-and-mouse actions out, over long horizons in a procedurally generated open world. The paper's framing of the gap is clean. Existing agents fall into two camps. One camp has native visuomotor control, a learned policy that maps pixels to key and mouse actions, but no long-horizon autonomy, so it executes single atomic tasks and cannot chase a multi-stage goal. The other camp has high-level planning, typically LLM or VLM based, but executes through environment APIs, predefined skills, or functional actions, which means it is not playing the game through the same interface a human uses, and those APIs are unavailable in most games. HiSTEVE's claim is that both timescales have to be integrated in a closed loop, with the low-level policy operating on the native interface only.

The architecture is a two-timescale hierarchy with a capability-aware agent harness at the top. At the slower planning timescale, the harness keeps a structured memory of task progress, with a goal, a progress tracker, and a needs list, and a VLM planner that selects the next atomic subtask, constrained to subtasks the VLA policy is actually capable of. The capability constraint is the point of the word capability-aware: the planner does not propose arbitrary subtasks from a skill library, it proposes from within the measured capability envelope of the execution policy. The hard subproblem at this timescale is completion detection. Terminating a subtask too early leaves it unfinished; terminating too late wastes actions and can undo progress already made. Their answer is a policy-coupled task completion state detector that conditions on the VLA policy's own context, rather than on a separate verifier model looking at the frame. The detector signals a candidate completion; the harness then verifies the task state and decides whether to advance, recover, or replan.

At the faster action timescale, a single VLA policy executes all subtasks with vision-only perception and native keyboard-and-mouse actions. The interesting engineering detail is how they handle the fact that a Minecraft agent's work is heterogeneous. Non-GUI control, moving, looking, mining, attacking, and GUI interaction, reading text, clicking inventory slots, using the creative menu, differ in both visual distribution and in the semantics of the mouse action. In the GUI regime the mouse is a click target; in the first-person regime it is a camera controller. A single policy trained across both regimes suffers interference between them. Their fix is structural: regime-specific RMSNorm branches, separate normalization for GUI and non-GUI tokens, and separate mouse-movement token embeddings for the two regimes. That is a cheap, low-risk architectural trick, and it is the kind of detail that usually explains whether a unified interface policy actually works or silently degrades on one half of the task distribution. The training pipeline is three stages. First, world knowledge injection, giving the VLA grounded knowledge of the game. Second, imitation learning from demonstrations. Third, reinforcement learning with environment feedback, fine-tuned specifically for recovery when execution deviates from the demonstration trajectories, which is where long-horizon agents actually fail in practice, not on the nominal path but on the off-distribution one.

The concrete instantiation is worth stating. The upper-level planner is GPT-4o, and the low-level VLA is initialized from Qwen2.5-VL-7B, which takes the fixed natural-language subtask instruction and the current first-person observation and autoregressively generates the tokenized keyboard-and-mouse action, running at 20 hertz. The completion detector is a smaller model, Qwen2.5-VL-3B, that shares the VLA's image encoder and additionally conditions on the VLA's own policy context, the hidden states of the instruction prefix before the first action token. It also looks back over historical frames sampled at 800, 600, 400, and 200 milliseconds before the query, while the VLA itself only sees the current frame. That asymmetry is deliberate: the detector gets temporal context the policy does not, so it can recognize that a subtask has completed even when the current frame alone is ambiguous. The reinforcement learning stage is notable in its detail. They use clipped GRPO updates over informative branch groups, but they flag that trajectory-level advantages penalize correct prefixes inside failed rollouts, so they construct local preference pairs from successful and failed branches, prioritizing the same rollout origin and matching structured signatures of inventory, GUI state, cursor position, and exploration. That is a thoughtful fix to a real credit-assignment problem in long-horizon RL, and it is the kind of thing that distinguishes a system that recovers from one that just follows the demo.

The results are on two suites. One more design note before the numbers. The harness treats each VLA invocation as bounded: the planner emits a semantic subtask, the VLA executes it for a limited number of steps, and the completion detector decides when the bounded invocation ends. Bounding invocations is what keeps the structured memory from growing without limit and keeps the policy context the completion detector reads from coherent, because it is the context of one subtask, not a whole episode. If the detector ever fails to fire, the harness has a recovery path that re-plans from the structured memory rather than continuing a stale subtask, which is the difference between an agent that stalls and one that recovers. On MineStudio Bench, atomic tasks in four families, mining, killing, crafting, and smelting, HiSTEVE scores strict pass@1 of 93.3, 86.7, 66.7, and 93.3 percent respectively, an overall average of 85.0 percent. Against the strongest baseline, the OpenHA text chain-of-attention variant, the four-family average goes from 65.0 to 85.0, gains of 20.0, 13.4, 26.7, and 20.0 points on the four families. Crafting is the weakest family and gets the largest gain, which they note indicates multi-step GUI manipulation remains harder than first-person mining and combat. On the OpenHA suite, which mixes embodied, GUI, and combat tasks, HiSTEVE gets a 37.4 percent average success rate against 36.8 for the baseline, a 0.6 point gain overall, concentrated in embodied tasks where success rises from 37.0 to 46.0 percent, while GUI and combat sit slightly below the baseline. On long-horizon benchmarks, family-level gains reach 28.6 points on LongBench, with the largest improvements on tasks requiring multi-stage resource production or reuse of information gathered during exploration. They also complete genuinely long tasks, like crafting an iron pickaxe from scratch, which requires ore collection, smelting, and tool assembly in sequence. The key ablation finding is that stronger atomic execution alone does not yield comparable long-horizon gains, which supports the paper's central claim that the harness, the planning side, is what unlocks the long-horizon behavior, not a better low-level policy by itself.

The skeptical read. The 85 percent on MineStudio atomic tasks is strong, and the per-family breakdown is honest about crafting still being weak. But the OpenHA number, 37.4 percent average success, is the number to sit with, because it is the mixed, harder suite, and the gain over the strongest baseline there is 0.6 points, essentially a tie. The large headline gains are on the atomic benchmark, where the baseline is much weaker. The long-horizon improvements are real, but they are reported at the family level and on a different suite, so the comparison is not as clean. The completion detector conditioned on policy context is a sensible design, but the paper does not deeply ablate how sensitive the system is to false positives versus false negatives in that detector, which is exactly the failure mode that determines whether a long-horizon agent stalls or backtracks. The regime-specific normalization trick is worth stealing for any unified GUI-plus-first-person policy, but you would want to verify it on your own task mix. The benchmark setup is also specific: MineStudio has 20 tasks with three trials each, OpenHA reports family-wise episode success across embodied, GUI, and combat, and LongBench is 14 goal-only tasks with 49 hidden diagnostic nodes, which means the long-horizon evaluation is instrumented at the sub-goal level, not just the final goal. The overall picture: a well-architected two-timescale system with a genuinely useful harness design and a strong atomic result, but a marginal result on the harder mixed suite, and the long-horizon claims rest on family-level numbers rather than a single clean headline. Trust the architecture and the atomic result. Re-verify the long-horizon and mixed-suite claims before betting a roadmap on them.
