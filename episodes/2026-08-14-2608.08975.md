How Can Rhetoric Reward-Hack AI Reviewers? Dissecting Rhetorical Sensitivity in AI-Based Peer Review, by Ming Li, Chenguang Wang, Xirui Li, Xinyue Zeng, and colleagues at the University of Maryland, Virginia Tech, MBZUAI, and the University of Waterloo. The question is precise: when the reported science is held fixed and only the rhetoric moves, which rhetorical dimensions shift AI reviewer scores, in which direction, and under what conditions. This matters to anyone wiring LLMs into review pipelines, because authors already use LLMs to revise manuscripts and venues are experimenting with LLM reviewers, and the two uses meet in one pipeline. Prior work showed presentation-only edits can raise AI review scores; this paper maps the structure of that vulnerability instead of just demonstrating it exists.

The experimental machine is substantial. From OpenReview, the authors sampled 120 anonymized ICLR 2026 submissions, twenty from each of six human-rating bands spanning one to eight point five, matched them to public arXiv LaTeX sources using token-based cosine similarity and five-gram Jaccard similarity to pick the right version, ran an agentic anonymization pass removing author names, affiliations, acknowledgments, status statements, and identity-bearing links with manual verification of both source and compiled PDF, and treated each verified project as a common baseline. Two rewriting agents, GPT-5.5 through Codex CLI and Opus 4.8 through Claude Code, then applied six prespecified rhetorical dimensions, each in opposing directions: novelty stance, assertive versus cautious claim framing; scope framing, broader versus narrower generalization claims; evidence framing, greater versus lesser emphasis on reported comparisons; contribution salience, foregrounded versus integrated contributions; technical register, formal versus plain expression; and linguistic complexity, sophisticated versus simpler vocabulary and syntax. The rewrite scope is deliberately maximal: agents may revise wording, organization, emphasis, captions, tables, and the interpretation of reported results across the whole manuscript, under soft constraints preserving methods, settings, reported values, evidence boundaries, and findings, enforced by programmatic checks on structural anchors like citation keys, labels, graphics paths, math environments, and bibliography files, plus compilation; detected violations go back to the agent for repair and unresolved ones get discarded. That produces twelve variants per paper per rewriter, 2,880 single-dimension rewrites, plus 240 joint rewrites applying all six positive directions at once at paragraph level rather than as six sequential edits, 480 recursive rewrites through three rounds where each round rewrites the previous round's output, and 480 reviewer-guided rewrites, for a 4,200-manuscript corpus costing $29,165.89 in direct API spend, of which single-dimension rewrites account for about nineteen and a half thousand dollars. Five blinded AI reviewers, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, Qwen 3.5 Flash, GPT-5 mini, GPT-5.5, and Claude Sonnet 5, scored compiled PDFs under standard and strict prompts aligned with ICLR guidelines, the strict prompt demanding concrete evidence for high ratings and instructing reviewers not to reward presentation unless it changes scientific assessment, yielding 42,396 valid review records analyzed as paired comparisons against the prompt-matched original with 95 percent bootstrap intervals over five thousand paper-level resamples.

The headline result is that sensitivity concentrates. Evidence framing and novelty stance produce the largest positive-negative contrasts in overall assessment, with scope framing a weaker second tier driven mostly by penalties under narrow framing rather than gains under broad framing; contribution salience, technical register, and lexical complexity barely move scores. Pooled across conditions, positive evidence framing averages plus 0.289 overall assessment against minus 0.303 for negative, novelty plus 0.187 against minus 0.353, while lexical complexity sits near zero at plus 0.017 against minus 0.006. In weak-accept probability, defined as the share of evaluations rating a paper six or higher on the ten-point scale, positive-negative contrasts reach 13 percentage points for evidence framing, 12 for novelty stance, and 9 for scope framing. The secondary rubric scores show the same selectivity from the other side: novelty and evidence contrasts land mainly in contribution and soundness rather than presentation, and unfavorable scope framing mostly lowers the contribution score, which suggests reviewers are translating rhetorical confidence directly into merit judgments. One number deserves special attention from any venue administrator: Sonnet 5 systematically marks everything down, averaging around minus 0.2 across all dimensions and directions, meaning roughly a fifth of a point of raw score movement in this study is reviewer identity, not text. The hierarchy holds across human quality bands, each dimension showing its intended ordering for more than one hundred of the 120 manuscripts, so stronger papers are not more resistant, but movement depends strongly on the AI reviewer's starting score: papers initially scored one to three rise up to plus 1.05 under positive evidence rewrites, papers initially at eight to ten fall to minus 0.19, a regression-to-the-mean pattern the authors flag as descriptive rather than causal.

The workflow results undercut the obvious exploitation strategy. Joint rewriting of all six positive dimensions is heavily rewriter-dependent: Opus 4.8 gains plus 0.289 standard and plus 0.463 strict on average, while GPT-5.5 manages only plus 0.021 and plus 0.045, and those near-zero means hide heterogeneous reviewer responses rather than invariance to rewriting; against the paper-matched mean of its own six positive single-dimension rewrites, the Opus 4.8 joint rewrite adds plus 0.160 while GPT-5.5 falls minus 0.074 below it, so combining favorable objectives does not reliably outperform applying them individually. Reviewer-guided rewriting, where the rewriter reads a validated review of an intermediate joint draft and revises accordingly, preserving stated strengths and addressing weaknesses through manuscript changes, improves over one pass only for Opus 4.8, moving standard gains from plus 0.289 to plus 0.396, but it never beats an unguided second pass at the same depth: guided-minus-unguided contrasts sit between minus 0.008 and minus 0.067 across all four rewriter-prompt averages. Recursive rewriting shows diminishing returns concentrated in round two, moving Opus 4.8's strict gain from plus 0.463 to plus 0.624 with round three adding little, while GPT-5.5 stays nearly flat at plus 0.080 standard and plus 0.153 strict by round three, and individual reviewer trajectories are uneven, some flattening or partially reversing earlier gains. Joint-rewrite effects also track starting score more than true quality, declining from plus 1.42 for papers initially scored one to three down to minus 0.88 for those initially at eight to ten. The clean decomposition across section six: reviewers generally prefer the same rhetorical directions while disagreeing on whether those directions raise or lower absolute scores relative to the original; the rewriter mainly sets the separation between opposing variants; and protocol changes mostly rescale. On that last point, the strict prompt lowers mean overall assessment from 6.296 to 4.934, a drop of 1.36 points hitting 95 percent of papers, while paper rankings stay nearly fixed with Spearman correlation 0.862, and rewrite sensitivity itself barely budges, cross-protocol correlation of effects at just 0.081, with Qwen becoming less positive, GPT-5 mini more positive, and Sonnet staying negative under strict prompting. Similar OA changes also co-move with different rubric dimensions per reviewer, one aligning them mainly with contribution, another splitting between contribution and soundness, which limits how much rubric-level explanations can be trusted. Stricter prompting buys severity, not robustness.

Skeptical read. The corpus is one venue, one year, papers with recoverable LaTeX sources, so generalization beyond ICLR-style machine learning reviewing is unverified. Some review records went missing, apparently non-randomly, because certain topics trip model safeguards, which could bias specific cells. The six dimensions are not orthogonal, so each full-paper intervention is a bundle of linguistic changes, not a coefficient on one feature. Most configurations use a single review per manuscript, and the authors themselves warn the framework is dual use and should not be pointed at live review processes. What would I trust: the dimension ordering, which replicates across two rewriters, five reviewers, two protocols, and multiple quality strata, and the finding that no elaborate workflow reliably amplifies the effect. What would I re-verify before relying on it: whether your own reviewer model behaves like Sonnet 5, uniformly negative, or like Qwen 3.5 Flash, wide-variance, since mitigation design differs sharply between those regimes, and whether strict rubrics change anything material given they demonstrably do not here. Bottom line: rhetorical reward hacking of AI reviewers is real but selective and configuration-dependent, concentrated in how evidence and novelty are framed rather than in prose polish. If you deploy LLM reviewing, audit for content-preserving sensitivity per model before trusting aggregate agreement numbers.
