What Does Privileged Information Add to On-Policy Self-Distillation? The first three authors are XiuYu Zhang, Wei Chow, and Junfeng Fang, from the National University of Singapore, with the remaining authors Zhenkai Liang and Tat-Seng Chua also at the National University of Singapore.

The paper asks a deceptively simple question about on-policy self-distillation, a training recipe in which a language model learns from a frozen copy of itself that can see extra information, like the answer to a problem or a worked solution. Giving the teacher model this privileged information seems obviously useful, the teacher can now point the student toward the right answer. But the paper's question is how much of the student's improvement actually comes from that privileged information, and how much comes from the distillation process itself, the simple act of a student being trained to match a teacher that scores its own rollouts. To separate the two, the authors need a design where the answer information is held fixed while everything else varies, and that design is the main contribution.

The construction is a reusable suite called AMPLE-MATH, which stands for Answer-Matched Privileged Levels of Explanation. It is built from OpenThoughts, and it contains 5,319 mathematical problems, each with six reasoning views that all share the same verified answer but differ in how much reasoning is exposed. The six views range from answer only, thirteen tokens on average, up through a gist at 76 tokens, key points at 284, a clean solution at 526, a summary at 841, and the full trace at 4,916 tokens. Because every view carries the same verified answer, the answer information is held constant across conditions, and what changes is the reasoning representation. Each view is then compared against a reference-free control, a distillation run where the teacher sees no privileged information at all, under the same training configuration. The setup is a thinking-enabled teacher supervising direct-response rollouts from the student, and the student is evaluated with thinking enabled. The two model families studied are Qwen3 at 1.7 billion parameters and SmolLM3 at 3 billion.

The central finding is that much of the improvement is already present without any reference. In Qwen3-1.7B, reference-free distillation, where the teacher sees nothing privileged, accounts for a large share of the student's improvement, both on in-domain problems and on external benchmarks, when evaluated with thinking enabled. The evidence for an additional benefit from the privileged information is modest in Qwen, and where it appears it is strongest for the clean solution view. In SmolLM3-3B, the full trace view adds two percentage points at step fifty beyond reference-free training. Two percentage points is a small number, and the paper is careful to say so. But there is a more important and stranger result. The benefit depends on what the student is trained to produce. At the same checkpoint, if you replace the short direct-response rollouts with long thinking-enabled rollouts, the gains from the privileged information turn into losses, in both model families, while the problems, the references, and the evaluation all stay fixed. The same reference helps under one student rollout regime and hurts under another.

That interaction is where the paper earns its attention. The authors dig into what the teacher's scores actually do on the student's responses, and they find two things. First, the correctness alignment, whether the teacher's likelihood shifts move the student toward correct or incorrect responses, changes depending on which responses are being scored. Second, in both rollout regimes, the teacher's scores put negative pressure on the sampled correction markers, the tokens where the student indicates it is reconsidering or correcting itself. Relaxing the loss at those markers barely changes how often the student uses them, which suggests the suppression is not the main driver. An apparent benefit from broader supervision mostly disappears once the checkpoints are matched, which is a useful reminder that a lot of the variation in these comparisons is just training dynamics rather than a real effect of the reference.

What would I trust? The experimental design, which is the real contribution here. Holding the verified answer fixed across six reasoning representations and comparing each against a matched reference-free control is exactly the right way to ask what the privileged information adds, and the AMPLE-MATH suite is reusable by other groups, which gives the result durability beyond this one paper. The finding that reference-free distillation captures much of the gain is a genuinely useful prior for anyone deciding whether to build a privileged-teacher pipeline, because it means the added complexity of giving the teacher the answer is not obviously buying much in Qwen. The rollout-regime interaction, where the same reference helps direct-response rollouts and hurts thinking-enabled rollouts, is the kind of result that would be hard to guess in advance, and it points at a real mechanism: what the reference contributes depends on the student responses it helps the teacher supervise.

What would I re-verify before building on it? First, the two-model scope. The conclusion that privileged information adds modest value is drawn from Qwen3-1.7B and SmolLM3-3B, two small models, and the effect sizes are small, with the strongest clean result being two percentage points in one family at one checkpoint. I would want to see whether the conclusion holds at the scale where these distillation recipes are actually deployed, because the teacher-student asymmetry, a thinking-enabled teacher scoring non-thinking prefixes, may behave differently when the student is large enough to already do most of the reasoning. Second, the mathematical domain. The construction relies on verified answers and a verifier to score correctness, which is cleanest in math. In domains without a verifiable answer, the whole comparison, privileged versus reference-free, becomes harder to run, and the small effect sizes in math suggest the domain may matter a lot. Third, the six views are all derived from the same source, OpenThoughts, so the quality of the reasoning representations is bounded by that corpus, and I would want to see whether a higher-quality clean solution, from a stronger source, changes where the benefit sits in the view ladder.

One more point on the practical implication. If reference-free distillation captures most of the gain, the simplest pipeline, no privileged information, no reference pipeline to build and serve, may be the right default, and the privileged-information machinery should be justified by a measured delta, not by intuition. That is a conservative and defensible position, and it is what the paper's data supports for these two model families.

The AMPLE-MATH construction also has a methodological point that extends beyond this paper. The six-view ladder, answer only through full trace, with a matched reference-free control, is a template for asking what any added supervision signal contributes, not just privileged reasoning. The design principle is to hold the thing you think is doing the work, in this case the verified answer, constant, and vary only the representation you are trying to credit. Most comparisons in this area conflate the two, giving a teacher both the answer and the explanation and attributing all of the gain to the package, when the answer alone may carry most of it. The ladder plus the matched control is the fix, and it is a reusable instrument for anyone evaluating a new supervision signal against what the baseline already provides. The cost of doing it right is the construction work: 5,319 problems, each with six verified views sharing one answer, is a substantial curation effort, and the fact that the authors released it as a reusable suite is what makes the template worth adopting rather than reimplementing.

Bottom line: AMPLE-MATH is a clean, reusable design for isolating what privileged information adds to on-policy self-distillation, and its central finding is that reference-free distillation already captures much of the student's improvement, with the added benefit of the privileged reference being modest, at most two percentage points in the strongest case, and dependent on the student's rollout regime. The practical upshot is that the complexity of a privileged teacher is not obviously worth it in these settings, and the rollout-regime interaction is the mechanism worth understanding before adding one.
