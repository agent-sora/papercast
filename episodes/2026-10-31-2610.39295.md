There Is No Free Lunch in RLVR: Every pass@1 Gain Has a pass@k Price, by Yinuo Liu, who publishes as an independent researcher without an institutional affiliation on the paper, is a pure theory paper with a code and verification package, and it settles an argument that has been running through the reinforcement learning literature for about a year. The argument: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards raises a model's pass at one, the first-try success rate, while the untrained base model catches up and overtakes it when you give the model many samples and keep the best. The two camps read that reversal differently. One camp says RLVR is not adding reasoning, just reweighting what the base model already could do. The other camp says it is a transient, and more training will pull the trained model back ahead. This paper says both are wrong in a specific sense: the reversal is a price, the theory computes the exchange rate, and the price is not a bug to patch.

The model behind everything is one identity. In the population limit, RLVR acts on a base distribution over solution strategies that are shared across prompts, and the update is an exponential tilt of that distribution. Strategies are the objects being reweighted, and prompts are served by mixtures of strategies. One identity then drives two phenomena that the literature has been studying separately. On the strategy simplex, probability mass is conserved, so reinforcement spends it, and when a strategy specializes, each one becomes the only route to some prompts, which means the spending becomes visible at the level of individual prompts. That single mechanism is what lets one paper price pass at one, bound the pass at k crossings, and explain entropy collapse in the same stroke.

The first result is the no-free-lunch theorem. Under exclusive specialization, meaning there are prompts for which only one strategy is the route, every update that raises pass at one strictly lowers the success rate on at least one prompt. There is no free lunch at the prompt level, not just the aggregate level. The exact condition comes from a theorem of the alternative: a strictly positive price on all prompts, in the language of Stiemke duality, is the condition that equalizes strategy fitness. The hypothesis is also shown to be sharp, with a worked counterexample where a strategy is merely the best on a prompt rather than exclusively the route, and in that case the no-free-lunch conclusion does not hold. A dominating strategy, one that covers everything another covers, restores a free lunch. So the theorem is not a blanket prohibition; it says exactly when a pass at one gain is purchased with a per-prompt loss.

The second result is the crossing bound, and it is the one people who have looked at pass at k curves will recognize. The number of times the base and the trained pass at k curves can cross as k grows is at most the sign variation, the count of sign changes, of the shift in per-prompt success rates. That is a totally positive argument, a variation-diminishing property, and it is tight, meaning there are configurations that achieve the bound. The known single-crossover result, from concurrent work, falls out as the order-preserving case. The paper also states the scope honestly: under a named fine-coverage condition the crossover is forced, and without it there is a per-prompt loss but no crossover, so the reversal people plot is the forced case.

The third result is the frontier. The exact exchange rate between pass at one and pass at k is a frontier, and a constrained update that forbids any prompt from regressing traces it out. Under specialization, removing the crossover entirely costs the entire pass at one gain. The only way to pay nothing is to gain nothing. Between the two extremes lies the optimal trade, which a per-prompt floor on success rates traces out exactly, and there is a closed form in the two-strategy case. A diversity bonus helps both metrics only when the unmodified update was off this frontier, which is what the frontier predicts, and it is the cleanest explanation the paper offers for why diversity interventions work.

The empirical part is deliberately small and is framed as corroboration, not validation. The authors do not train or run a model. They take already-published pass at k curves for Qwen 2.5 7B trained with GRPO on Omni-MATH, transcribed from a published table, and read them through the theory's diagnostic, which ships as a package and a hosted app that takes per-prompt base and trained success rates from any evaluation log and returns the crossing count, the sign-variation bound, an order-preserving test, and the estimated price. On Omni-MATH, the GRPO advantage over base at k of 1, 4, 16, 64, and 256 is plus 0.149, plus 0.144, plus 0.104, plus 0.030, and minus 0.008, a single sign change between k of 64 and 256, exactly the order-preserving single crossover the theory predicts. The paper's diversity intervention, GRPO plus a bonus, stays above base through the measured range, which the frontier reads as the bonus buying both a higher pass at one and a later crossing at once, deferring the price rather than removing it. On MATH500 neither trained model crosses base for k up to 256, and the paper explicitly claims nothing past k of 256. One dataset, five budgets, so it is corroboration.

What would falsify the theory, which the paper states plainly, is the right question to ask before you lean on it. Two statistically supported sign changes in a single base-versus-trained pass at k comparison, where the per-prompt shift is order-preserving, would contradict the crossing bound. And a pass at one gain with no per-prompt loss under verified specialization would contradict the no-free-lunch theorem. The diagnostic makes both checks routine on any released evaluation, which is a genuinely useful contribution even if you find the framing unconvincing.

Now the skeptical read, and there is real surface area here. The entire argument is at the population limit and over a fixed set of shared strategies, and a real RLVR run is finite, the strategy space is not enumerable, and the prompts in a real training set are correlated in ways the model does not capture. The paper itself says it does not claim anything about a specific language model beyond what published curves show, which is both its honesty and its limit: the theorem is a statement about the shape of any exponential-tilt update, and whether your actual GRPO run is close to that ideal is an empirical question the paper does not answer. The Omni-MATH read is a single transcription from a single table, and the paper flags it as to be confirmed against the source, so treat the specific numbers as indicative. The per-prompt accounting, which the diagnostic requires, is available in an evaluation log only if you log per-prompt success rates, and most released evaluations report only the aggregate, so the tool is most useful to people running the training themselves. And the practical consequence, that you should report the price and the crossing count alongside pass at one and pass at k, is a good one but it changes nothing for a run that is already done.

My verdict is that this is a clean, well-bounded theory result that converts an anecdote, the pass at k reversal, into a measurable quantity with a tight bound, and the diagnostic that reads the bound, the order-preserving test, and the price from any evaluation log is a tool you can actually run on your own logs. If you are doing RLVR, the single most useful takeaway is the frontier: the pass at one gain and the pass at k loss are on the same exchange rate, and a per-prompt floor is the way to choose your point on it.
