"Scaling to tens of thousands of test-time iterations with loop-native attention residuals," by Pengxiang Li, Dilxat Muhtar, and Di He, from the Hong Kong Polytechnic University, the Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and the ELLIS Institute Tübingen, the Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems, and the Tübingen AI Center. This is squarely in the reasoning-model topology space, so it is in scope on the standing rule. The claim is a specific one: looped Transformers, the family of models that repeatedly apply a shared block to get more depth without more parameters, need their own kind of residual connection, because the way they currently connect iterations is actively hurting them as the number of iterations grows.

Let me set up the problem, because the diagnosis is the interesting part. A looped Transformer repeatedly applies a shared block, and the most common way to connect consecutive iterations is what they call the carry-last rule: the final output of one loop is fed directly into the next. The problem is that this makes the unrolled computation equivalent to a deep network with shared weights, which reintroduces the familiar curse of depth. And worse, carry-last has a specific failure mode they document. Increasing loop iterations can actually reduce reasoning accuracy, because noisy state updates overwrite correct intermediate deductions and can even undo a completed solution. Once an error arises in an earlier loop, often from long-range propagation through the recurrence, the later loops find it hard to correct, because they are recovering information from an already-degraded representation. They show this concretely on Sudoku-Extreme: a carry-last baseline, which they call TRM, reaches a peak exact accuracy of 62.2 percent at 468 effective layers and then declines to 58.1 percent at 1,872 layers. They also show that TRM turns a large fraction of its own correct intermediate predictions into incorrect values in later updates, and that puzzles it has already solved start getting un-solved again.

Their fix is called InfiLoop, a loop-native residual connection. Instead of passing the newest candidate straight through, they replace the carry-last rule with learned attention over the recurrent outputs. A shared pseudo-query scores each candidate when it is produced, and a learned exponential decay discounts earlier contributions. The resulting normalized aggregation forms the input to the next iteration. The mathematically important property is that the shared scoring and the geometric decay admit an exact streaming recurrence: you need only a weighted-state vector and a normalization scalar per token, and the persistent memory is constant, independent of the loop count. That is a real reduction compared with a full attention-residuals rule that would need to store and attend over every preceding representation, growing linearly in memory. They show the recurrence is equivalent to an adaptive residual update: the new state is the old state plus a step-size times the difference between the new candidate and the old state, where the step-size compares the candidate's content weight against the retained historical mass. So the model can suppress low-scoring proposals and accept high-scoring ones, and because the weights are positive and sum to one, the aggregated state stays inside the convex hull of the candidates, so the aggregation does not amplify the norm. They also show the framework applies to both standard looped Transformers and nested recurrent reasoners like TRM and HRM, by applying the same rule independently to each recurrent axis, adding only a scorer-decay pair and two accumulators per axis while keeping the aggregation state independent of the loop count.

Now the results. The model is a compact 7 million parameter recursive reasoner with two Transformer layers per shared block, hidden dimension 512. On Sudoku-Extreme it reaches 97.9 percent exact accuracy, compared with 94.2 percent for the best damping baseline FPRM and 74.7 percent for the carry-last TRM. On Maze-Hard it is 87.4 percent. On ARC-AGI-1 it reaches 49.1 percent pass at 2, and on the much harder ARC-AGI-2 it reaches 13.6 percent pass at 2, nearly doubling TRM at 7.8 percent and beating FPRM at 6.2 percent. The scaling result is the headline. Under test-time depth scaling on Sudoku, InfiLoop reaches 90.9 percent at 1,872 layer evaluations, which exceeds FPRM's 90.1 percent at 3,744 layer evaluations, so it matches a competitor with half the compute. At 24,960 layer evaluations, InfiLoop is at 92.74 percent, FPRM at 91.63 percent, and TRM at 83.65 percent. The key contrast is that TRM's accuracy that InfiLoop reaches at 936 layers is never matched by TRM even at 24,960 layers, more than 26 times deeper. In other words, for the carry-last model, adding depth past a point actively hurts, while InfiLoop keeps improving. They also report ablations that pin down the design. Applying the learned connection to both the inner and outer recurrent axes beats applying it to either axis alone across every budget, reaching 89.7 percent at the largest budget against roughly 79 to 80 percent for single-axis. And the decay direction matters a lot: forward decay, where older outputs weigh less, works, while reverse decay, where older outputs weigh more, collapses to about 2 percent, and learning the decay rate from an initialization of 0.55 gives the best result.

There is also an interpretation result I found genuinely interesting. They inspect the learned step-size, the adaptive residual coefficient. If the scorer gave equal weight to every output, the step-size would converge to a fixed value, so any deviation reflects learned content preference. What they see is an emergent self-verification behavior: during unsolved search, the step-size stays suppressed, so the model is cautious and does not let noisy candidate states dominate; when a valid solution appears, the step-size surges and accepts the output that completes the solution; and once solved, the state essentially freezes. They back this up with a perturbation experiment: they inject a perturbation into the recurrent state and unroll, and InfiLoop retains all of its solved puzzles across all tested perturbation scales, while TRM loses 15 to 20 percent of its solutions even without perturbation, and diverges to order-one distance from the unperturbed trajectory within 12 updates.

Let me be honest about the scope. What I would trust is the diagnosis and the mechanism, because the failure mode of carry-last overwriting correct intermediate states is well-documented with specific numbers, and the adaptive-residual interpretation plus the perturbation-stability result are the kind of internal evidence that tells you the mechanism is doing what they say. What I would caveat is the benchmark scope. These are symbolic puzzle and abstract visual transformation tasks, Sudoku, Mazes, and ARC, which is exactly where recursive computation is supposed to shine, and the absolute numbers on ARC-AGI-2, at 13.6 percent, are still low. The model is 7 million parameters, so this is a proof of mechanism rather than a claim about scaling to frontier reasoning. The question I would want answered is whether this carry-last-bottleneck and the learned-attention fix transfer to open-ended reasoning, natural-language chain-of-thought, or larger models, rather than just closed symbolic puzzles with exact-match scoring. That said, the constant-memory exact recurrence is a clean, adoptable contribution that does not depend on any of those caveats.

Here is the bottom line. InfiLoop identifies a real, well-evidenced bottleneck in deep looped Transformers, namely that the carry-last state-replacement rule overwrites correct intermediate deductions and destabilizes solved states, and fixes it with a loop-native attention residual whose exact streaming recurrence keeps memory constant while letting the model suppress noisy updates and lock in solutions. If you are working on looped or recurrent-depth reasoning architectures, the diagnosis, the adaptive-residual interpretation, and the constant-memory recurrence are all worth taking seriously, but treat the benchmark gains as specific to symbolic puzzles until you see the mechanism transfer to open-ended reasoning.
