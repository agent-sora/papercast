TILDE, short for Teacher-in-the-Loop Distillation from Embodied Foundation Models, is from the University of Hong Kong, written by Yuxiang Lu, Yantong Liu, and Jinghua Hou, along with Xueli Sun, Ruihua Han, Zhe Liu, and Hengshuang Zhao.

The setup is a familiar asymmetry in embodied AI. Large-scale action pretraining, the kind that produces embodied foundation models like pi-zero point five and X-VLA, gives manipulation policies strong generalization and closed-loop robustness. But reproducing that pretraining requires large robot data collections and serious compute, and most labs and most robot deployments do not have either. The question the paper tackles: can a small, cheap policy without action pretraining inherit the capabilities of a pretrained foundation-model teacher, without paying the pretraining bill? Their answer is a distillation loop called TILDE, which stands for Teacher-In-the-Loop Distillation, combined with an autonomous data-collection pipeline called AutoDAgger.

The motivation numbers are worth stating plainly. On the standard LIBERO benchmarks, policies with and without large-scale action pretraining perform similarly on the easier suites: pi-zero point five hits 98.8 on LIBERO-Spatial and 97.0 on LIBERO-Object, while its non-pretrained variant gets 98.2 and 97.4. The gap is invisible there. It appears where it matters. On the long-horizon LIBERO-10 suite, the pretrained policy reaches 98.6 and 97.6 while the non-pretrained variants fall to 92.4 and 87.4. Under distribution shift, the LIBERO-Plus background and robot perturbations, the gap widens further. And on the real-world Pick Beverage task, the numbers are 78.4, 74.3, 79.2, 16.7, and 26.7 across the model variants, with the non-pretrained students collapsing to double digits. That is the capability the distillation has to transfer: not in-distribution competence, which the student already has, but robustness under shift and on long horizons.

TILDE has three supervision sources, and the structure of the gap each one fills is the core of the method. First, demonstration data: the standard offline distillation signal, where the student imitates teacher or human demonstrations. Its limitation is distribution mismatch, the student at execution time visits states the demonstrations never covered, and the teacher's advice there is undefined. Second, on-policy distillation at student-induced states: the student rolls out, and at the states the student actually reaches, the teacher provides corrective action targets. This extends supervision to the student's own state distribution, but it has a hole: when the student fails, the rollout ends, so the system never observes what happens after the failure and how it gets recovered. Third, teacher-executed recovery trajectories: when the student fails, the teacher takes over from the failure state, executes the recovery, and the resulting trajectory becomes training data. This is the signal that carries the robustness. The three sources cover the demonstration distribution, the student's on-policy distribution, and the failure-and-recovery distribution, and the paper's argument is that the third is the one that encodes the generalization the pretraining originally provided.

AutoDAgger automates the expensive part of that third source. In the classic human-in-the-loop version, a human operator watches every student execution, judges when progress has stalled, and takes over with teleoperation to demonstrate the recovery. That is slow, and it caps throughput at human bandwidth. AutoDAgger replaces the human with two automated components. A task monitor, implemented with the Robometer reward model, estimates task progress during the student's rollout and triggers an intervention when progress stalls. Then the teacher policy itself takes over from the current state and executes the recovery, generating the corrective trajectory directly. No human in the loop at all. The validation of the monitor is included in the paper: on LIBERO, the binary success-or-failure prediction from the monitor has precision of 0.907 to 0.988, recall of 0.934 to 1.000, and F1 of 0.920 to 0.994 across the four suites, so the trigger is reliable enough to run unattended. The monitor design has an interesting property worth noting: it is a reward model, not a progress estimator with its own learned state, so its reliability is inherited from how well the reward model captures task completion. On tasks where completion is ambiguous, or where the reward model has been trained on a narrow task family, the trigger quality is the first thing to degrade, and the paper's LIBERO numbers are the best case for the monitor, not a guarantee for your tasks.

The three-supervision-source structure also has a practical ordering. Demonstration data is cheap to collect once, on-policy rollouts cost student inference time, and recovery trajectories cost teacher inference time, which is the most expensive component because the teacher is a large foundation model. The paper does not report the relative data volumes or the training-time breakdown across the three sources, which is the number a practitioner would need to budget for, and its absence is a small gap in the recipe's completeness.

The results come in two parts. On the main distillation experiments with the non-pretrained students, TILDE closes most of the gap to the pretrained policies on the robustness benchmarks, transferring the closed-loop behavior that the student lacked. On the cross-architecture transfer, they distill from the pi-zero point five teacher into SmolVLA, a different architecture without action pretraining, on LIBERO. TILDE lifts the student from 81.2 average to 89.6, with the long-horizon LIBERO-10 suite going from 64.6 to 79.6, a 15-point gain. The offline distillation baseline only reaches 83.5 average, and actually drops on LIBERO-10 to 63.8 versus the student's 64.6, which is a useful negative: pure offline imitation does not transfer the robustness, and in this setting makes the hard suite slightly worse. The cross-architecture result matters because the distillation is purely action-space alignment, no shared feature space, so the teacher and student can be architecturally unrelated.

The AutoDAgger efficiency comparison against the human-in-the-loop baseline, HG-DAgger, is the practical headline. Recovery trajectory collection is more than 2 times faster with automated teacher takeover, which the paper attributes to the teacher generating corrective actions immediately from the current state, with no human reaction and control overhead. Recovery success rates are comparable between the two methods, with the caveat that some failure states are inherently hard to recover from for either. The compounding effect is shown on the Fold Dishcloth task: increasing the AutoDAgger dataset to 60 episodes raises the score to 65.3, matching HG-DAgger with 30 episodes, and with the 2.53 times speedup in recovery time, the autonomous pipeline can collect more recovery data in the same wall-clock budget.

The method's boundary conditions are worth stating explicitly, because they determine where TILDE applies. The teacher must be a single policy that can be queried at arbitrary states, so a teacher that is itself an ensemble or a multi-stage pipeline does not fit the formulation directly. The student must be able to roll out in the same state space as the teacher, which holds within a single embodiment but not across embodiments. And the reward model that drives the monitor must be available and calibrated for the target tasks, which is a nontrivial prerequisite that the paper treats as given. Within those conditions, the three-source supervision design is a clean decomposition, and the recovery-trajectory signal is the component that most existing distillation setups are missing.

The skeptical read: the real-world evaluation is a single task, Pick Beverage, with a small number of episodes, so the real-robot claims rest on thin ground and deserve replication before you plan a deployment around them. The Robometer-based monitor is evaluated on LIBERO, which is a simulation suite with clean task boundaries; real manipulation tasks have ambiguous progress signals, and the trigger reliability on messy real data is the unproven part of the autonomy claim. The teacher has to be a large pretrained foundation model, which means the method inherits the availability constraint it is trying to work around, you still need access to pi-zero point five or an equivalent, and the licensing and compute situation for those teachers varies. The 15-point LIBERO-10 gain is large and encouraging, but it is one suite with one student architecture, and the generalization across task families is not established. If you have access to a strong EFM teacher and a small student you need to deploy on hardware without the teacher's compute, TILDE is a well-motivated recipe, and the action-space-only alignment makes it unusually easy to wire into an existing student. The parts to re-verify before adoption are the monitor's trigger quality on your own tasks and the real-world transfer beyond the single demonstration task.
