FinCosmos: An Open-World Evaluation Paradigm for Agents in Finance, by Yupeng Cao, Xueqing Peng, and Lingfei Qian, from The Fin AI, with collaborators at Stevens Institute of Technology, MBZUAI, the University of Manchester, and the University of Montreal, is an evaluation framework for financial agents that keeps all three things real financial work keeps open: the user's intent, the data, and the timeline. The headline result is that across four coding agents and one tool-less model over 150 sessions on ten tasks, not a single session is accepted, and every session in which the simulated investor says he is satisfied still fails a programmatic check.

The motivation is the silent failure. An agent working on the open web for a user can produce a report that looks exactly as convincing as a correct one while getting the wrong strategy, using data it never actually verified, or running a backtest that peeks into the future. The authors argue that every existing financial benchmark closes the real world somewhere: it fixes the task statement, hands over curated data, or enforces the time cutoff inside its own pipeline. Each closure removes a class of silent failure instead of measuring it, so none of them tests what an agent actually delivers when intent, data, and time are all open. That is the gap FinCosmos is designed to fill, and the comparison table in the paper is blunt about it: every benchmark they list opens at most one of the three dimensions, and FinCosmos opens all three at once.

The framework has three components. The strategy generator samples a hidden strategy from a compositional grammar over six components of a quantitative trading strategy: stock selection, market timing, portfolio construction, risk control, execution, and transaction cost. Stock selection alone has factor families from fundamentals, value, quality, growth, to technical momentum and short-term reversal, to information signals like analyst revisions and insider buying, to biotech event factors like catalyst proximity and FDA outcomes, plus combination rules like equal weighting, information-coefficient weighting, and gradient boosting. Coherence rules exclude combinations that do not make sense, for example risk parity without a covariance estimator. The total strategy space is about 3.8 million distinct strategies before parameter values are drawn, which matters for benchmark longevity: when a released task set saturates or leaks, you draw new ones from the same grammar. The released benchmark freezes the first ten sampled tasks, with seeds from zero to nine, for the public leaderboard.

The user simulator is a language model that plays the user, and here the design detail worth copying is the separation between persona and belief. The persona determines who the user is and how they speak; the belief is the hidden strategy itself, entered into the prompt as plain-language convictions with exact numbers, like "cut to cash if the drawdown reaches 20 percent." A disclosure policy fixes how the belief is revealed: the user states a vague request, answers questions, and discloses details only when the agent asks. So eliciting the request is itself part of what is being tested, and the prompts explicitly forbid evaluation vocabulary, words like "point-in-time" or "cutoff," so the agent cannot game the setup. There are three personas: a retail investor, a generalist analyst, and a biotech specialist.

The agent gets no data. It receives only the channels through which it may reach data, and it must search the open web, call data APIs, write code, and build its own backtest under a fixed per-task data cutoff. Respecting time becomes a property the evaluator checks rather than something the harness enforces. The deliverable is graded on 45 dimensions, combining programmatic verification with checks of strategy fidelity against the hidden specification. Programs check data provenance, point-in-time correctness, and whether the reported metrics can be independently recomputed from the delivered trade log. Fifteen of the dimensions are vetoes: a run is accepted only when the user is satisfied and none of the 15 veto checks fails, so a silent failure structurally cannot pass. That reference-free grading is the part I would take most seriously as an engineering pattern, because it removes the reference-answer bottleneck that blocks most benchmark scaling.

Now the results, and they are severe. They evaluated four general-purpose coding agents plus one model without tools, on the ten frozen tasks, three independent sessions per task, 150 sessions total. No session is accepted. Pre-veto quality scores range from 20.2 to 37.2 out of 100, which means even the best agent fails to get close. The grades are diagnostic in a useful way: of the 120 agent sessions, 25 actually deliver the investor's intended strategy, 15 of those also keep their data within the cutoff, 14 also backtest without look-ahead, and none delivers a trade log that reproduces its own reported returns. On the elicitation side, agents raise only 54 to 64 percent of the strategy details they were not told, and of the details they do raise, they get 66 to 83 percent wrong. The two ends of the workflow are where agents break: pinning down what the user actually wants, and delivering verifiable results.

The paper also defends why each design piece is necessary, which is the part most benchmark papers skip. The investor signed off in 23 percent of sessions, and an LLM judge applying the same vetoes would accept 11 percent of sessions. Yet every one of those sessions fails a program check. So grading by user satisfaction, or by an LLM judge, would count silent failures as successes and would even reorder the ranking of the agents. Human experts attribute 88.33 percent of failed runs to the agent rather than the benchmark, and the LLM judge agrees with human experts at a kappa of 0.87. That expert validation matters, because the whole framework leans on a simulated user, and the known literature says simulator quality shifts success rates a lot depending on which model plays the user.

Finally, the benchmark is meant to be solvable, and they demonstrate that with their feedback loop. With FinCosmos's leak-free diagnostic feedback, an agent with persistent memory learned all ten tasks, none of which it passed on the first attempt. The feedback tells the agent what failed without revealing the hidden strategy, so the learning signal is real but not a cheat. The leaderboard is live at leaderboard.fincosmos.com.co, and the task generator and evaluator are released.

The skeptical assessment. First, the strategy grammar defines the space of what "a financial strategy" even is in this benchmark. Six components with the options they picked is a reasonable coverage of quantitative trading, but it excludes whole categories of work, like macro or credit or derivatives strategies, and the compositional grammar means the benchmark measures how well agents handle quant-style multi-factor work specifically. If you are evaluating an agent for a different kind of financial job, this is not your benchmark. Second, the user simulator is an examiner, not a user, and the paper is honest about the fidelity limits of LLM simulators, but the entire elicitation measurement rides on one model playing three personas. I would want to see the elicitation statistics under a second simulator model before treating the 54 to 64 percent figure as a property of the agents rather than of the simulator. Third, the 45-dimension grader with 15 vetoes is itself a substantial artifact, and the kappa of 0.87 with human experts is reassuring but measured on a limited sample; the veto set is where false negatives would hide, and a veto that fires on a quirk of the delivery format rather than a real violation would understate agents. The programmatic recomputation of metrics from the delivered trade log is the strongest check in the design and the one I would trust most.

Bottom line: FinCosmos is the most honest evaluation design for financial agents I have seen this year, and its zero-acceptance result is a real finding about where agent capability actually ends, not a benchmark calibration failure. If you build financial agents, the reference-free grading with programmatic verification and veto checks is the pattern to steal even if you never use their task generator.
