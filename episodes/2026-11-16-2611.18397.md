The paper is Gamer: A Generalist Vision-Language-Action Foundation Model for Long-Horizon Real-Time Game Agent, by Run Luo, Kuan Zhang, Qi Deng, and the rest of the miHoYo Honkai AI R and D Team. Everything in this episode comes from the paper itself, which runs through a complete training recipe for a screen-to-action model that plays commercial games with nothing but a keyboard and a mouse.

The problem setup is deliberate. Game agents have a long history: prior work matched or beat humans on Atari, Minecraft, and StarCraft. But almost all of those results relied on privileged internal state, a tailored action space, or a dedicated simulator API. The moment you want the agent to run on a real commercial client, none of that is available. You get pixels on a screen and a virtual keyboard and mouse. So the authors define the bar as: the agent must operate exactly like a human player does, perceiving raw pixels and acting through standard device-level inputs. Games are then chosen as the testbed precisely because they are hard and controllable: complex 3D scenes, multi-stage quests, resources to manage, feedback that arrives late, and tasks that can run for hours.

The architecture is a single end-to-end model. At every step it takes the current screen, the task context, a short-term history of recent interactions, a long-term memory store, and a reasoning trace, and it emits native keyboard and mouse actions. There is no intermediate action abstraction; the output space is the literal device events a human would produce. That design choice drives most of the rest of the paper, because the whole training stack has to work with that output space.

Training happens in three stages. The first is continued pre-training on a dataset built from tens of thousands of hours of device-level keyboard and mouse interaction. These trajectories span simulated games and embodied environments, so the model is meant to learn environmental dynamics across both physical and virtual domains. The data comes from several sources: about 2,400 hours of gameplay in Honkai Star Rail alone, operation-level annotations of roughly 261,000 samples, and subgoal-level supervision in which about 37 percent of the operation-level trajectories get intermediate subgoals attached. There is also an embodied and egocentric stream of about 6.3 billion tokens drawn from embodied reasoning and egocentric corpora. The point of this stage is not to teach the model a specific game; it is to make the model fluent in the joint space of what a screen looks like and what a keypress does next.

The second stage is what the authors call sparse-thinking instruction tuning. The idea is that a reasoning model that thinks at full verbosity on every single keypress is wasteful in a real-time setting, where you have milliseconds, not seconds. Sparse thinking trains the model to compress or skip its internal reasoning on routine steps while keeping full reasoning available for genuinely hard decisions. In practice this reduces redundant computation while preserving the reasoning needed for complex choices, and the paper reports it as a meaningful contributor to the real-time operating efficiency later.

The third stage is policy training with a multi-turn reinforcement learning method. The paper calls out the three things that make this hard, and they are all real engineering problems rather than academic ones. First, finite context windows: a game session is longer than the model's context, so the training has to handle truncation and summarization without losing the thread. Second, accumulated rollout errors: in multi-turn interaction, small action errors compound, and naive policy gradients struggle with that credit assignment. Third, sparse rewards: in an open-world game you might not get a terminal reward for a very long time, so the learning signal is thin. Their answer is a stable multi-turn RL recipe designed specifically for these conditions, though the paper is somewhat high-level about the exact objective and the details live in the deployment and scaling sections.

The deployment side gets as much attention as the training, and that is unusual and worth calling out. Real-time interaction introduces a system bottleneck that most agent papers ignore: if inference latency is high, the action executes after the observed game state has changed, and the agent is effectively playing a stale world. Their answer is a cloud-edge inference engine that serves many simulated game environments concurrently, supports unbounded-context execution, and keeps online inference latency low. They also describe a streaming video understanding component with a streaming cache strategy, and they report it directly: on streaming video benchmarks, the streaming engine scores 83.29 on StreamingBench and 62.3 on OVO-Bench, and it cuts query latency from 0.53 seconds to 0.08 seconds relative to the DSCache baseline with the same base model. With a larger 9-billion-parameter base model the same latency falls from 1.56 seconds to 0.16 seconds. Those are concrete numbers and they matter, because latency in a game loop is not a nice-to-have.

Now the results. The paper reports both real-time scores, where the agent plays live, and time-paused scores, where the environment is frozen during inference. The real-time score is the honest number, and the paper reports the difference between the two as a diagnostic: how much quality does real-time latency cost? On their 3D open-world evaluations, the policy achieves an average real-time score of 29.5, and a comparison policy built on the Qwen 3.5 35B Mixture of Experts base reaches 31.5. Both exceed the strongest listed general-purpose multimodal baseline. On one specific benchmark, MCU, the two continued-pre-training policies score 47.7 and 65.3 respectively, which the authors describe as a particularly clear separation. On the 2D side, they report competitive results across a range of conventional game benchmarks, and they run a reduced benchmark of 40 task instances across seven game types where instruction tuning broadens success across distinct minigame mechanics, with non-zero mean success in all seven types.

The scaling analysis is where I would actually go read the paper, because it is the part that separates this from a demo. They run GameScaling, which does block-wise holdout tests over model size, data budget, and game mixture. The honest finding is that the data scaling is strong but the within-mixture fit is weak: a high overall fit score coexists with a within-mixture R-squared of only 0.079. In plain language: the model scales well with more hours of keyboard and mouse data, but within a fixed game, the relationship between data and performance is noisy. That is a fair and useful result, and it tempers the "scaling keyboard and mouse trajectories works" headline.

The ablations also include a comparison of the reasoning stages. On a specific task, Bestial Ferocity, the scores move from 13.9 percent, to 47.9 percent, to 69.1 percent as stages are added, with instruction tuning contributing 37.8 points and the final stage 21.2. So the reasoning stack is doing real work, not just decoration.

The skeptical assessment. First, the evaluation is almost entirely in-house. The 3D results are on the authors' own games, with their own harness, and the commercial-game setting means the exact configuration, difficulty, and starting states are hard to reproduce externally. The time-paused versus real-time delta is a nice diagnostic, but the real-time numbers are what count and they are reported as scores rather than task completion rates in the 3D setting. Second, the RL stage is described at a level that makes it hard to replicate the stability claims; if you are trying to run multi-turn RL on a commercial game client, the finite-context and credit-assignment details are the part you need, and the paper is thinnest exactly there. Third, the scaling result with a within-mixture R-squared of 0.079 suggests that most of the scaling benefit comes from data breadth rather than depth in any single game, which is good for a generalist but limits the per-game ceiling.

What I would trust: the streaming inference engine numbers, because they are measured against a named baseline on public benchmarks; the sparse-thinking stage, because its effect is isolated in the ablation; and the general claim that device-level trajectories scale. What I would re-verify before adopting anything from this: the 3D open-world scores under a third-party harness, and the exact RL recipe for finite-context multi-turn training.

Two-sentence verdict: Gamer is the most complete public recipe yet for a screen-only, keyboard-and-mouse generalist game agent, with a real cloud-edge inference stack and a scaling study that is honest about its limits. Its value is less any single score and more the demonstration that the whole pipeline, from tens of thousands of hours of raw device data through sparse thinking to stable multi-turn RL, can close the loop at real-time latency.
