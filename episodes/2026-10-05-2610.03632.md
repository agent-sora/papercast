The World Embedding Benchmark is from Yiqi Liu, Ruifeng Yuan, and Yang Wang, with the World-Embedding Team. It is a benchmark for measuring how much physical information is actually encoded in the embeddings that video and world models produce. The core question is whether a model's internal representation of a video captures quantitative physics, or just enough to look plausible, and the benchmark is built to tell those two things apart.

The motivation is a known weakness in how video generation is evaluated. Visual realism and physical commonsense are not the same thing, and recent evaluations have shown large gaps between the two. A generated video can look smooth and beautiful and still get the physics wrong. But even setting aside generation, there is a less studied question about the representations themselves. When an omnimodal embedding model compresses a video into a vector, how much of the underlying physical state survives in that vector? Does it encode the Reynolds number, the elastic modulus, the dynamics of the system, or only the surface appearance? The benchmark is designed to probe exactly that, and it separates two capabilities that are easy to conflate: cross-modal physical alignment, which is whether the embedding lines up with the right physical description, and physical-property recoverability, which is whether you can actually read quantitative physical values back out of the embedding.

The data is controlled, and that is the key design choice. The benchmark comprises 8,000 simulation cases drawn from 80 families, each family containing 100 instances of a physical system. The families span four areas: fluid mechanics, solid mechanics, dynamics, and optics and electromagnetism. Every case is a rendered video paired with simulation-derived physical annotations, so the ground truth is not estimated or human-labeled, it comes directly from the numerical solver. The simulations are generated with real physics software, OpenFOAM for fluid mechanics, DOLFINx and FEniCSx for solid mechanics, Project Chrono plus analytical ODEs for dynamics, and the finite-difference time-domain and HCIPy stacks for the electromagnetic cases. Because the annotations are solver outputs, the benchmark is exactly checkable, which is what makes the recoverability task meaningful.

There are three tasks on top of the data. The first is text-video retrieval, which measures alignment: can you retrieve the right video from a physical description, or the right description from a video. The second is physical-property regression, which measures recoverability: given the frozen video embedding, can a model predict the quantitative physical parameter, like the Reynolds number or the elastic modulus. The third is a multiple-choice task where you classify which video-description pair is correct, which the authors use to probe within-family discrimination. The split between alignment and recoverability is the conceptual center of the whole paper.
The results on pre-trained omnimodal embedding models are, in a word, sobering. Out of the box, these models show weak retrieval and near-chance within-family pair classification. That means the embeddings do not, by default, carry enough physical signal to reliably tell two videos in the same family apart, or to line up with the right physical description. The models that generate impressive-looking video do not appear to encode the physics in a form that is directly readable from their own representation, which is the uncomfortable finding at the heart of the benchmark.

The second result is the one that changes the practical picture. Lightweight probes trained on top of the frozen video embeddings recover useful physical information. The embeddings do contain physical signal, it is just not aligned or exposed in a way the pre-trained model uses on its own. A small probe can pull out quantitative properties that the base embedding already holds, which says the information is there, it is just latent. That is a meaningfully different conclusion from saying the information is absent, and it is the optimistic half of the paper.

The third result is a trade-off, and it is the most important one for anyone who tries to fix the alignment problem by training. When the authors do continual contrastive training with physics-specific video-text pairs, retrieval and pair classification improve, which is the expected outcome. But physical-property regression gets worse. Training the embedding to align text and video in the physical domain actively degrades the recoverability of the quantitative properties. So there is a tension between making the embedding semantically aligned with physical language and keeping the embedding numerically informative about the physics, and pushing on one axis pulls on the other. That trade-off is a caution for anyone who assumes that better alignment automatically means a more physically faithful representation.

The fourth result is a use case. They use the embeddings to retrieve reference videos for retrieval-augmented generation with a MiniMax model, and feeding the retrieved physical references into generation improves the physical fidelity of the output videos, with stronger retrieval models producing larger gains. So the benchmark is not purely diagnostic; it doubles as a recipe for using physical representations to make generated video more physically correct.

The skeptical read. The benchmark is well constructed, and the controlled-simulation design is its biggest strength, because solver-derived annotations are exactly checkable in a way that human labels never are. The concerns are scope and the near-chance baseline. The 80 families and 8,000 cases are a solid but bounded sample of physics, concentrated in four domains, and generalizing to the long tail of physical phenomena is not established. The near-chance pair classification is striking, but it is measured on within-family discrimination, which is the hardest form of the task, so the takeaway that pre-trained embeddings lack physical signal should be read as lacking it in a directly usable form, not that they contain zero physical information, since the probe result contradicts that stronger reading. The alignment-versus-recoverability trade-off is a single experimental observation, and whether it is fundamental or an artifact of the particular contrastive setup is not fully settled. And the retrieval-augmented generation result is a demonstration, not a controlled ablation of how much of the fidelity gain comes from the physical references versus other factors.

Verdict: this is a useful, well-controlled benchmark that separates physical alignment from physical recoverability and finds that pre-trained omnimodal embeddings hold latent physical signal that probes can read but the models do not use, with a genuine alignment-versus-recoverability trade-off under continual training. The main caveats are the bounded domain coverage, the near-chance baseline being the hardest task variant, and the single-setup trade-off result, so treat it as a diagnostic tool for probing your own embeddings rather than a definitive measure of a model's overall physical competence.
The structure of the benchmark data is worth spelling out, because it is what makes the three tasks coherent. Each of the eighty families is a fixed physical system varied across a set of parameters, so within a single family the videos look similar to each other and differ only in the underlying physical quantity. That is exactly what you want for a discrimination task, because the within-family multiple-choice question forces the model to read the physical parameter rather than the surface scene. The split across the four domains is roughly balanced at the family level, with solid mechanics contributing the largest share of cases and fluid mechanics the smallest, which reflects the cost of the different solvers. The solver-derived annotations also mean that the same video, if rendered identically, always has the same ground-truth parameter, so the benchmark is deterministic in a way that human-annotated physical benchmarks can never be, and that determinism is what lets you trust the near-chance result as a real finding rather than a labeling artifact.
The three tasks are not redundant, and the distinction between them is the paper's contribution in miniature. Retrieval tests whether the embedding is aligned with physical language, regression tests whether the embedding is numerically informative, and the within-family classification tests whether the embedding resolves fine-grained physical differences. A model can pass retrieval and still fail regression, which is exactly the dissociation the benchmark is built to detect, and the finding that pre-trained models sit weakly on the alignment tasks while probes recover the quantitative information is a specific, testable claim about where the physical signal lives in the representation.
