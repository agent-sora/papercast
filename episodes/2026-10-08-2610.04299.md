Questioning the Questions is from Jinyuan Li, Chengsong Huang, and Langlin Huang, at Washington University in St. Louis, with Shiping Gao at the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, and Jiaxin Huang as the corresponding author. The paper works on self-evolving reasoning models, the class of methods where a model improves by generating its own questions and then learning to answer them, a loop that is attractive precisely because it needs no external data. The known failure mode of this loop is collapse: over successive rounds of self-training, performance degrades, which is frustrating because the whole pitch is that the model gets better the more it trains on itself. Their contribution is a diagnosis of why it degrades, and then a method, R-Quest, that sustains the self-evolution across many rounds by fixing the two specific quality problems in the self-generated questions. The framing is that self-evolution is not failing because the model cannot learn from its own output, it is failing because the output it learns from is quietly getting worse, and that is a fixable problem rather than a fundamental limit.

The diagnosis is the interesting part, and it identifies two recurring problems in the self-generated question stream. The first is validity. A meaningful fraction of the model's own questions are simply invalid, meaning they do not have a well-defined answer, or the model's proposed answer does not actually follow from the question. This is not a small rounding error; it is the dominant mode of failure in later rounds. And the problem gets worse over rounds: the valid-question rate falls from 76.5 percent in round one to 39.5 percent by round five, so more than half of what the model is training on in later rounds is broken. The mechanism is intuitive once you see it: a model that is already struggling with a topic generates weaker questions about that topic, and those weaker questions are more likely to be ill-posed, so the invalidity is self-reinforcing. Worse, the standard answer-consistency filter, which is the usual way people try to clean this up, makes it worse, because it preferentially retains the questions whose answers the model happens to be confident about, and after filtering the valid-question rate drops further, to about 27.6 percent in round five. So the cleanup mechanism is actively concentrating the invalid questions, which is a subtle and useful negative result, because it means the most common mitigation in current self-evolution pipelines is counterproductive.

The second problem is diversity collapse. Existing controls for question diversity are based on lexical similarity, so they cluster questions that use the same words and drop near-duplicates. But that misses mathematically equivalent questions expressed in different words. A question about the area of a triangle and a question about half the base times the height are the same problem in different clothes, and a BLEU-based or token-similarity filter treats them as distinct, so the model keeps training on rephrasings of a small set of underlying problems while believing it has a diverse curriculum. The paper quantifies this: under their clustering, a small set of question types accounts for 59.5 percent of the sample, and once you let mathematically equivalent questions collapse into one type, the effective diversity of the training set is much lower than the surface lexical diversity suggests. There is also a compounding effect with the validity problem: the invalid questions that survive the filter tend to cluster around the same few broken problem types, so in later rounds the model is both less diverse and less valid, and the two failure modes reinforce each other rather than being independent. That interaction is the part of the diagnosis that is hardest to see from the outside and is, I think, the reason a single-component fix would not be enough.

The fix, R-Quest, addresses both problems. For validity, they introduce a validity-aware component that learns to recognize and reject invalid questions rather than just filtering by answer consistency. They train a solver to detect invalid questions, and report that its balanced accuracy on that detection task rises from about 64.8 percent after initialization to 72.9 percent in round ten, and its invalid-question recall reaches 86.36 percent by round ten, whereas a baseline, R-Zero, stays between 10 and 26 percent across all rounds. For diversity, they replace lexical similarity with a mathematical-equivalence-aware clustering, so that rephrasings of the same underlying problem are counted as one, and they enforce a same-type fraction constraint to keep the question stream from collapsing onto a few problem types.

The solver side has a subtler design point worth calling out. They use a validity-aware initialization, where the solver starts from a base that has already seen the distinction between a well-posed question and a broken one, and they add a small replay fraction, about 10 percent, from the original distribution into each round's training mix, so the model does not drift so far from the original task that it starts optimizing for the self-generated distribution alone. That replay is one of the standard stabilizers in iterative self-training, but the paper's specific contribution is that the validity signal is what lets the replay be small, because most of the quality control is happening at the question-filtering stage rather than in the solver's training objective.

The result is that self-evolution stops collapsing. After ten rounds of self-evolution, R-Quest is 5.75 points above the base model, and it beats the base Qwen3-4B-Base by 5.47 points and the OctoThinker-3B baseline by 5.79 points on a seven-benchmark math average. In the component ablations, the validity-aware question component is the single largest contributor, adding several points on its own, which tells you that cleaning the question stream is more important than the fancy diversity controls, and that the diversity component adds a further chunk on top. The method is evaluated on both Qwen3-4B-Base and OctoThinker-3B, so it is not a one-model result, and the code is released.

The evaluation setup is worth a line because it is what makes the collapse measurable in the first place. They track the valid-question rate and the solver's accuracy on valid questions across all ten rounds, and they report the balanced accuracy of the invalid-question detector separately, which is a cleaner metric for the validity component than raw accuracy because a trivial model that accepts every question scores 50 percent on balanced accuracy without detecting anything. They also report the invalid-question recall directly, which is the capability that actually matters in production, because a detector that finds most broken questions is worth more than one that has high balanced accuracy but low recall. The fact that they instrument both the question side and the solver side across all rounds, rather than just reporting the final round's benchmark score, is what lets them attribute the improvement to the quality controls rather than to a lucky late-round recovery.

A skeptical read. The validity diagnosis is the strongest part, and the finding that answer-consistency filtering concentrates invalid questions is a genuinely useful warning, because a lot of self-evolution pipelines use exactly that filter and would be quietly degrading their own training data. I would want to see the validity detector's precision, not just its recall, because an 86 percent recall with unreported precision means it is also rejecting some valid questions, and over ten rounds that rejection bias could shape what the model learns to find valid. The second thing I would re-verify is the mathematical-equivalence clustering, because deciding when two math questions are the same problem is itself a hard judgment, and if the clustering is too aggressive it will drop genuinely distinct problems and the diversity gain is partly an artifact of over-merging. The third: the gains are on 4B and 3B models over ten rounds, which is a reasonable regime for self-evolution research, but self-evolution dynamics can change at scale, and I would not assume the same validity rate holds for a frontier model generating its own questions, where the invalid-question distribution could look very different.

Bottom line: self-evolving reasoning models collapse because the self-generated questions get both less valid and less diverse over time, and the specific fix, a validity-aware rejector plus math-equivalence-aware diversity control, is a reasonable and well-motivated patch. Before you adopt it, check the validity detector's precision and the aggressiveness of the equivalence clustering, because both of those choices quietly determine how much of the question stream the model is allowed to learn from.
