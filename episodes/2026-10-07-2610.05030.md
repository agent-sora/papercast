EVISKILL, Grounding Skill Evolution in Replayable Evidence, is from Yan Zhou, Yili Wang, and Yiwei Dai, with the full team of five authors at the School of Artificial Intelligence at Jilin University in Changchun. The paper works in a hot corner of agentic research: letting an LLM agent accumulate reusable procedural knowledge, what these papers call skills, from its own experience, without updating any model weights. The premise is that an agent maintains a document of reusable instructions, and after it interacts with tasks it edits that document so it does better next time. The paper's complaint is that the existing methods edit that document based on plausibility, not on evidence, and the result is a skill book that quietly accumulates mistakes.

The motivation comes from two preliminary studies that are worth sitting with, because they are the actual empirical justification for the whole framework. The first re-runs edits that an existing method, Trace2Skill, generated from real trajectories, applying each edit and re-executing the tasks that motivated it. The finding is that experience-derived edits do not reliably do what they claim. Across three interactive benchmarks, ALFWorld, AppWorld, and ScienceWorld, a large share of the edits either do nothing or make things worse. In their aggregate, the accept, reflect, and reject split is roughly 55, 25, and 20 percent on AppWorld, 60, 15, and 25 on ScienceWorld, and 75, 10, and 15 on ALFWorld. So even the edits that come out of a successful interaction are, in a meaningful fraction of cases, not actually fixing the behavior that motivated them.

The second preliminary study targets the revision level. Most skill-evolution loops discard an entire batch of edits when the batch fails a global validation check, but a batch is a bundle of edits with different effects, so a rejected bundle can still contain genuinely useful individual edits. They test this by having a strong model pick the few most promising edits out of rejected batches and applying just those. The selectively retained edits improve performance on the associated tasks even though the whole batch they came from was rejected: gains of about 4 points on ALFWorld, 3 points on AppWorld, and 12.5 points on ScienceWorld over the previously validated skill. The takeaway is symmetric: an edit is not justified merely because it was derived from experience, and it is not invalidated merely because the batch it sat in failed.

EVISKILL organizes the evolution loop around a central data structure called the Replayable Evidence Card. A Card records a proposed skill correction together with the specific trajectory range that supports it: the task, the epoch, and the step interval, so the evidence can later be re-executed. The method has three stages. First, evidence-grounded edit synthesis. An LLM evidence extractor reads the current epoch's trajectories and emits Cards, each linking a local observation to a proposed fix. Cards are grouped into evidence windows by embedding the Card text with a small local model, Qwen3 Embedding 0.6B, and clustering with HDBSCAN, so related corrections get edited together rather than one at a time. An LLM editor then turns each window into candidate edits, each edit carrying provenance links back to its supporting Cards.

Second, replay-guided edit verification. This is the part that distinguishes the paper. For each candidate edit, the system replays the exact trajectory range that Card cited. It loads the recorded task, fast-forwards the environment to the recorded prefix, then lets the agent re-execute the target interval under the edited skill, and compares the re-executed segment against the original. An evaluator returns accept, reflect, or reject with feedback. So an edit only survives if, when you actually run the behavior it claims to fix, the behavior changes as intended. Third, cross-epoch evidence propagation. When a consolidated batch passes global validation, the validated skill is updated. When it fails, edits that individually survived replay are not thrown away; they go into a provisional edit ledger and keep participating in the working skill for the next epoch, where new experience can either confirm or contradict them.

The evaluation uses six agent backbones, three GPT variants at medium reasoning and three Qwen models, on the same three interactive benchmarks, with a GPT 5.5 model doing the extraction, editing, and evaluation at temperature 0.7 for the action agent. The headline result is that EVISKILL wins or ties on nearly every backbone-benchmark cell. Under the strongest backbone, GPT 5.5, it reaches 96.27 on ALFWorld, 94.64 on AppWorld, and 83.41 on ScienceWorld, against 90.30, 85.71, and 76.30 with no skill at all, and against baselines like EvoSkill that collapse to 54.5 on ScienceWorld. The pattern is consistent: the evidence-and-replay filtering mostly helps, and it helps most where the baselines are worst.

Ablations isolate the two mechanisms. Removing the replay-guided verification lets unsupported revisions slip through and drops the scores, because the system reverts to trusting that an edit is valid because it was derived from a real trajectory, which is exactly the failure the first preliminary study showed is not reliable. Removing the cross-epoch propagation means a rejected batch's useful edits are discarded immediately rather than carried forward, which costs the most on ScienceWorld, where the signal for which edits are good is the noisiest and a single validation round is not enough to sort them. The case studies in the paper show the mechanism working as intended: an edit that looked plausible from the trajectory but, on replay, produced no change in behavior gets rejected, while an edit from a globally rejected batch that does change behavior on replay gets retained into the provisional ledger and later confirmed.

There are real costs and real limits here, and I would want to be clear about them before anyone treats this as a drop-in. The pipeline is heavy. Each evolution epoch traverses the whole training task set, the extractor, editor, and evaluator all run a large reasoning model, and each edit is replayed on up to three trajectory ranges with an additional post-rejection replay pass. This is not a lightweight fine-tuning substitute; it is a substantial amount of LLM and environment compute per unit of skill improvement, and the gains are measured on interactive benchmarks where the task count is in the low hundreds. The provisional ledger is capped at 16 live edits, cards go stale after two replay rejections, and evidence windows hold four to twenty cards, so the system is deliberately bounded, which is good for stability but means it is tuned to a particular scale of skill book.

The deeper limitation is that the whole framework is only as good as the replay environment, and replay fidelity is the unstated assumption. If the recorded prefix cannot faithfully reconstruct the environment state, or if the task is not deterministic enough that re-executing the target interval is meaningful, then the accept, reflect, reject signal is measuring the wrong thing. On ALFWorld and AppWorld that assumption is fairly safe, but the method does not come with a general argument for when replay is a valid verifier, and that is the piece I would re-verify before adopting it in a less controllable domain.

Bottom line: this is a genuinely useful reframing of skill evolution, from editing by plausibility to editing by replayed evidence, and the two preliminary studies are honest, well-motivated justifications rather than a post-hoc story. The trade-off is a heavy, multi-model, replay-dependent pipeline that is clearly tuned to interactive benchmarks, so I would trust the method's logic and the benchmark results, and re-verify the replay-fidelity assumption and the compute cost in my own setting before building on it.

One more implementation detail worth flagging, because it changes the cost picture. The method separates a validated skill, which only changes when a full batch passes global validation, from a working skill, which is what the agent actually runs with and which includes the provisional ledger. That separation is what makes the cross-epoch propagation safe: the working skill can carry unproven edits without contaminating the validated one, and a bad provisional edit costs you at most the gap between the two, not a regression of the committed skill. If you are building your own version, that validated-versus-working split is the piece to copy first, even if you skip the rest.
