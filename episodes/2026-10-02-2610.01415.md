Beyond Memory, Harnessing Long-Horizon Agents with Explicit Belief States. The first three authors are Yu Luo, Jiamin Jiang, and Yimin Zuo, working across Nankai University, Alibaba Group, and Tsinghua University, with the work done during an internship at Alibaba.

The problem is long-horizon agency. An agent interacts with an environment over many steps, and the standard move is to keep the raw interaction history, or a memory system that selects and compresses it, and feed that to the model. The paper's objection is that accumulating the history, even a curated one, does not guarantee a coherent picture of the current world. The formal target is the belief state, the posterior over the current latent world given everything the agent has seen, which is a sufficient statistic for decision-making and can be updated recursively after each action and observation. The gap is that no existing framework maintains such a belief during the episode. Some work represents goals relative to state, but does not enforce that the belief is internally consistent and evidence-supported. Other work detects when a belief has drifted and truncates the rollout for offline training, but offers no way to recover inside the episode that is already running. So the belief, if it exists, is fragile, and when it goes wrong the agent keeps acting.

They name the failure mode Belief Trapping: the agent continues to act without making meaningful progress toward the goal, because its internal picture has quietly diverged from reality. The key point is that trapping is a distinct failure from a wrong belief. A wrong belief gets corrected the next time the environment provides contradicting evidence. A trapped agent, by contrast, is in a self-consistent local loop. Its beliefs are internally coherent and supported by the evidence it has seen, but that evidence no longer advances the goal, so nothing contradicts the belief, so the agent never notices it has stopped moving forward. The motivating example is a real ALFWorld task, put a hot mug in the cabinet. Under implicit belief reconstruction, the agent's running picture of the mug's state and location drifts, it keeps taking actions that are consistent with its stale belief, and it never reaches the goal. Under an explicit belief, the drift is visible, and the agent can correct course. The paper also makes the formal connection to the belief-state literature explicit. The belief is the posterior over the current latent world given the history, a sufficient statistic for decision-making that can be updated recursively, and the contribution is to make that posterior an explicit, maintained, and checked object inside a live episode rather than an implicit byproduct of the context window.

The framework, which they call PoS, has three moving parts. It constructs a task-conditioned belief state that combines an estimate of the current world with the unresolved task requirements, so it states explicitly what the agent still needs to learn and what it still needs to accomplish. This is the key structural choice. The belief is not just a summary of where the agent thinks it is, but a pairing of that estimate with the open requirements, so the gap between what is true and what still needs to be done is written down explicitly. A pure world-state summary can be fully consistent and still miss the goal, because it says nothing about what is left. By carrying the requirements alongside the state, the framework makes that gap a first-class object it can monitor. It then validates that belief for internal consistency and for evidential support from the interaction history, so the belief is not just fluent but grounded. And it monitors task progress to detect trapping, after which recovery is tailored to both the trap and the current belief, rather than a generic reset.

The recovery step is where the maintenance earns its cost. When trapping is detected, the agent does not just restart or drop the last few steps. It diagnoses which part of the belief is stale and which requirement is stalled, and the recovery action is chosen to address that specific pair. A belief that says the mug is in the sink when it is actually in the microwave gets a recovery that re-grounds the mug's location, not a recovery that forgets the last action. The point of factorizing the recovery over the belief and the trap is that the two failures, a wrong belief and a stalled requirement, have different fixes, and a generic recovery conflates them.

The results span four benchmarks and three backbones. The benchmarks cover embodied manipulation, ALFWorld, a long-context tool-use benchmark, an incident root-cause analysis task on one hundred incidents, and a clinical diagnosis task. The baselines are the raw trajectory, three memory methods, ACON, PACE, and HiAgent, and a long-horizon harness. On the Qwen backbone, PoS reaches 88.81 on ALFWorld against 72.39 for the strongest harness baseline, 56.38 on the long-context benchmark against 52.57, and 38.83 on root-cause analysis against 26.21. The same pattern holds on the other two backbones, Kimi and GLM. The ablations are the more interesting part. Removing the consistency validation step drops the ALFWorld number from 88.81 to 73.88, and removing the trapping diagnosis drops it to 73.13, so both maintenance steps carry real weight, and the cost-performance study shows the extra belief-maintenance computation is what buys the accuracy, not a free lunch.

The scaling experiments are the part that most directly tests the framework's robustness to long context. On the long-context tool-use benchmark they grow the context from 8,000 tokens to 256,000 tokens and track task success across the three backbones. The point is that as the raw history grows, the implicit-baseline agents degrade because the relevant state gets buried in an ever-larger context, while PoS, which maintains a compact explicit belief, stays comparatively flat, so the benefit of an explicit belief state should grow with context length rather than shrink. That is the resilience claim, and it is the argument for why maintaining a belief is cheaper than reading an ever-larger transcript at inference time. There is also a subtler point in the benchmark mix. ALFWorld is the shortest of the four benchmarks in absolute context length, but it is the one where belief trapping is most likely to bite, because the tasks are short enough that a wrong belief can persist for the whole episode without the environment producing a strong enough contradicting observation to correct it. The incident root-cause and clinical diagnosis tasks are the opposite, they have long contexts but the evidence arrives in a more structured, document-like form, so the belief is easier to maintain and the gain over a memory baseline is smaller. The fact that the framework wins on both the short-trap-prone task and the long-context tasks, with the largest margins on ALFWorld, is the pattern you would expect if the benefit really comes from catching and recovering from traps rather than from compressing context, and it is the clearest evidence in the paper that the two failure modes, a wrong belief and a stalled requirement, are distinct and that treating them separately is what buys the accuracy.

Skeptically, this is inference-time machinery, so it spends extra tokens to build and maintain the belief, and the paper is candid that the cost-performance tradeoff is the open problem, that reducing the overhead while keeping the gain is future work. The belief is an LLM-generated object, so its consistency is only as good as the model that writes and checks it, and the validation is a prompt-level procedure, not a formal guarantee. The benchmarks are also a mixed bag of task types, so a single framework spanning embodied and diagnostic tasks is a broad claim, and the margins, while consistent, are measured on a specific set of backbones. The honest read is that making the belief explicit and then actively maintaining it, checking it and recovering when it traps, is a reasonable and well-motivated step beyond passive memory, with a clear failure mode and a clean ablation, but the overhead and the reliance on the backbone to police its own belief are the real open questions.
