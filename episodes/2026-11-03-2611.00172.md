From Fragmentation to Fusion: A Survey and Roadmap for Omnimodal Embeddings, by Ziyin Zhang, Yuyu Guo, and Ying Zhang, from Ant Group with co-authors at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, the Australian National University, Tongji University, and the National University of Singapore, is a position paper about the quietest infrastructure in machine learning: the embedding model. Embeddings underpin virtually everything, search, recommendation, retrieval-augmented generation, memory for agents, and the paper's opening observation is that despite a shared mathematical foundation, the field is fragmented by modality. The text-embedding community, the vision-embedding community, the audio and video communities, largely work in isolation, seldom citing each other, even though the same contrastive-learning machinery and the same transformer backbones keep reappearing in every modality. The paper's thesis is that this fragmentation is not just a sociology problem, it is obscuring a real convergence, and it proposes a framework for reasoning about that convergence without pretending the modalities are the same.

The framework is the paper's actual contribution, and it is worth taking seriously because it is a capability ladder, not a taxonomy of papers. Three progressively harder capabilities. First, coverage: can the embedding handle the input, any modality, any granularity, from a token to a span to a full document to a clip of audio. Second, comparability: can two embeddings from different modalities be compared in a shared space, is a picture of a dog close to the word dog. Third, compositionality: can you compose embeddings, add them, combine a text embedding with an image embedding to represent a query that is genuinely both. And then the paper maps three technical paths onto the ladder, in order. Transformation broadens coverage: project everything into one modality, caption the image and embed the caption. Alignment enables comparability: contrastive learning across modalities, the CLIP recipe. And joint embedding, which the paper calls the terminus, aims at compositionality: train a single model that natively embeds all modalities into one space where arithmetic on the vectors means something. The paper's argument is that the field has been doing these three things in parallel without naming them, and that naming them is what lets you see where the real frontier is, which is not better alignment, that is largely solved at the pairwise level, but compositionality, where almost nothing works yet.

The empirical spine of the paper is a reading of the literature the paper calls a granularity shift: embedding research is moving from whole-document and whole-image embeddings toward token-level, span-level, and fine-grained ones, driven by the needs of retrieval and of agents that index and remember. The paper makes a pointed claim here that representation research is falling behind generation. The models that jointly perceive four modalities are being indexed and remembered by retrieval systems that are bimodal, pairwise-aligned, or text-centric, and the gap between what the generator can perceive and what the retriever can store and find is a structural bottleneck for retrieval-augmented systems. That is a fair and under-appreciated point: a lot of the failure modes in retrieval-augmented generation are not in the language model, they are in the index, and the index is built by embedding models that were designed for a simpler world. The paper also reports that works explicitly crossing modality boundaries account for under five percent of the community's output, which is the quantitative version of the fragmentation claim, and it is the number that makes the position legible.

Where I would push back, and where a skeptical reader should, is on the strength of the convergence claim. The paper is right that the mathematical machinery is shared, contrastive objectives and transformer encoders recur everywhere, but shared machinery is a weaker statement than shared principles, and the modality-specific challenges the paper concedes are underexplored are exactly where the machinery stops transferring. Audio embeddings and video embeddings have temporal structure that text embeddings do not, and the granularity shift is not uniform across modalities. The three-capability ladder is clean, but the paper does not show a single experiment where a system designed for compositionality beats one designed for alignment, because no such system exists yet, and that is the honest limit of a position paper in this space. The roadmap is real, the ladder is a useful object, and the under-five-percent number is a genuine finding, but the fusion the title promises is a destination the field has not yet built a road toward, and the paper is at its best when it is mapping the territory and at its weakest when it is naming the destination. The practical implication for a builder, though, is clear and actionable: if you are running a retrieval or memory system today, the cheapest big win is probably not a better language model, it is an index that can actually see what your generator can see, and that is a sentence worth taking out of this paper.

The three paths the paper maps onto the ladder are also worth a word each, because they are not equal in maturity and the paper is honest about it. Transformation, the first path, is the cheapest and the least principled: caption the image, transcribe the audio, embed the text, and you have coverage of every modality at the price of information loss at every boundary, and it is the path most production systems are on today without realizing they have made a bet. Alignment, the second path, is where the field's money and its best results have been for years, and it is largely solved at the pairwise level, the picture-of-a-dog-is-close-to-the-word-dog property is a given, which is exactly why the paper says the frontier is not here. Joint embedding, the terminus, is the only path that promises compositionality, and it is the only one with essentially no deployed system behind it yet, which is the gap the whole roadmap is pointing at. Reading the ladder with that maturity gradient on it, the paper's actual claim becomes precise: the field has finished the easy rungs and is standing at the base of the hard one.

There is a structural reason the fragmentation persists that the paper names only in passing, and it is worth expanding, because it explains why the convergence the paper argues for has not already happened. The evaluation infrastructure is modality-locked. The text-embedding community benchmarks on retrieval and classification suites for text, the vision community on image retrieval and classification, and the cross-modal work, which is the under-five-percent minority, has no shared benchmark that would force the two communities to compare in the same space. So the incentives that would drive convergence are missing at the measurement level, not just the citation level: a group improving a text embedding has no reason to test it on an image, because there is no standard image test that would register the gain. That is why the paper's granularity shift matters more than it looks. Moving to token-level and span-level embeddings is the precondition for a shared benchmark, because fine-grained embeddings are the only kind that can be evaluated across modalities on the same task, a span of text and a region of an image can both be the answer to a query, but two whole documents cannot. The paper's terminus, joint embedding, is therefore not just a model architecture, it is a bet that the evaluation will follow the granularity, and that bet is the part of the roadmap I would watch. If the next generation of embedding benchmarks is fine-grained and cross-modal, the ladder in this paper becomes a schedule, and if it is not, the field keeps doing its three parallel things and the fragmentation is stable.

My verdict is that this is a well-constructed position paper that gives a fragmented field a shared vocabulary, and the coverage-comparability-compositionality ladder is the object to cite. The representation-is-falling-behind-generation argument is the most important claim in it. But it is a survey with a roadmap, not a result, and the fusion it advocates is still a research program rather than a method, so read it to know what embedding research should be trying to do next, not to know what it has already done. The single most useful thing it will do for a practitioner is reframe a familiar problem: the next time a retrieval system fails to find something the model clearly can perceive, the failure is in the index, and the index is an embedding model, and the fix is a finer-grained, cross-modal one, not a bigger generator.
