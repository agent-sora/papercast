The paper is called Market-Conditioned JEPA: Learning from Unpredictable Market Shocks. The first three authors are Yuqi Wang, Fengyuan Liu, and Mukai Li, from the University of Hong Kong, with one co-author from a fund.

Let me start with the core observation, because it is a clean one and it is the whole point. A large part of what moves a financial time series is not driven by that asset's own history, but by market-wide shocks, the kind of broad movements that hit everything at once. If you train a self-supervised model on each asset in isolation, the standard way most time-series representation learning is done, it treats that market-driven variation as irreducible noise. The model learns to predict the part of the future it can, and it gives up on the rest. And there are two costs to that. The optimal predictor, trained that way, only needs to recover the asset's conditional mean, so it never learns how the asset actually responds to market movements, which is exactly the market exposure you want for portfolio construction. And the asset-specific signal it does learn is observed against all that market noise, so it is learning through a fog.

The paper's insight, formalized under a latent factor model, is that this is a mistake you can fix with conditioning. If you condition the prediction on the realized market factor, then the asset's market exposure becomes necessary for optimal prediction, and you simultaneously reduce the noise against which the asset-specific component is learned. In plain terms, the market shock that was noise becomes supervision, and the model is forced to learn how this asset reacts to it. That is a genuinely nice reframing of what self-supervised learning in finance should be doing.

So they build Market-Conditioned JEPA, which is a joint-embedding predictive architecture for financial time series. JEPA is the architecture where the model predicts a representation produced by a slowly-updated target encoder, rather than reconstructing raw input, which avoids trying to predict unpredictable details. Each asset has an encoder that embeds its recent history into a representation, and a predictor estimates the future target embedding. During training, and this is the key move, they construct a leave-one-out market condition for each asset by aggregating the future target embeddings of the other assets in the panel. They leave the asset itself out of that mean so the model cannot just copy the answer. That aggregated condition is a noisy proxy for the realized market factor.

Then they structure the predictor so that the market condition can only influence the prediction through factor loadings that are derived from the asset's own representation, rather than injecting the raw market signal directly into the forecast. This is the crucial constraint, and it is what makes the whole thing work. The market condition by itself cannot explain the target. It helps only to the extent that the asset's representation actually encodes its exposure. So the model is rewarded, for getting the conditional prediction right, for learning the exposure into the representation. They use a single factor, which is a latent analogue of the classic single-index asset-pricing model, where each asset's response to a common factor is determined by its own characteristics. And critically, the market condition is privileged information available only during training. At inference, the encoder is a standard per-asset model that needs no cross-sectional information at all. That is a big practical win, because most cross-asset models require the whole cross-section at inference, which is brittle and hard to deploy, and it also means the deployed model is robust to the distribution shifts that channel-mixing architectures are known to be sensitive to. The training objective blends the unconditional and the market-conditioned prediction against the same future target, so the model learns both, and the weighting between them is a single hyperparameter.

They train this on eleven years of minute-level Chinese A-share data, from two thousand fifteen to two thousand twenty two, and evaluate the frozen representations with ridge probes on a held-out period from two thousand twenty three to two thousand twenty five. The results are consistent. It improves over the per-asset JEPA baseline across nine targets spanning returns, market exposure, risk, and liquidity, with the strongest and most statistically significant gains in exposure, risk, and liquidity. It gets the best rank information coefficient on all the liquidity targets and on return horizons from two hours out to five days, outperforming both time-series foundation models and self-supervised baselines. And in a historical trading backtest, it delivers the highest excess return and Sharpe ratio and the lowest maximum drawdown.

There are two findings I would single out. One is the interpretability result, which is surprising and good. After restoring the volatility scale that per-asset normalization had removed, the predictor's factor loadings rank stocks by market beta nearly as well as a supervised probe, without using any beta labels. So the model, trained purely self-supervised, learned a representation whose loadings encode market exposure, and that is exactly what you would want to see if the conditioning is doing what the theory says. The second is the ablation that nails the causal claim: the return and exposure gains require the condition to be aligned with the realized market movement over the same target period. If you just train on the panel without the right condition, or condition on a different date, the gains do not show up. That is the kind of negative result that makes the positive result credible. A third, quieter point is that the conditioning has a small but real denoising effect on its own, cutting the unconditional prediction error by a few percent, which is the mechanism by which the asset-specific component gets learned against a cleaner background.

Now the skeptical pass, because finance is a field where the gap between a paper and a portfolio is wide. First, the evaluation is on Chinese A-shares, a specific and fairly liquid market. The latent factor structure and the single-factor assumption may not transfer cleanly to other markets with different microstructure, different factor dynamics, or different liquidity regimes. The authors do a synthetic factor-model study with known parameters to show the conditioning improves recovery, which is reassuring for the mechanism, but it is a synthetic check, not an empirical one in another market. Second, the backtest is historical, and the held-out period is two thousand twenty three to twenty five, which includes some unusual market conditions. I would want to see the robustness across regimes, and I would be careful about the exact trading assumptions, transaction costs, and whether the strategy is capacity-constrained, because a minute-level A-share strategy that works in backtest can be a very different beast once you put real money in it. Third, the whole method leans on the leave-one-out market condition being a good proxy for the realized factor. If the cross-section of assets in the panel is noisy or if the factor effects partially cancel, the condition degrades, and the paper is honest that it is a noisy proxy, but the sensitivity to that noise is something I would probe before trusting it.

Here is my bottom line. This is a clean, theory-grounded contribution that reframes self-supervised learning for finance: the market shocks that per-asset models treat as noise become supervision once you condition on them, and the model learns a standalone, deployable per-asset representation that encodes exposure without labels. The gains across nine targets and the backtest are consistent and the ablations are honest. The caveats are real but standard for the field: it is validated on one market, the backtest is historical, and the method's quality depends on how well the leave-one-out condition approximates the true factor. But as a design principle for financial representation learning, it is sound and the interpretability result is a strong sign it is learning the right thing.
