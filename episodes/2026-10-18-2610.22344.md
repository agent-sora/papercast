The paper is MLExpert, from Shangheng Du, Jinxin Shi, and Shuaiyu Zhang, from East China Normal University and the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, with Liang He as a co-author. The system is a thirty-five billion parameter mixture-of-experts agentic model, built specifically for autonomous machine learning engineering, and the headline number is that its post-training takes the base model's medal rate on MLE-Bench Lite from about thirty-eight percent to about seventy-three percent, putting a small active-parameter model in the same range as much larger frontier models. The framing the authors use is AI for AI, the idea that language models are moving from assisting scientific work toward autonomously developing and improving other AI systems, and machine learning engineering is the concrete setting where that has to work, because it is not one-shot generation but long-horizon optimization through repeated execution, feedback, and revision.

The setting matters, and it is worth laying out, because it is why the training method is what it is. A machine learning engineering task is a Kaggle-style competition. You get a dataset, a metric, and a submission format, and you have to iterate: build an initial model, run it, read the score, change a feature or a hyperparameter, run again. A single run can take a long time, and a full competition contains many such runs, so the feedback the model gets on any one decision is the outcome of several preceding decisions, not the decision itself. That is the core difficulty the paper is trying to address, and it is the reason the reinforcement learning is structured the way it is. Most existing machine learning engineering systems are workflow-driven. A predefined pipeline or a set of search rules controls the sequencing of experiments and the stage transitions, and the language model is called in at designated steps to generate or debug code. That works, but it puts the control logic in hand-designed scaffolding rather than in the model, which is exactly what you want to move into the model if the goal is a generalist agent.

The training has two parts. The first is a hierarchical agentic supervised fine-tune. The first stage teaches general coding and tool-use from existing trajectories. The second stage teaches the specific machine learning engineering behavior, using teacher trajectories on the competition tasks plus scientific experimentation trajectories derived from real papers. The tasks come from a Kaggle-MLE Factory, which the authors built to construct executable competition tasks automatically. It starts from a snapshot of competition metadata containing more than eleven thousand entries, in the paper's own count 11,649, screens for relevance, data availability, and offline evaluability, and selects a diverse set across vision, text, audio, tabular, and time series. The second training part is the interesting one, called Windowed Interaction RL. Instead of treating the whole multi-turn trajectory as the unit of credit, WIRL treats the stretch of decisions between two successive experiment results as a single execution window and assigns that window one shared, outcome-based reward. That is a meaningful design choice, because it matches the reward to the actual decision boundary: the reward arrives when you see a result, and the decisions that produced it are the ones in the most recent window. The system also collects rollouts asynchronously and applies policy correction, so the learner can start updating before a long trajectory finishes, which is what makes training on these long, expensive runs tractable at all.

The data side deserves a line, because the generalist claim rests on it. The Kaggle-MLE Factory is not a hand-picked set of competitions. It is a pipeline over a snapshot of competition entries that screens for relevance, for whether the data is still downloadable, and for whether the task can be evaluated offline without a live leaderboard, and then selects a diverse subset across the modalities, vision, text, audio, tabular, and time series. The scientific trajectories, drawn from real papers, are what give the model the behavior of designing an experiment and interpreting a result, which is a different skill from tuning a hyperparameter, and it is the reason the NatureBench transfer is in the results at all. The mixture-of-experts architecture, thirty-five billion total with about three billion active, is a practical choice. It means the inference cost during the long rollouts, which are the expensive part of the training, is that of a three billion model, which is what makes collecting these long, multi-run trajectories feasible in the first place.

The results are the point. On MLE-Bench Lite, the medal rate, the percentage of tasks earning any medal, goes from about thirty-eight percent for the base model to about seventy-three percent for MLExpert. The ablation shows the two stages each contribute, with the supervised fine-tune doing most of the lifting and the reinforcement learning pushing it further with online execution feedback. The comparison table is the most striking part. At the same scale, thirty-five billion total with about three billion active, the base Qwen model sits around forty-two percent on a general coding harness, and MLExpert reaches about seventy percent on that same harness, a huge jump that is not specific to the authors' own scaffold. Against the larger frontier models, MLExpert lands in the same band as models like Kimi-K3 and GLM, which are far larger, and beats several of them on the medal metric. The generalization story is also there: the learned optimization capability transfers to NatureBench, to agent scaffold optimization, and to algorithm optimization, which is the authors' argument that they trained a capability rather than a benchmark-fitting artifact.

What I would trust is the windowed credit assignment, because it is the piece most directly tied to the actual structure of the task, and it is the kind of insight that comes from really understanding what the feedback loop looks like. Assigning reward to the execution window, the decisions between two observed results, is a clean way to solve the delayed-credit problem that plagues long-horizon agent training, and the asynchronous collection with policy correction is the right engineering answer to the fact that these runs are slow. The generalization across harnesses, from the authors' own to a general-purpose coding agent, is the strongest evidence that this is a real capability gain and not overfitting to a scaffold, because it means the improvement survives being dropped into an environment the training never saw.

What I would re-verify is the scale of the frontier comparison and the medal-rate metric itself. The medal rate is a coarse, threshold-based metric, and a jump from thirty-eight to seventy-three percent is genuinely large, but I would want to see the distribution of the gains, because medal rates can move a lot from a small number of tasks that cross a threshold. The comparison to the frontier models is the headline, but those numbers come from their own harness or from published results, and the exact match between MLExpert and Kimi-K3 at seventy-two point seven percent is the sort of tie that is more suggestive than conclusive. The scientific-trajectory data from Nature-family papers is interesting for the NatureBench result, but it raises the question of how much of the transfer to scientific tasks is real optimization skill versus exposure to that specific data distribution. And the whole premise, that a three-billion-active model can match frontier models at machine learning engineering, is impressive enough that I would want to see it hold on harder, longer competitions, not just the Lite subset.

Two-sentence verdict: MLExpert is a serious step toward generalist machine learning engineering agents, and its core contribution is a training recipe, hierarchical supervised fine-tuning plus windowed interaction reinforcement learning with asynchronous collection, that matches reward to the real decision boundaries of long experimental runs and shows the capability transfers across harnesses and tasks. The jump to frontier-competitive medal rates at a small active scale is the striking claim, but the Lite subset and the threshold-based medal metric are the honest limits, and I would want the harder, full-length competitions before calling the frontier match robust.
