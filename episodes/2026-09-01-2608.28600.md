SHAPE of Chain-of-Thought in Math Reasoning, by Jonghyun Song, Sangjun Song, and Minjae Oh, from the Graduate School of Data Science and the Department of Mathematics Education at Seoul National University. The premise: stop treating a math chain-of-thought as a bag of tokens and start reading it the way a mathematics educator would, tracking which interpretation of the problem the model is working in and which concrete mathematical moves it makes.

The framework, called SHAPE, borrows two objects from decades of mathematical problem-solving research. The first is a semantic space, the evolving interpretation under which the model is solving a problem, algebraic, geometric, numerical, and so on. A single correct solution can move between several of these; the model might set up an equation, abandon it to try concrete numbers, then come back to the algebra. The second is a heuristic, the visible mathematical action taken inside a space: simplifying the problem, working backward, introducing an auxiliary object, making cases. These come from Pólya, Schoenfeld, and the think-aloud protocol tradition in mathematics education, where a human solver's verbalizations have been coded into exactly this kind of space-and-move structure for decades. The pipeline is an annotator, they use Grok-4.1-Fast for the labeling, that reads a CoT trace content unit by content unit, tags the heuristic of each unit, and tracks the semantic-space state as it changes, maintaining a memory buffer so it knows when the model is returning to a space it already visited versus opening a new one.

Why not just use the existing CoT analyses? Because the paper's premise is that none of them track the mathematical interpretation. Length tells you the trace is long, not that the reasoning is good. Self-revision markers tell you the model noticed something was off, not what it did about it. Episode labels like plan, implement, verify tell you the phase, not the mathematics. And graph or tree structure analyses tell you the shape of the reasoning, again without the content. The gap SHAPE is filling is the interpretive layer, the answer to what the model thinks the problem is at any given step, and that is the layer that mathematics education has argued, for a long time, actually determines whether a solver gets stuck or breaks through.

The first use of the lens is a predictive one, and it is the most direct evidence for the whole approach. They take the 100-problem Omni-MATH subset used by a prior benchmark, with CoT traces from 15 models, and train logistic regression classifiers to predict whether a trace ends in a correct answer. Surface features do almost nothing: CoT length alone is near chance, an AUROC of about 0.50, and adding reasoning-token counts does not move it. Self-revision markers, words like wait, aha, recheck, do better, around 0.62, and the episode-frequency features from a competing method, ThinkARM, match that. SHAPE's heuristic-frequency features beat all of them, and the effective number of semantic spaces a model concentrates its reasoning in separates correct from incorrect traces. The interpretation is clean: what predicts correctness is the mathematical substance of the reasoning, not its length or its surface markers.

The second finding is structural and, I think, the more interesting one. Across models, reasoning models show higher coverage of semantic space and higher transition density than instruction-tuned models, so extended reasoning does not just make traces longer, it changes how the model traverses interpretation space. The effective number of semantic spaces for the open-source reasoning models sits in the 1.8 to 2.5 range with transition ratios of 0.4 to 0.5, while the instruction-tuned models without extended reasoning sit lower, around 1.4 to 1.7 spaces and 0.2 to 0.3 transition ratios. That is a qualitative difference in traversal, not just a difference in length. More subtly, incorrect traces show higher transition ratios than correct ones, meaning the model revisits the same semantic spaces more intensively rather than making forward progress. That is a quantified version of the overthinking phenomenon, and it is measured in a space that is actually about the mathematics rather than about token counts. The practical upshot is that you can now look at a failing trace and see that the model is stuck oscillating between two interpretations instead of advancing, which is a diagnosis you cannot get from a perplexity number or a pass rate.

The third contribution is the one that gives the paper a training payoff. They ask whether reinforcement learning genuinely broadens a model's strategic repertoire, and the answer is no. Using density and coverage metrics on the heuristic-frequency distributions of successful traces, they show that RLVR post-training concentrates the model into a narrower region of strategy space that was already present in the base model. The Qwen 1.7B base to GRPO pair has a density of 1.22 and a coverage of 0.87; the Olmo 7B to its RLVR variant has density 1.25 and coverage 0.71; the unrelated-model baseline sits at density 0.52, so the high density is real mode-seeking, not just overlap. A PCA projection makes it visible: the post-trained successful traces pile onto the peak of the base distribution and leave the tail uncovered. RL is not teaching new strategies; it is pruning the base model's existing ones down to what scores.

The closing experiment turns the diagnosis into a training signal. They train two planning-based GRPO variants on Qwen 1.7B base over the MATH training split, evaluated on MATH-Perturb. The control adds an explicit planning step during rollout; the proposed variant keeps that structure but also injects a list of eleven mathematical heuristics into the rollout prompt. On the hard split, which is the one that actually requires a different strategy, the base model scores 11.84 on average, the planning control 14.52, and the heuristic-augmented version 17.72, with a matching pass-at-64 gain. The gap between the two variants comes entirely from the heuristic information in the prompt, so the claim is that explicitly diversifying the strategic moves available to the model during RL is enough to buy measurable accuracy on the problems that demand it.

Now the skeptical read. First, the entire framework rests on the annotator's fidelity. The heuristics and semantic spaces are assigned by a specific LLM, and the taxonomy, however grounded in educational research, is one human-consolidated choice; I would want inter-annotator agreement or at least a second annotator before treating the numbers as stable measurements rather than reflections of the labeling model's biases. Second, the predictive results are on 100 problems and 15 models, which is a reasonable diagnostic set but small, and the classifier is logistic regression, so the gains are only as good as the linear separability of the features. Third, and most important for anyone who wants to act on this, the mode-seeking result and the heuristic-augmented RL gain are both single-model, single-family results, Qwen 1.7B for the training payoff and one base-to-RL pair per family for the density analysis, so I would treat the claim that RL narrows strategies as well-motivated but not yet established across the model landscape. The MATH-Perturb hard split is a good stress test, but it is one 115-problem set. If you implement this: the cheapest high-value move is the diagnostic, run the semantic-space and heuristic tracking on your own math traces and look at whether your post-trained model's successful traces are concentrating; that is a read-only experiment that costs one annotation pass. The training move, adding a heuristic list to your RLVR rollout prompt, is a two-line change and the paper's evidence for it is preliminary, so I would A/B it on a held-out hard split before betting on it. The riskiest thing to copy is assuming the taxonomy transfers to your domain without re-validating the annotator on your problem distribution.

Bottom line: this is a genuinely well-grounded diagnostic framework that replaces surface CoT features with mathematically meaningful ones, and it produces a counterintuitive, testable result, that RL post-training narrows rather than broadens a model's strategic repertoire, plus a small but real training gain from injecting heuristics. The main caveat is that it all sits on the shoulders of a single LLM annotator and a small set of models, so treat the measurements as a promising lens to apply to your own traces rather than settled law.
