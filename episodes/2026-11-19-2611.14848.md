Tideway: Phase-Aware Tiered Memory Management for MoE Inference on Consumer Devices, by Yifei Wang, Tsun-yu Yang, and Yechen Xu, at Duke University, is a systems paper for running large Mixture-of-Experts models on a consumer PC with a discrete GPU and a modest amount of system RAM. The claim is that the three-tier memory hierarchy, GPU VRAM, host RAM, and SSD, is being managed badly by existing systems, and that treating VRAM and RAM as a single unified pool, with layouts that adapt to whether you are in prefill or decode, recovers large speedups. The headline results on an RTX 5090 with 32 gigabytes of VRAM and RAM budgets from 16 to 128 gigabytes: on average, 1.97 times faster end-to-end latency, 2.40 times faster time-to-first-token, and 1.61 times faster time-per-output-token, all against the fastest existing baseline, with end-to-end speedups reaching 3.18 times in the best configurations. The code is public, which matters for a systems paper, because the entire contribution is in the engineering.

The motivation is a real hardware regime. The paper notes that 16 to 64 gigabytes of system RAM is the mainstream configuration for the RTX 5090 population, covering 64 to 84 percent of the machines in the survey data it cites, while 128 gigabytes appears in at most 11 percent. Frontier MoE models, meanwhile, are enormous in memory: the paper tabulates weights for Gemma 4 26B at about 48 gigabytes in BF16, Qwen 3.6 35B active 3B at about 67 gigabytes, DeepSeek V4 Flash at about 155 gigabytes in FP4, GLM 5.3 Flash at about 306 gigabytes in FP8, Kimi K2.6 at about 554 gigabytes in INT4, and DeepSeek V4 Pro at about 831 gigabytes. So even a well-equipped consumer box has to spill MoE weights across all three tiers, and the question is how to move experts between those tiers without thrashing. The existing systems, llama.cpp, SSD-LLaMA, FlashMoE, treat the tiers as independent caches, and the paper's motivating measurement shows the consequence: prefill traffic from the SSD ranges from 52 percent to 99 percent of expert traffic depending on the configuration, which means the SSD, the slowest tier, is doing the heavy lifting in the phase where latency matters most.

The design has two coupled pieces. First, phase-aware memory layouts: prefill and decode have fundamentally different access patterns, prefill touches many experts in a burst while decode touches a narrow set per token, so the placement of experts across the tiers should differ between the two phases rather than being a single static assignment. The paper frames this against a static oracle that assigns a fixed 45 percent of experts to the SSD, and Tideway's adaptive placement beats it. Second, an execution engine that optimizes CUDA Graph execution under expert paging, so that the dynamic movement of experts does not disrupt the graph-based kernel launch efficiency that makes MoE decode fast in the first place. The two pieces are coupled because a layout that is good for one phase can be bad for the other, and the execution engine has to absorb the paging without stalling.

The reason the phase split matters is that the two phases put different pressure on different tiers. During prefill, the model processes a long prompt in parallel, and the routing activates a broad, relatively unpredictable set of experts at once, so the hot set is wide and the SSD, the slowest tier, ends up doing most of the serving. During decode, the model generates one token at a time, and the active expert set narrows and becomes far more predictable, so a modest amount of RAM can hold nearly all of the hot experts and the SSD can drop out of the hot path entirely. A static placement has to compromise between these two regimes, and the compromise is what leaves performance on the table: it either keeps too many experts on the SSD, where prefill pays, or it overloads RAM with experts that decode will never touch, where the memory footprint is wasted. Tideway's phase-aware allocator resolves this by recomputing the placement at the prefill-to-decode boundary, moving the working set up into RAM before decoding starts and letting the cold experts fall back, so each phase runs against a layout tuned to its own access pattern rather than a single static split.

The evaluation covers 270 fixed-shape configurations with input and output lengths from 128 tokens up to 2 thousand, plus trace-driven runs on real agent workloads, which is a broader evaluation than many systems papers of this type.

The skeptical reading. First, the baselines are the current best open systems, but the comparison is on a single GPU class, the 5090 with 32 gigabytes, and the speedup magnitudes are RAM-budget-dependent: on a machine with 128 gigabytes of RAM the spill is smaller and the win is smaller, while on a 16 gigabyte machine the win is larger but the absolute performance is still constrained by the SSD. The 1.97 times average is an average across that whole range. Second, the models evaluated are three, Gemma 4 26B, Qwen 3.6 35B, and DeepSeek V4 Flash, which is a reasonable representative set but not a sweep across MoE architectures, and MoE expert routing distributions vary enough that a placement strategy tuned on these three may not transfer. Third, the CUDA Graph optimization under paging is the part most likely to be fragile across driver versions and GPU generations, and the paper's results are on one driver stack. Fourth, the trace-driven agent evaluation is the most realistic workload, but agent traces have a particular prefill-to-decode ratio, and the speedup on other workload mixes, especially heavily streaming or heavily batched serving, is not characterized.

For implementation: if you are serving a frontier MoE model on a consumer box, the recipe is (1) model the three tiers as one pool with a unified allocator, (2) choose expert placement per phase, prefill versus decode, rather than statically, (3) keep CUDA Graphs alive across expert page moves. The practical dependency is that the phase-aware layout needs a way to measure which experts are hot in each phase, so you will want the access-frequency instrumentation the paper describes, or an equivalent profiling pass, before the placement logic can be tuned to your own model rather than the paper's three. The allocator itself is the part to build first, because the phase-aware placement and the graph-aware paging are both downstream of it, and a unified allocator that treats VRAM, RAM, and SSD as one address space with a cost model for each tier is what lets the placement and paging logic reason about where to put a given expert without hard-coding tier boundaries. The evaluation stack is TTFT, TPOT, end-to-end latency, and SSD traffic, measured across a grid of RAM budgets and sequence lengths, plus agent traces. The cheapest way to validate the core claim is to reproduce the SSD-traffic measurement on your own model and RAM budget: if your prefill is already pulling most expert traffic from the SSD, the phase-aware placement is likely to pay off; if your RAM budget already holds most experts, the win is smaller.

To summarize the bottom line. Tideway is a solid, code-available systems contribution that identifies a concrete inefficiency, treating a three-tier memory hierarchy as three independent caches, and fixes it with phase-aware placement plus graph-aware paging, for roughly 2 times average end-to-end speedups on a 5090 with consumer RAM. The limits are single-GPU-class evaluation, a three-model sweep, and driver-specific graph optimizations. If your deployment target is a high-end consumer or workstation machine running a frontier MoE model, this is worth running before you reach for a datacenter quote, and the SSD-traffic measurement is the quick first check for whether the win will land on your particular piece of hardware.
