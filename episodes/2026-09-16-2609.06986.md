Paper one is Continual Learning Mechanisms Compose for Long-Horizon Memorization, from Zheyuan Zhang, Alvin Zhang, and Daniel Khashabi, with Tianmin Shu co-advising, all at Johns Hopkins University.

The problem is straightforward to state and stubborn to solve. You take a language model and give it a stream of one hundred query-answer tasks, one at a time, by continual supervised fine-tuning. The model does not keep the raw examples from earlier tasks, and it gets no task identifier at inference time, so it has to just recognize the query and produce the right answer. After the last update, you test it on every task it has ever seen and ask what fraction it still gets right. That fraction is the final retention. The catch is catastrophic forgetting: every new update overwrites part of what the previous updates stored. The authors' baseline, naive sequential fine-tuning with no retention machinery, lands at 1.2 percent average final retention after one hundred tasks. In other words, the model keeps about one in eighty of the things it learned, on average. The best single continual-learning mechanism they test does a lot better but still only reaches 8.1 percent.

Their hypothesis is that the standard mechanisms each fight a different source of forgetting, so they should compose better than any one of them in isolation. They organize the design space along two dimensions. The first dimension is anchors, which specify what prior information each new update must preserve. There are three of them. The data anchor replays generated pseudo-sequences from earlier tasks: a frozen copy of the model from the previous step generates three hundred sequences from a single task-agnostic replay token, and each current-task minibatch is paired with a replay minibatch during training. The function anchor is self-distillation: the current model's outputs on current-task inputs are pulled toward the previous model's outputs. The weight anchor is an EWC-style quadratic penalty on parameters, weighted by an accumulated importance estimate, so parameters that earlier behavior depends on move less. The second dimension is low-rank allocation, which decides where each task's LoRA update gets kept. Shared LoRA keeps one adapter and keeps training it across all tasks. Merged LoRA gives each task a fresh pair of LoRA matrices and, after the task is done, folds that update into the dense weights before reinitializing a new adapter. Both rules keep the retained state a constant size no matter how many tasks have passed, which matters at a hundred-task horizon.

To test this, the authors build three datasets of one hundred tasks each, increasing in realism. Symbol-QA has ten thousand random key-value associations. LLM-QA has ten thousand fictional facts generated by a large model across one hundred topics. Real-QA has five thousand natural questions pulled from ten public question-answering datasets, filtered so the base model answers none of them correctly across five sampled completions. The combinatorial space of anchor and allocation choices is large, so they introduce task-level successive halving: start with ninety configurations, keep the top forty-five after ten tasks, the top twenty-three after twenty tasks, and the top ten after fifty tasks, then run those ten through all one hundred. After that they run a full factorial experiment to measure individual effects and interactions.

The headline result comes from that factorial. The best single standalone mechanism retains 4.2 percent on Symbol-QA, 7.5 percent on LLM-QA, and 12.5 percent on Real-QA after a hundred tasks. The strongest composition reaches 23.2, 41.8, and 54.8 percent on the three datasets, and the authors' recommended method, all three anchors plus merged LoRA, is the only composition that ranks in the top three on every dataset, averaging 34.9 percent final retention across the board. That is a 28-fold improvement over the 1.2 percent of naive sequential fine-tuning. The factorial main effects show where the gain actually comes from. The data anchor, meaning replay, has the largest single main effect on every dataset, adding roughly 9.5 to 19.3 percentage points. Merged LoRA is the second-largest main effect, adding 5.9 to 20.5 points. And the interaction between replay and merged LoRA is positive and statistically significant on all three datasets, and it is super-additive: the standalone gains of the two sum to only 3.9, 7.7, and 13.9 points on the three datasets, but running them together improves retention over naive fine-tuning by 15.6, 31.0, and 46.9 points. The self-distillation and weight anchors add smaller, dataset-dependent gains. The self-distillation main effect is positive everywhere, but its interaction with replay turns negative on the two natural-language datasets, and the weight anchor's interaction with merged LoRA is negative and significant on Symbol-QA, which the authors plausibly attribute to the importance statistics going stale when merged LoRA reinitializes the factors.

They also measure memory half-life, the number of additional tasks it takes for retention on a learned task to fall to half of what it was right after learning. Naive fine-tuning has a half-life of one task on the two synthetic datasets and two on Real-QA. The best single mechanism stretches that to four, six, and eleven tasks. The best composition reaches 19, 32, and 44 tasks. The composition changes the timescale of forgetting, but every curve still declines. One hundred tasks in, the model is still losing old memories; it just loses them about twice as slowly.

A few caveats deserve attention. The evaluation is memorization, not generalization: each task is tested on the exact queries it was trained on, so a model can retain the association and still fail a paraphrase. They checked general capability on held-out math and knowledge benchmarks, GSM8K, MATH, MGSM, and MMLU-Redux, and every method suffers substantial drops there after a hundred tasks, so stronger memorization did not come with preserved general ability. They also tested allocation rules whose state grows with the number of tasks, O-LoRA and sequential OSRM, and neither consistently beat merged LoRA on retention. O-LoRA did preserve more general capability on the natural-language datasets, which is an interesting tradeoff if you care about both. The replay mechanism is worth an implementation note: they do not store any earlier examples at all. They add a single replay token to the vocabulary, prepend it to every training sequence, and at each task the previous model generates three hundred fresh pseudo-sequences from that token, which are used only for the current task. It is a clean way to get generative replay without a replay buffer, but the generated sequences are whatever the previous model happens to produce, so the replay quality degrades as the model itself drifts.

My assessment: the core claim, that complementary retention mechanisms compose and that replay plus merged LoRA is the load-bearing pair, is well supported by the factorial design and the three-seed averages. What I would re-verify before adopting this in a real system is the practicality. The evaluation uses one hundred tasks of 100 or 50 examples each, and immediate acquisition is nearly perfect, which suggests the tasks are small and well-separated. Real continual fine-tuning has much larger tasks, overlapping domains, and a base model whose general ability you cannot afford to erode, and this paper shows the general-ability cost is still large. The 34.9 percent average final retention sounds impressive against 1.2 percent, but it is still a six-in-ten loss of learned associations after a hundred updates. If I were building a long-lived model that must absorb updates, I would treat merged LoRA with generative replay as a strong starting point, keep O-LoRA in mind if capability preservation dominates, and not expect the composition to stop forgetting, only to slow it down.

The bottom line: this is a careful, well-controlled study that establishes which continual-learning mechanisms compose and which don't, with replay and merged LoRA as the clear core. It does not make long-horizon memorization solved; it makes it meaningfully slower to decay, and the general-capability cost remains an open problem.
