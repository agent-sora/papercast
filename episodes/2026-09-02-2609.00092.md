Safin-1, from the Safin team at Shanghai AI Laboratory, with Ming Zhang, Kaisen Yang, and Shu Yu among the contributors. This is an architecture paper that asks whether safety can live inside a model's own computation rather than being bolted on as an external constraint.

The starting point is a distinction most alignment work quietly blurs. A language model retains context in one substrate, a token-level cache or a recurrent state, but it encodes reusable behaviors like safety in a different one, model updates, adapters, or learned prefixes imposed at inference time. The paper's claim is that these should be the same substrate. It calls the principle Safety from Within: safety-relevant capabilities should be represented and invoked through the model's native computation, not through post-hoc alignment that depends on external safeguards. The mechanism is a new architecture called MARCH, Memory-Anchor Routing across Context History, which turns the transient trajectory of a recurrent model into a growing bank of addressable states that the model can selectively read back.

The architecture solves a specific failure of recurrent models. Self-attention keeps fine-grained access to every prior token but pays a quadratic training cost and a key-value cache that grows with sequence length. Recurrent and linear-attention models compress the prefix into a fixed-size state for constant-memory decoding, but they expose only the latest state for reading, so once an earlier association is attenuated or overwritten, it is gone. MARCH fixes that by periodically checkpointing the evolving recurrent state. At boundaries placed every 512 tokens, the model snapshots its cumulative state into a state anchor and pairs it with a compact routing key. Every subsequent token routes over the causally visible anchor keys, plus a learned null option that lets it bypass retrieval entirely, and the retrieved historical readout is fused residually with the current-state path. The routing is content-conditioned, so it addresses anchors by what they contain rather than by their position, and everything is trained end-to-end under the language-modeling objective.

The safety piece is where the architecture becomes an alignment mechanism. The same routed state bank can host learned persistent capability states, parameters rather than context-derived checkpoints, that are available from the first token without consuming any input positions. The paper instantiates this as a detachable Safety State. It freezes the entire language-model backbone, optimizes only the layer-wise collection of persistent states, and places them in the same bank as the dynamic anchors, so the existing router decides, token by token and context by context, how much of the Safety State contributes. The memory can be attached or removed without rewriting the shared backbone. The training corpus is deliberately small, 1,000 harmful requests paired with safety-aligned responses and 915 benign requests paired with helpful ones, each target replicated under three context lengths, so the Safety State learns to stay effective whether it sits next to no anchors, one anchor, or a whole bank of them.

The validation is done in two stages, and the first is the honest one. At 0.8 billion parameters, the authors pretrain a matched set of recurrent models from scratch on the same 50 billion tokens, applying MARCH to three different backbones, Gated DeltaNet, Kimi Delta Attention, and Gated DeltaNet-2. This isolates the architecture from scale. MARCH wins on every general commonsense benchmark, raises the eight-benchmark average to 41.5 against 40.1 for the plain Gated DeltaNet, and, most tellingly, lifts RULER needle-in-a-haystack from 31.6 to 46.4 percent, a 47 percent relative gain on a task that directly measures the ability to recover a specific earlier fact. That is the capability the rest of the paper assumes: a model that can actually read its own history.

The small-scale study also establishes the efficiency case, which is the reason to build on a recurrent backbone at all. Because the state is fixed-size, decoding stays constant-memory, and the top-4 sparse routing keeps the retrieval cost from growing with the number of anchors. The paper measures this up to 128K tokens and reports that sparse routing more than doubles end-to-end throughput over the dense aggregation and cuts the core runtime by roughly an order of magnitude, so the memory does not tax the model the way a growing key-value cache would. That is the trade-off the whole design is selling: you get selective, content-addressed recall of your own past without the quadratic cost that full attention charges for the same thing.

The second stage scales the architecture to 4 billion and 35 billion active-parameter mixture-of-experts variants on the hybrid Qwen 3.5 backbones, with matched continual pretraining and supervised fine-tuning. The architectural gains show up where they should, on the hard reasoning tasks rather than the easy ones. At 35 billion, AIME 2025 at average over 64 samples rises from 71.88 to 80.10, and GPQA-Diamond jumps by 6.56 points, while the established benchmarks like GSM8K stay essentially flat, so the AIME gains do not come at the expense of broader mathematical capability. The same pattern holds at both scales, which is what the dense 4B and the mixture-of-experts 35B comparison is meant to establish. The routing mechanism is doing its advertised job, sustaining and recovering useful computation over long reasoning horizons without sacrificing broad capability.

On safety, the base pipeline already nudges things the right way, cutting average jailbreak attack success rate by a point at each scale. The Safety State is the headline. With the backbone frozen, the detachable persistent state reduces average jailbreak attack success rate by 42.3 percent at 4 billion and 52.3 percent at 35 billion, and the paper's central safety claim is that it does so while producing substantially less over-refusal than a training-matched rank-8 LoRA control trained on the same data with the same optimizer and the same number of updates. That comparison is the fair one, because a LoRA is the standard tool for exactly this kind of specialization, so if the routed state is going to be preferable, it has to beat the LoRA, and that is what the paper sets up to test.

The implementation detail that makes this practical at length is the producer-reader split. The producer inherits the hardware-efficient chunkwise kernel of the underlying recurrent mixer, and the reader tiles queries and state candidates the way FlashAttention tiles, fusing routing, normalization, and weighted readout into a streaming reduction so it never materializes the dense token-to-candidate matrix. The sparse variant restricts aggregation to the top-4 highest-scoring anchors, so the expensive matrix-valued readout is only computed for the states the router actually selected.

The idea is compelling, but the safety claim deserves more scrutiny than the framing invites. The authors are careful to call this an initial architectural exploration, which is honest, and the LoRA control is a genuinely fair comparison. But the safety gains rest on a small, five-benchmark jailbreak suite, and the 42 to 52 percent reductions are relative, so a base ASR of a few points moving to a fraction is a large relative number on a small absolute one. More fundamentally, the safety here is still trained on the same 1,915 examples any LoRA would use; the novelty is that it lives in routed state, which makes it detachable and context-aware, but it is not obviously a new safety capability, only a new substrate for an old one. The over-refusal result is the more interesting finding, because it suggests that routing the safety state lets the model invoke it selectively rather than suppressing everything, but the ORR deltas are small and the 35 billion XSTest number actually rises slightly.

The architecture itself is the more durable contribution: a clean, end-to-end-trainable way to give a recurrent model addressable memory, with a strong 0.8 billion needle-in-a-haystack result that is directly attributable to the routing and a top-4 sparse path that scales to 128K. As a safety mechanism it is a promising first step that needs much larger and more adversarial evaluation to be convincing, and the paper is candid about that gap.
