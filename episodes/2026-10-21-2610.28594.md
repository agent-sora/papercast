The paper is Learning the 4D World through Point Trajectory Forecasting, from Byungwoo Jeon, Sunghwan Hong, and Dohyeon Kim, spanning KAIST, ETH Zurich, Seoul National University, New York University, and the RLWRLD lab, with senior advising from Saining Xie and Jinwoo Shin. It introduces VPM, the Video-Point Model, a world model that does something specific and testable: given a multi-view video history and a language instruction, it forecasts the future videos from arbitrary viewpoints and, at the same time, the three-dimensional trajectories of persistent scene points. The central hypothesis is that learning where identifiable points will move helps the visual predictor produce more faithful forecasts, because a shared description of motion anchors the appearance changes in every camera.

The motivation is a gap in how video world models are validated. Predicting a plausible future video does not by itself establish that the forecast describes coherent three-dimensional motion, because a single camera can be visually convincing while geometrically wrong. Multi-view histories give you the evidence to check that, because the same evolving scene has to be consistent across cameras, but only if the model actually relates the observations across those cameras to a common prediction of how the scene evolves. The authors build on prior geometric supervision for video modeling, including pointmap prediction and trajectory-derived representation alignment, and their specific contribution is to jointly forecast future images and persistent three-dimensional point trajectories, where each trajectory follows the same physical point over time and across views. That is the key difference from prior point-tracking work: they are not tracking within observed frames, they are forecasting beyond them, which means the model has to anticipate correspondences without access to future frames.

The model is built on Cosmos 3, a Mixture-of-Transformers backbone with a reasoner tower and a generator tower that share a joint attention operation. The generator is trained with rectified flow matching on future-video tokens. To handle multiple cameras, VPM packs the latent video tokens from all cameras into a single sequence on a shared temporal grid, and it distinguishes camera geometry from view identity in two ways. First, for each video token it computes the Plucker ray of its image region from the intrinsics and pose, and a lightweight encoder turns that ray into a per-token camera embedding that both adds to the token features and rotates the attention queries and keys. Second, and this is the clean architectural move, it adds a fourth axis to the rotary positional encoding. Standard three-dimensional positional encoding cannot tell apart two tokens that share temporal and spatial coordinates but come from different cameras, so they assign each token a view index and add a fourth rotary term with a learnable view frequency. Initializing that frequency to zero recovers the original three-dimensional encoding at the start of training, which means the extension is a soft, learnable addition rather than a hard re-parameterization.

The trajectory head is where the point supervision lives. It operates on the query and key features from an intermediate self-attention layer, reshaped into per-view feature grids. For each query point, it samples a source feature and computes correlations with key features around the current estimate in every view and timestep, then a transformer with attention factorized over space, time, and views refines the estimate over a number of iterations. At each timestep it predicts a two-dimensional position, a depth, and a confidence for every view, and unprojects those into the fixed reference frame. During training, the future-frame features come from partially denoised tokens rather than clean observations, so the head learns to infer motion from the model's own predictive features. There are two supervision signals. A trajectory loss, a truncated Huber loss on the two-dimensional positions and displacements and on log-depth plus a binary cross-entropy confidence term. And a cross-view correspondence loss, which treats correspondence as classification over the spatial cells of a target frame and pushes the generator's attention features to associate the same physical point across views and time. Both feed geometric signal back into the shared video representation.

Training is staged. First the generation pathway, camera embeddings, and view frequencies are adapted with the flow-matching loss on all multi-view sources, including ones without trajectory annotations. Then only the trajectory head and the view frequencies are trained with the trajectory loss, freezing everything else. Finally the video backbone and trajectory head are jointly optimized with the combined objective of flow matching plus the trajectory and correspondence losses. The dataset is VPM-Data, ten thousand training clips of synchronized multi-view video paired with persistent three-dimensional trajectories, covering tabletop manipulation and urban dynamics, and it is complemented by VP-WorldBench, an evaluation suite spanning simulation and real environments. They also build a robot-control variant where robot actions are added as a token modality in the generator and denoised jointly with the future videos.

The results support the hypothesis in the way a good ablation should. The modality-wise ablation is the core evidence: adding point trajectories yields the largest gains in both video fidelity and three-dimensional motion accuracy, and adding actions on top of trajectories performs best on all splits and metrics. On the benchmarks, the video-plus-trajectory variant improves over the video-only counterpart on both video quality and trajectory accuracy, and it is the point supervision specifically that closes the multi-view consistency gap, because the video-only model trails a geometry-aware baseline while the video-plus-trajectory model surpasses it. For robot control, the action-enabled model with trajectories achieves the best success rates on all the simulation suites and the highest success rate on every real-robot task, and the gain over the model without trajectories is consistent. They also show a zero-shot, training-free result where predicted trajectories are lifted to three dimensions by triangulating the views and converted to joint-space actions through inverse kinematics, demonstrating that the forecast trajectories alone carry actionable information.

There is a clean mechanistic story behind why the trajectories help the video, not just the geometry. They attribute the action gain to three properties, and the first is the most important: on robot data the trajectories are computed from joint states by forward kinematics, so they describe the exact same motion as the actions but grounded in three dimensions and in every view at once, which teaches the backbone to connect the low-dimensional action channel to the full spatial motion. The second is that the trajectories are dense, hundreds of points per timestep rather than a single low-dimensional action, so they carry far more information about where things are going. The third is that object trajectories, where available, specify where the manipulated objects should end up, which encodes the goal directly. The correspondence loss is doing real work here too, because it is the term that pushes the shared attention features to associate the same physical point across views and time, which is what lets the trajectory head read motion off the generator's own predictive features rather than needing a separate geometry module.

Where I would be skeptical before adopting this. The headline claim is that point supervision improves visual forecasting, and the ablation does show that, but the magnitude of the visual gain is modest next to the motion gain, so the practical value is concentrated in the geometric consistency and the robot-control transfer rather than in raw video fidelity. The data pipeline is a real dependency: the three-dimensional trajectories for robot video are extracted from known CAD geometry and forward kinematics, which is clean supervision in simulation but does not transfer to the real world without that geometry, and the authors acknowledge that the geometry used to construct the supervision can be unreliable. The benchmarks are largely their own, VP-WorldBench, so the cross-benchmark generality is partly self-defined. The engineering gotcha is that the view-axis positional encoding and the Plucker camera embeddings have to be tuned together, and the staged training order matters, because the joint optimization stage is where the video backbone absorbs the geometric signal and doing it before the trajectory head is stable will not reproduce the numbers. It is a solid, well-controlled contribution to four-dimensional world modeling, and the persistent-trajectory-as-shared-motion-supervision framing is the part worth borrowing for any multi-view forecasting model you build.
