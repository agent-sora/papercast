Reading the Unreadable, by Jaeseong Lee, Jinwoo Kim, and Jinho Jeong, at CIPLAB at Yonsei University, asks a question that sounds simple but turns out to be a genuine blind spot in image super-resolution: can a model read text that is too degraded to read from the pixels alone? The setting is text-aware image super-resolution, which tries to recover a sharp high-resolution image from a blurry, low-resolution input while keeping any text in the scene legible. The core claim is that the existing pipeline treats this as a pure visual restoration problem, but the humans doing the reading are not. When a distant sign is nearly illegible, a person does not read it from the local pixels. They read it from the surrounding context, from the pattern of the numbers, from prior knowledge of a brand name or a street they have seen before. That reasoning is exactly the part the models are missing.

The paper builds two diagnostic tools to make the point measurable. The first is a benchmark called ReasonText, a human-curated set of low-resolution to high-resolution image pairs in which each text instance is assigned a difficulty level. Level 1 instances are readable from the text region alone. Level 2 instances can only be read once the annotator is shown the surrounding context and asked to reason. Level 3 instances are unreadable even with context and are excluded from scoring. The second tool is a metric called Ground-Truth Text-Crop Accuracy, which runs an optical character recognition model directly on the ground-truth text regions of the super-resolution output, bypassing the text detection step that contaminates most existing evaluations. The result of applying both tools to twelve recent super-resolution models is the paper's central finding, and it is unambiguous: every single model's accuracy drops sharply on the reasoning-required text, regardless of architecture, training paradigm, or whether the model was explicitly designed for text.

<!--C1-->

The size of the drop is the striking part. The lowest Level 1 accuracy across the twelve models, at 45.7 percent, is still higher than the highest Level 2 accuracy, at 24.5 percent. In other words, the ranges of the two levels do not overlap at all. A model that is good at reading text it can see, but cannot read the text that requires reasoning, is not a marginal gap, it is a wall. The authors note that the ground-truth high-resolution image, which is the oracle, reaches 90.7 percent on the reasoning-required text, so the gap is not because the text is genuinely unreadable, it is because the models cannot reason their way to it. The result holds regardless of the architecture, whether the model is based on a generative adversarial network, a diffusion model, a few-step diffusion, or an autoregressive approach, and it holds even for the models that were explicitly built to be text-aware. That last point is the one that matters, because it means adding text modules, which is what the field has been doing, does not close the reasoning gap.

The remedy is built from the two observations the paper starts from. The first is that a text-conditioned super-resolution model, one that takes a caption as input, restores text much better when the caption actually contains the correct transcript of the text in the image. The second is that a modern multimodal large language model can read the degraded text from the low-resolution image through reasoning, even when the super-resolution model cannot. The proposed method, called Reasoning Transfer via Captioning, or RTC, wires these two together. An external multimodal large language model looks at the low-resolution image and infers what the degraded text says, and writes that inference into a caption, which is then fed to the text-conditioned super-resolution model as its conditioning. Both models stay frozen, so the whole thing is training-free, and it can be attached to any text-conditioned super-resolution model without modifying it. The results are consistent and, in one case, surprising. RTC improves the text restoration accuracy for all six text-conditioned models, and the gains are larger on the reasoning-required text, which is exactly where the models were failing. For one of the models, the reasoning-required accuracy jumps from 24.5 to 45.8 percent, which is a large absolute gain on the hardest category. The general image quality, measured by the perceptual metrics, stays stable, so the method is not buying text accuracy at the cost of the rest of the image. The surprising result is the reversal the title points toward. Without RTC, the text-aware model is the best of the group, which makes sense. But with RTC, the order flips, and a plain real-world super-resolution model with the reasoning plug-in beats the text-aware model with the same reasoning plug-in, even though the text-aware model was built specifically for this job. The authors' interpretation is the paper's most provocative claim: once you supply the reasoning, the current text-aware modules may be adding nothing, because the reasoning does the work those modules were supposed to do.

The honest limits are stated clearly in the conclusion. Even the best model with RTC still falls well short of the oracle on the reasoning-required text, 45.8 percent against 90.7, which tells you that most of the remaining gap is not in reading the text but in rendering it faithfully once you know what it says. The benchmark itself is modest in scale, built from real camera-captured images and limited to alphanumeric text, so it does not cover other writing systems or a wide range of scenes. And the plug-in only works on super-resolution models that already have a text channel, so it cannot be attached to a model with no text conditioning at all. None of that undercuts the core finding, which is that text-aware super-resolution has a systematic blind spot on exactly the text that is hardest to recover, and that a training-free reasoning plug-in closes a large part of the gap. The broader claim, that reasoning should be a first-class design axis for these models rather than an afterthought bolted on as a module, is the thing worth taking from the paper, and it is a claim the reversal result genuinely supports. It is worth pausing on why the result is surprising enough to matter. The whole research direction of text-aware super-resolution has been built on the assumption that the reason plain super-resolution fails on text is that it does not attend to the text, and the obvious fix is to add a text-detection, segmentation, or spotting module that tells the model where the text is. The reversal says that once the model is told what the text says, the attention to where the text is stops being the binding constraint. That is a real reframing of where the difficulty lies, and it suggests that a lot of the engineering that has gone into text modules may have been aimed at the wrong part of the problem. The natural next step, which the authors point to, is to build the reasoning directly into the super-resolution model so that it does not need an external caption at all, but that is future work, and the plug-in stands on its own as evidence that the reasoning is the missing ingredient. The benchmark is also a small one by modern standards, a few hundred real camera-captured images focused on alphanumeric text, so the result should be read as a directional finding rather than a saturation of the problem space. The authors are honest about that scope, and the value is in the clarity of the finding: the failure is reasoning, not perception, and the fix is to supply the reasoning. Whether that framing survives a larger and more varied benchmark is the open question, but the wall between the two levels, with no overlap between them, is the kind of clean result that tends to hold even as the absolute numbers shift.
