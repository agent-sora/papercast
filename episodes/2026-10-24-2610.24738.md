The paper is Editing Images into Reusable Layers via Compositional Edit-Prior Distillation, from Wen Xue and Yong Liu at South China University of Technology and Tsinghua University, Xueheng Li at the University of Science and Technology of China, with the rest of the team spread across Peng Cheng Laboratory and JD.com, where the corresponding author works. The problem is one that anyone who has built an e-commerce content pipeline will recognize: modern instruction-driven editing models give you one flattened output image. You cannot move the text you just generated, swap the object you just inserted, or reuse anything from the result without regenerating the whole canvas and praying the unrelated regions stay put. The authors call LayerEditor a unified framework that does instruction-driven editing and RGBA layer generation in one model, and they ship a hundred-thousand-example training set, two new test sets, and a distillation recipe to keep the editing quality from collapsing when the model starts outputting layer stacks.

The core architectural move is what they call In-Context RGB-Alpha Token Modeling. Instead of treating the edited canvas and the layers as separate outputs, the model represents the edited canvas and an ordered sequence of RGBA layers inside a single token sequence, the same way modern multimodal diffusion transformers already pack text and image tokens together. Each layer is an ordered element, so the model can be told, at inference time, what content should live on which layer and how many layers to produce. A dedicated alpha decoder on top recovers continuous opacity values, which is the piece most decomposition pipelines get wrong: hard-edged binary masks instead of the semitransparent boundaries that real design assets have. The ordering matters for composition, because compositing layers in the wrong order gives you a different image even when every individual layer is correct.

The second piece is the training problem. If you take a pretrained editing model and just extend its output space to include layers, the editing capability degrades: the edited canvas gets artifacts and quality drops, because the model is now optimizing a new output distribution it was never trained on. Their fix is on-policy Compositional Edit-Prior Distillation, abbreviated C-EPD. The student, the layered editing model, runs its own denoising trajectory, and at intermediate states along that trajectory a frozen editing teacher is queried. The teacher supervises two targets at once: the directly edited canvas, and the image you get by compositing the student's predicted layers on top of the source. Distilling on the student's own states, on-policy, is the part that matters, because off-policy distillation from teacher-generated trajectories leaves a distribution mismatch that shows up exactly on the hard examples. The net effect is that the model learns to emit layers without forgetting how to edit.


One methodological detail from the training setup is worth flagging for anyone reproducing this. The distillation runs on-policy at intermediate denoising states, which means the teacher and student are looking at the same partially denoised canvas at the same noise level, and the supervision targets are computed from that shared state. That is a more expensive training loop than a standard distillation pass, because you have to run the frozen teacher at multiple intermediate states per example, and the paper's inference settings in the appendix show the layer count and content are exposed as explicit controls at generation time. The variable layer count support is not a post-hoc trick either: the ordered token sequence means the model can stop emitting layers when the content is fully covered, and the benchmarks include explicit layer-count settings where you ask for a specific number of layers and measure how well the model respects it.
The data side is where the paper earns its keep for anyone who wants to reproduce this. LayerEditor-100K is a paired dataset of about 100,000 examples, each with a source image, an editing instruction, an edited target, and the ordered RGBA layer stack underneath that target. It splits into roughly 48,300 text edits, 37,000 object edits, and 15,000 poster-level appearance edits. Text operations cover movement, recoloring, replacement, and translation; object operations cover addition, removal, movement, recoloring, and replacement. Every target carries between two and ten layers, so the model learns variable layer counts rather than a fixed stack size. For evaluation they built two disjoint test sets: Crello-Testset with 2,994 cases derived from native designs, and ScaleEdit-Testset with 1,000 cases from flattened editing pairs with recovered target layers. Both have dedicated settings for layer-count control and user-specified layer content, which existing benchmarks simply do not test.

The headline results on Crello-Testset are a clean sweep, and I want to read you the numbers because the margins are not small. The strongest flat-output competitor, Seedream5-Pro in its joint setting, scores a composite SSIM of 0.7902 and a composite FID of 55.33, with an alpha IoU of 0.5139. LayerEditor scores a composite SSIM of 0.8351, a composite FID of 33.86, an RGBA L1 of 0.0350 against 0.1344, and an alpha IoU of 0.6074. The rubric scores, which are human-style ratings from one to ten, show the same pattern: LayerEditor gets 6.14 on edit consistency and 6.83 on layer segmentation, versus 4.14 and 5.22 for the best Seedream variant, and the decomposition baselines like FireRed plus Qwen-Image-Layered sit around 1.5 to 5 on those columns depending on the variant, with their alpha accuracy mostly under 1.5. On ScaleEdit-Testset the picture repeats: LayerEditor at 0.8839 composite SSIM and 39.48 composite FID, alpha IoU of 0.6802, while Seedream5-Pro joint is at 0.8365 and 59.62 with alpha IoU 0.6583, and the decomposition stacks fall to alpha IoU values between 0.42 and 0.49. The qualitative claim, rendered from the predicted RGBA stacks rather than independently generated canvases, is that the recompositions are coherent and the layers are individually manipulable.


A few limitations are stated or implied that bear on how far you can push these numbers. The benchmarks are design-asset oriented, Crello-style posters and scaled editing pairs, so the layer structure being recovered is the layer structure a human designer would plausibly build, with text blocks, object groups, and decoration layers. That is a strong prior for the target distribution, and it means the model is being graded on reproducing an organized layer hierarchy rather than on the harder question of which layering of an arbitrary photographic edit would be most useful downstream. The paper does not report latency or cost for the layered generation pass against the flat pass, which matters in practice because the ordered RGBA token sequence is a longer output than a single canvas, and the on-policy distillation training loop multiplies teacher forward passes. Neither is disqualifying, but both are the kinds of engineering numbers you want before wiring this into a production editing stack, and I would plan on the layered path being meaningfully slower per edit than the flat path.
So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the dataset and benchmark construction: paired supervision with ordered layers, variable layer counts, and test sets built independently from the training source are exactly the right call, and the rubric plus objective split is honest. What I would re-verify is the competitive frame. Seedream5-Pro is an API model accessed through platform restrictions, and it failed to produce usable outputs on five Crello documents in both settings, so its numbers are computed on the remaining cases only. The decomposition baselines are assembled pipelines, FireRed-Image-Edit plus Qwen-Image-Layered with various control and fine-tuning variants, and some of those variants, like the plain FR plus QIL control setting, look almost broken, with alpha IoU of 0.12 and rubric scores in the low teens over ten, which flatters the comparison. The alpha IoU column is also the one where a strong dedicated decomposition model would be the real competitor, and I would want to see it against current state of the art on layer decomposition before betting a product on the numbers. The C-EPD distillation recipe is the part I would actually adopt even if I never shipped the full model: distilling a frozen editor onto the student's own denoising states, supervising both the canvas and the composition of the layers, is a clean fix for output-space extension that generalizes beyond RGBA.

Bottom line: LayerEditor is a credible unification of editing and layer decomposition, with the margins on its own test sets being large enough to matter, and the C-EPD distillation trick is worth stealing regardless. Before adopting it for production, re-run the comparison against a dedicated state-of-the-art layer decomposer and your own API editing model, because the current leaderboard is assembled from variants that include some quite weak baselines.
