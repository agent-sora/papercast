Is Complex Agentic Memory Always Needed? Naive RAG Is Stronger Than We Thought, by Zhengye Bao, Shengtao Zhang, and Haokai Zhang, from Xi'an Jiaotong University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Zhejiang University, and the Chinese University of Hong Kong.

Long-term conversational agents have a memory problem. The conversation history grows unboundedly, so systems build memory layers on top of raw history: they summarize, mutate, and reorganize the stored text into memos, knowledge graphs, or hierarchical stores, then learn retrieval strategies over those structures. A whole category of recent work, often called agentic memory, treats the memory store as something the agent actively edits. This paper asks the blunt question: is any of that complexity actually helping? Their baseline is deliberately dumb. They call it Raw-RAG. You take the raw conversation history, chunk it, embed the chunks with a dense retriever, rerank the top candidates, and feed the retrieved chunks to a reader LLM. No summaries, no memos, no learned write policy, no structural transformation. Just retrieval over the unedited text.

The benchmark suite is broad. They evaluate on LoCoMo, LongMemEval, MEME, and MemoryAgentBench, paired with several reader models, for seven benchmark-model settings. In six of the seven settings, Raw-RAG ranks first among the compared memory systems, beating the strongest agentic memory system by anywhere from 0.13 to 5.53 points. The one setting where it does not lead, full-context beats Raw-RAG by 19.98 points, which is the expected ceiling effect: if your context window can hold the entire history, no memory system needs to do any work. The margin over agentic memory varies by benchmark. Raw-RAG leads LoCoMo by 0.65 to 0.95 points, LongMemEval by 0.13 points, MEME with GPT-4o-mini by 2.24 points, and MemoryAgentBench by 4.48 to 5.53 points. The biggest margins are on MemoryAgentBench, the benchmark most focused on long-horizon memory reasoning, which is a somewhat uncomfortable result for the complexity camp, though the paper is honest about it.

The cost story reinforces the accuracy story. On the BEAM-1M dataset, a one-million-token conversation corpus, they measure cumulative sequential ingestion time. Raw-RAG ingests in about 0.3 hours. The compared systems range from 3.2 hours to 27.0 hours, which the paper expresses as 10x to 83x the ingestion cost. If the transformed memory store does not buy you accuracy and costs an order of magnitude or more in ingestion time, the economic argument for the raw baseline is strong. For a production system ingesting millions of conversations, that difference is not academic.

The second half of the paper is the mechanistic explanation, and it is the part worth reading carefully. They decompose memory performance into coverage and utilization. Coverage is the fraction of necessary evidence items that the retrieval step actually surfaces. Utilization is the fraction of retrieved evidence that the reader LLM actually makes use of. Raw-RAG has the highest coverage on both main benchmarks, 45.29 percent and 76.35 percent, while the compared systems vary more widely. The utilization gap is smaller: across systems it spans only 80.00 to 89.71 percent on MemoryAgentBench. The paper's claim is that coverage differences dominate. Useful evidence in long conversations is not just the answer value. It also includes the relations and dependency paths needed to derive the answer. The evidence they identify includes answer values, the relations between facts, and the derivation paths that connect a current question to historical context.

The write-decision analysis is a nice negative result. They instrument the agentic memory systems and count what fraction of write operations are actual mutations, meaning persisted text-changing updates or deletions, versus no-ops. On LoCoMo, with 33,902 write decisions, 78.48 percent are no-ops and only 17.13 percent are adds. Updates plus deletions together account for 4.39 percent. On LongMemEval, with 428,858 write decisions, 73.04 percent are no-ops, adds are 24.51 percent, and updates plus deletions are 2.46 percent. So the elaborate write policy that these systems learn is, in practice, doing almost nothing most of the time. There is a secondary implication here that the paper does not spell out: if 95 percent of write operations are no-ops, then the learned write policy is mostly learning when not to act, which is a much weaker claim than learning to curate memory. The value of the policy is concentrated in the small fraction of decisions where it does mutate, and the paper's coverage analysis suggests that even those mutations do not improve the evidence that reaches the reader.

The full-context comparison deserves a careful reading. Full-context wins its setting by 19.98 points, which is large, but the setting pairs a long-context reader with the entire raw history in the prompt window. That is not a memory system at all, it is a context-window solution, and it only works because the benchmark conversations fit. The honest framing is that Raw-RAG is the best you can do with a retriever when the history does not fit in the window, and the gap to full-context is the price of compression. For deployments where histories are bounded, the full-context result is the real baseline to beat, not the agentic memory systems. The paper also tests whether giving a reader model complete access to a transformed memory store closes the gap to raw evidence. It does not. Systems with full access to the transformed stores land 7.58 to 29.38 points below raw evidence, with all paired 95 percent confidence intervals excluding zero. The transformed representation itself loses information, independent of retrieval quality.

The ablations on the Raw-RAG side are instructive. Returning one item instead of the default number of retrieved chunks lowers total accuracy by 11.73 points, with a Holm-adjusted p-value of 0.0012. Returning five, ten, thirty, or sixty items stays within 1.3 points of the baseline. Top-120 loses 14.57 points, driven by long-range understanding and conflict resolution, so there is a real cost to retrieving too much. Chunk-size sensitivity is mild. Atomic-fact extraction as a preprocessing step reduces test-time learning from 47.68 percent to 15.37 percent, a 19.62 point collapse, which is a direct demonstration that decomposing raw text into atomic facts breaks the dependency structure the reader needs.

The retrieval configuration study has a practical shape to it: there is a flat plateau between roughly five and sixty retrieved items, and the failure modes on either side are different. Too few items and the reader simply lacks the evidence; too many items and the reader is overwhelmed by irrelevant history, which degrades exactly the long-range and conflict-resolution capabilities that the memory task is testing. For a production system, the flat plateau is good news: the retriever is not a sensitive hyperparameter in the middle of the range, and you can tune the number of retrieved items to your latency budget without a meaningful accuracy penalty. The coverage-versus-utilization decomposition also has a design consequence. If your system's failures are coverage failures, the fix is in the retriever, better embeddings, better chunking, or returning more candidates. If your failures are utilization failures, the fix is in the reader or the prompt that presents the retrieved evidence. The paper's data suggests the former is the common case for current memory systems, which is a cheaper fix than most teams assume, because it does not require retraining anything.

The skeptical read: the baseline is strong but it is still a single-retriever baseline, and the comparison set is the memory systems the authors chose to compare. If a future memory system designs its transformations to preserve dependency paths rather than compressing them, the result could reverse. The benchmarks are all conversation-centric memory benchmarks. The paper does not test settings where the history is genuinely enormous, beyond what even a large-context reader can handle, and where a curated store is not a nicety but a requirement. The 0.13 point margin on LongMemEval is within noise for most practical purposes, and the paper leans on the aggregate across settings. The write-decision statistics come from the compared systems' own instrumentation, so they describe those systems' policies, not a proof that mutation is never useful. The strongest claim the paper actually supports is narrower than the title: on current conversation-memory benchmarks with current reader models, none of the published agentic memory transformations beat naive dense retrieval plus reranking over raw history, and the ingestion cost of the transformations is an order of magnitude higher. If you are building a conversational agent with long history and you are considering a custom memory pipeline, the responsible baseline to beat is a reranked raw-history retrieval, and the bar for justifying your complexity is higher than it was last year.
