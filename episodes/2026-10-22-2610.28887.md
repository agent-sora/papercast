The paper is Building AI Research Teams for Autonomous Discovery of Linear Attention Architectures, from Yujian Liu and Li An at UC Santa Barbara, Zelin He at Penn State, and Jacob Helwig, Rohit Jain, and Siyu Zhu at LinkedIn, with Shiyu Chang as the senior author. This one sits at the intersection of two standing interests, because it is both a multi-agent research-team design and, as its concrete test case, the discovery of a new linear attention architecture, which is a topological change to the core memory mechanism of a language model. So it gets an episode on both counts. The premise is that autonomous research agents can now propose ideas, modify code, and run experiments, and that in a mature field like linear attention the search can get stuck in a local minimum because all the agents share similar reasoning patterns. Their answer is to build the team out of researchers with genuinely different disciplinary backgrounds, so that the exploration is orthogonal rather than redundant.

The system has four load-bearing components. First, disciplinary grounding: each of the sixteen research agents is initialized with a different profile containing domain knowledge, mathematical tools, and a way of approaching problems, so a researcher from signal processing reasons differently from one from optimization. Second, persistent private histories: each agent keeps its own research log, separate from the shared evidence, which matters because the paper finds that accumulated private experience changes what an agent proposes even when the shared evidence is identical. Third, shared experimental evidence: all agents see the same results, so one researcher's finding can seed another's next experiment. And fourth, adaptive allocation of research opportunities: because experiments are expensive and the team can only test a limited number of ideas, the system decides which researcher gets to propose and run the next experiment, balancing exploration against exploitation, rather than giving everyone a fixed turn. The ablations are the real argument, and they are clean. Remove the disciplinary backgrounds and the search plateaus early. Remove the private histories and the researchers make less progress despite identical shared evidence. Replace the adaptive allocation with uniform selection and the team underperforms within the same budget. Each organizational piece earns its keep.

The concrete discovery is Context-First DeltaNet, or CFDN. They start from Gated DeltaNet-2, GDN2, a recent linear attention baseline that stores information in a recurrent matrix and retrieves it through a query-dependent read. The team's search, tracked across iterations, climbs from a utility of zero point seven one five at the starting point through intermediate architectural changes, a recurrent context, a static address origin, a normalized correction, and a matrix-first variant, to the context-first arrangement that becomes CFDN at a utility of one point zero four five. The architectural difference is specific: CFDN adds a recurrent context vector that contributes the same additive term to every query, and it changes the update order, correcting the context vector first and then updating the matrix using the error that remains, whereas GDN2 applies a channel-wise log-decay to the matrix and does the correction in the other order. The context vector has no passive decay, which is a deliberate asymmetry. The compute cost is essentially identical, CFDN costs about one point one five billion FLOPs per token against GDN2's, so this is not a speed tradeoff; it is a same-budget architecture change.

They then scale the strongest discovery from one hundred and fifty million to one point three billion parameters, and at that scale CFDN outperforms GDN2 on language modeling, common-sense reasoning, and both synthetic and real-world retrieval tasks. The real-world retrieval suite is held out of the search feedback, used only for final evaluation, which is the right discipline. They do not claim the method found a better architecture than a human team could; they claim it found one faster, within a controlled budget, and that the ablations isolate which organizational properties drive the speed.

The budget control is where the experimental design is strongest, and it is worth detailing. The candidate architectures are FLOP-matched against the baseline at both scales. At the search scale, GDN2 costs about one point one five billion FLOPs per token and receives six thousand four hundred fifty-three training updates; CFDN costs the same per-token FLOPs, within a rounding difference, and receives six thousand four hundred fifty-two updates. So the comparison is not a bigger model beating a smaller one; it is two different recurrent update rules spending the same compute. The baseline itself is a serious one: Gated DeltaNet-2, a recent linear attention design that decouples the erase and write paths in the delta-rule update, with separate full projections for queries, keys, values, and channel-wise erase and write gates. The search also uses a pace-based selection mechanism rather than a fixed number of evaluations: a candidate must reach one-quarter, one-half, three-quarters, and the full amount of its own compute allocation within one point two times the corresponding baseline elapsed times, which means slow-to-train architectures are naturally pruned and the team's budget is measured in progress per unit time, not just gradient steps. The FLOP accounting is documented precisely enough to reproduce: ordinary token-local scalar and vector processing is excluded from the count, projections and the memory computation are included, and custom operators are counted at three times the standard forward cost as a uniform approximation. At the one point three billion scale, the retrieval evaluation is split deliberately: a synthetic needle-in-a-haystack suite with all cells weighted equally enters the search feedback, while a real-world retrieval suite is held out and used only for final evaluation of the scaled models, so the search cannot overfit to the real benchmark. The reasoning evaluation is zero-shot, with no chat template and no context repetition, which measures the raw capability of the update rule rather than the prompting layer around it. The paper also documents model configurations, training hyperparameters, data preparation, and the FLOP-matched budget allocation in a reproducibility appendix, and reproduces one complete researcher profile, so the sixteen agents' initialization is inspectable rather than a black-box ensemble. They are explicit about one thing: their safeguards do not guarantee correctness or novelty, which is a fair framing for a search system. The skeptical part. The utility score is a composite the authors defined, blending NLL, a reasoning accuracy, and retrieval, with explicit tolerances for what counts as a regression, so the one point zero four five against zero point seven one five is a search-internal number, and the durable claim is the held-out one point three billion comparison, which is real but is a single scaled model against a single baseline. The ablations, while clean, are ablations of organizational components in one search run, and the claim that diversity and private memory matter could be confounded with the particular sixteen profiles they chose. The FLOPs equivalence is reassuring for cost but says nothing about whether the context vector's no-decay property is stable across training scales or whether it overfits to the one point three billion configuration. And the whole result is a single successful search; the paper does not report how often the team fails to find an improvement, which is the number that would tell you whether this is a reliable discovery engine or a well-documented lucky run.

The verdict: this is one of the more honest autonomous-research papers of the year, because it separates the organizational design, which is the general contribution, from the single architecture it happened to find, and it ablates each design choice against the same budget. If you build multi-agent research systems, the four-component structure, diverse grounding, private memory, shared evidence, and adaptive allocation, is a concrete template with the ablations to justify each piece; if you care about linear attention specifically, CFDN is a small but real same-budget improvement over GDN2 worth a look, and the context-first correction order is the detail to notice.
