Welcome back, everyone. Today's paper is "Training Chemical Plausibility-Aware Large Language Models for Single-Step Retrosynthesis," by Bogdan Zagribelnyy, Ivan Ilin, Nikita Bondarev, Maksim Kuznetsov, Mathieu Reymond, Vladimir Aladinskiy, Alex Aliper, and Alex Zhavoronkov of Insilico Medicine, with labs spanning Abu Dhabi, Montreal, and Hong Kong. It carries thirty-three upvotes on today's board, and underneath the chemistry it is making a methodological argument the broader ML community should hear: some problems are intrinsically one-to-many, and forcing them into single-answer evaluation quietly breaks both your training signal and your benchmarks.

Here is the setup. Retrosynthesis asks the question a chemist faces when staring at a target molecule: what known reaction could produce this? It is the core step of computer-aided synthesis planning, and drug discovery lives or dies on synthetic accessibility — designing a compound nobody can manufacture is designing nothing. The structural difficulty is that the answer is rarely unique: a target molecule typically admits many valid precursor sets through many different reaction types, all chemically plausible, several industrially sensible. Single-answer protocols — predict the one right disconnection, score against one ground truth — punish diversity and reward memorizing the majority route. The authors' response is Top-K prompting, used consistently at both training and inference: the model learns to emit a ranked set of plausible disconnections rather than a lone guess, which matches the task's actual geometry instead of fighting it. It sounds almost too simple, which is exactly the right reaction to have; the substance lies in how the rest of the system keeps that freedom honest.

The substance arrives in three pieces. First, scale: the team compiled CREED-CCV-two plus USPTO-XL, roughly forty-five point six million verified reactions — an ultra-large-scale corpus dwarfing the classic benchmark datasets this area usually trains on, which matters because reaction-space coverage is the resource constraint on generalization to unfamiliar targets. Second, the model itself, C3LM — Chemistry Constraint-Consistent Language Model — initialized from the LFM2 2.6B open-weight checkpoint and trained for fifty thousand steps on that corpus under the Top-K regime, then sharpened with online reinforcement learning fine-tuning using GRPO, the same group-relative policy optimization made popular by reasoning-model training. Third, the part giving the paper its name: plausibility-aware fine-tuning that integrates ChemCensor-based rewards, a chemical validity checker acting as a hard filter against molecules that violate valence constraints or other basic chemistry, alongside novelty-oriented rewards that push the model beyond regurgitating the most common routes toward exploring less-traveled but legitimate corners of reaction space. Reward the model for being chemically legal and for not always saying the obvious thing, and the one-to-many freedom becomes a feature instead of a hallucination generator. The authors are candid that optimizing against ChemCensor creates partial circularity with evaluation on ChemCensor metrics; their defense is that they are engineering practical usefulness, not clean science.

The headline result is state-of-the-art performance on URSA-expert-twenty-twenty-six, evaluated out-of-distribution — a benchmark deliberately separated from the training distribution, which is the honest way to test whether forty-five million reactions bought understanding or just better memorization. Just as interesting is the analysis behind the headline: comparing reaction uniqueness across model families shows that large language models and conventional template-based models explore largely complementary reaction spaces. The neural model proposes routes the rule-based system never considers, and vice versa; their candidate sets overlap far less than either's accuracy alone would suggest. That finding motivates the paper's forward-looking recommendation — ensemble retrosynthesis, where heterogeneous systems vote — and it is arguably worth more to practitioners than another leaderboard point, because it tells you the current generation of tools fail differently, which is precisely the condition under which combining them helps. The scoring protocol behind that headline is also more careful than most: predictions from every model are pooled, sorted by ChemCensor support against synthetic precedents drawn from public USPTO-full supplemented by the commercial Pistachio database, and the best fifteen reactant sets retained per target before metrics are computed.

Now the skepticism, and chemistry papers asking for trust carry a particular burden. First, "state-of-the-art" is claimed on one out-of-distribution benchmark from the authors' own evaluation framing; competing systems were tuned for standard splits, and until someone reruns the comparison under matched conditions the ranking is soft. Second, ChemCensor as reward and filter is itself software with its own coverage limits — plausibility-as-defined-by-your-checker is not plausibility-as-certified-by-a-medicinal-chemist, and valence-legal garbage remains a real failure class. Third, Top-K evaluation has a well-known grading subtlety: scoring any-of-top-K hits inflates apparent quality as K grows, since random guessing also accumulates credit, so the exact K and metric conventions deserve scrutiny before accepting the margin. Fourth, novelty rewards have a failure mode the paper acknowledges only implicitly — rewarding unusual routes can elicit plausible-looking nonsense that slips past the censor — and no wet-lab validation appears anywhere in the paper: zero reactions were actually run. Fifth, industrial incentives cut toward exactly this kind of claim, so replication by academic groups is not optional politeness here, it is the whole ballgame.
The scale claim deserves unpacking, because forty-five point six million is not a round-up of existing resources. USPTO-XL contributes the community's standard extracted reaction corpus, but CREED-CCV-two is the new ingredient: a compiled and verified collection layered on top, with the verification step doing quiet, unglamorous work — reaction records in public databases carry duplicates, mislabeled roles, and outright errors, and training a model to imitate chemistry from dirty data bakes those errors into its notion of plausibility. The pipeline matters as much as the count. A language model trained on forty-five million clean reactions sees orders of magnitude more reaction types, reagent combinations, and rare transformations than models raised on the hundred-thousand-scale benchmarks that defined the field for a decade, which is precisely what you want when the evaluation target is out-of-distribution molecules nobody curated.

The reward ablation gives the fine-tuning story its numbers: on their evaluation set, adding the ChemCensor reward to supervised fine-tuning lifts every Top-K metric by a few points, and stacking the novelty reward on top contributes a comparable further bump across the board — two independent rewards, each pulling its own weight rather than one doing all the work. The prompting setup is also more engineered than it sounds: fifteen diverse natural-language templates adapted from MolInstructions wrap each target SMILES, so Top-K aggregates predictions across paraphrases instead of betting on one phrasing. One more angle worth flagging: the Top-K regime quietly changes what the model is optimizing during fine-tuning itself, not just at test time. A single-answer objective actively suppresses minority-correct routes — the gradient pushes mass toward the modal disconnection even when several alternatives are equally valid, so the trained policy is systematically overconfident about route choice before it ever meets a benchmark. Training on ranked sets keeps the posterior honest throughout learning, which is presumably part of why the uniqueness analysis shows such broad candidate coverage rather than collapse onto popular reactions. There is an analogy the ML crowd will recognize immediately: this is the same argument for best-of-n evaluation over greedy decoding, applied at training time rather than bolted on afterward. The complementarity finding also quietly indicts how the field has been comparing models. If LLM-based and template-based systems explore genuinely different reaction spaces, then leaderboard rankings computed on the same fixed benchmark are measuring performance on a shared slice that flatters whichever family matches the benchmark's construction history — and the standard practice of reporting single-model accuracy actively hides the fact that a trivial ensemble would dominate either system alone. The authors stop short of building that ensemble, which is honest but leaves obvious money on the table: rank-fuse C3LM's candidates with a conventional planner's, deduplicate by product set, and you would likely clear both systems' individual out-of-distribution numbers. That nobody appears to have done this systematically before suggests the field's single-answer habits run deeper than metrics — into how training sets are curated, how baselines are chosen, and which failure modes anyone bothers to measure.
Bottom line: this is careful industrial-scale engineering that correctly identifies single-answer evaluation as the original sin of retrosynthesis modeling, and the complementarity finding alone justifies the read for anyone building synthesis tools. But treat the leaderboard crown as provisional pending independent replication, treat the absence of any wet-lab confirmation as the elephant in the flask, and steal the transferable lesson — when your task is one-to-many, train and evaluate on distributions, not points — because it applies far beyond chemistry.
