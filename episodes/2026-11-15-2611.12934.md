EarthRecord: From Pixels to Land Change with Multimodal Agents over Four Decades of Satellite Imagery, by Haoran Zhang, Huizhen Sun, and Hanbin Wen, from AIEyes Technologies, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology in Guangzhou, Guangdong University of Technology, and the University of Hong Kong, is a benchmark that tests whether multimodal agents can turn four decades of raw satellite imagery into reliable land-change analysis. The central finding is a two-layer gap: leading agents complete most of the analyses end to end, but they drop sharply when the answer requires mapping from imagery to what is on the ground, and when one satellite's data is silently altered, about half of their correct answers flip to follow the altered pixels instead of the reality.

The motivation is that existing earth-observation benchmarks do not test the part of the job that is hard. Vision-language and remote-sensing benchmarks pose questions on curated, clean images. The newer agent benchmarks hand the agent preprocessed inputs, short-term tasks, or dedicated data-fetch tools. But real land-change analysis, tracking forest loss, surface water, settlement growth, fire, begins with raw multi-decadal archives from multiple sensors, and the analyst has to screen clouds, map transitions from imagery, and harmonize measurements across sensors that disagree with each other. That is where the real work is, and it is exactly what prior benchmarks skip. There is also a second, subtler problem that the benchmark is built around: a sensor measures radiance, which depends on the atmosphere, the viewing geometry, and the instrument itself, not just the ground. The satellite record is full of famous cases where the pixels moved but the ground did not: apparent tropospheric cooling from orbital decay, vegetation browning from a degrading sensor, an Amazon green-up that turned out to be a viewing-geometry effect. So even a correct computation from the record can be wrong about the ground, and the benchmark needs a way to tell those apart.

The data is the contribution in one sense. EarthRecord pairs 56 sites with the raw record as distributed: unscaled single-band rasters from Landsat, MODIS, VIIRS, and Sentinel-2, spanning 1984 to 2026. Cloud masking, mapping, and cross-sensor harmonization are all left to the agent. That is a deliberate contrast with benchmarks that hand over preprocessed, cloud-cleared, harmonized products, because the masking and harmonization steps are where real analysts spend most of their time and where the error bars on any downstream number actually come from. There are 238 questions across ten analysis tasks, and the questions are sorted into three kinds: 153 are computable from the record, 73 ask what is actually on the ground, which requires mapping from imagery, and 12 have insufficient evidence, which is a legitimate answer the agent is supposed to be able to give. Scoring is pass at three across three passes, against withheld references drawn from global products, agreement among methods, and visual interpretation. The dataset is released on Hugging Face and the code on GitHub.

The design feature I think is the real intellectual contribution is the pair of controls. The first is the closed book, where the record is simply removed and the agent must answer from prior knowledge alone. That measures how much of what agents say about a region is really just regional priors, the geographic stereotypes baked into training data, and it is a cheap and elegant way to separate "the model knows this region" from "the model computed this from the pixels." The second is the instrument pair, where one satellite's data in a pair of sensors is altered, with nothing changing on the ground, so that the agent is forced to cross-check between sensors. A model that genuinely reconciles multi-sensor evidence should notice the disagreement, or at least not follow the altered sensor blindly; a model that just computes faithfully from whatever it is handed will follow the alteration without comment, which is exactly the behavior the control is designed to expose. In the paper's own example, a forest's greenness is asked about between 2001 and 2025. Closed book, GPT-6 Sol answers "decrease" at confidence 57, which is just the prior that western Argentine Gran Chaco has been deforested. Given the record, it computes a slope of plus 0.00017 NDVI per year with a p-value of 0.835 and correctly says no significant change, at confidence 97. But when the Terra satellite's data is altered while Aqua stays the same, it answers "decrease" again, now at confidence 89, following the altered pixels. The forest did not change. The model computed faithfully from the pixels it was given, and the pixels were wrong.

The results across twelve multimodal agents. The four leading agents score 81.0 to 85.4 percent pass at three on the 226 computable and recognition questions, which is strong end-to-end performance, and it is fair to say that current frontier agents can genuinely carry out this workflow. But the six leading models drop from 92.0 percent on questions computed from record variables to 61.1 percent on questions that require mapping from imagery. And the failure mode on the mapping questions is diagnostic: runs often abstain early, mostly without ever viewing an image, or the agent judges inconsistently across its three passes. So the bottleneck is not computation, it is the visual-to-ground step, the part where the agent has to look at pixels and say what they depict.

The controls then measure how much the answers rest on the pixels versus the ground. Providing the record lifts all twelve models on the computable questions, which shows the agents really are using the data, though regional priors persist on the recognition questions. The instrument-pair control is the sharper result: when one satellite's data is altered, 53.5 percent of the right answers turn wrong and follow the altered pixels, and very few of the failed runs attribute the breakage to the instrument. That is the headline negative result, and it is a clean measurement of a real weakness: the agents can compute from pixels, they cannot yet reconcile pixels with ground truth across sensors.

The skeptical assessment. First, 56 sites and 238 questions is a solid benchmark, but the question pool is small enough that a strong agent could be tuned to its structure, and the ten task families are fixed, so I would want to see the task generator or at least a holdout protocol before treating the 81 to 85 percent ceiling as a stable measurement rather than a first snapshot. Second, the pass at three scoring across three passes is a reliability measure, which is good, but the 12 insufficient-evidence questions are a very small slice of the benchmark, and the abstention behavior is where earth-observation agents would most need to be tested, so I would weight that slice more than its 5 percent share of the questions. Third, the instrument-pair control is clever, but the alteration protocol matters a lot: how much the satellite's data was changed, and in which bands, determines whether the test is measuring cross-sensor reconciliation or just sensitivity to any perturbation. The paper says red plus 20 percent and near-infrared minus 20 percent in the figure, which is a large change, and I would want the dose-response, what happens at smaller alterations, to know whether the 53.5 percent figure is a cliff or the middle of a slope. And the finding that few failed runs attribute the break to the instrument is interesting but underexplored in the paper; that is a calibration failure worth a paper of its own, and it is where I would push the authors next.

Bottom line: EarthRecord is the most methodologically careful earth-observation agent benchmark I have seen, and the instrument-pair control is a genuinely new evaluation primitive that any agent benchmark dealing with sensor data should copy. The 53.5 percent figure is the number to remember: today's agents read pixels well, compute from them faithfully, and still read the ground poorly, because they treat the sensor record as ground truth instead of evidence.
