LongCat-DeepResearch Technical Report, from the Meituan LongCat Team. The first named authors on the byline are He Zhu and Yue Xu, with a 31-person author list from Meituan, the Chinese food delivery and retail company that has been releasing the LongCat family of open-weight models.

The problem is open-ended deep research. An agent must investigate an underspecified question, gather evidence from diverse external sources, and synthesize a comprehensive long-form report. The hard part, which the paper states well, is that the evidence requirements cannot be fully specified before the investigation begins. As an analysis develops, emerging explanations expose missing evidence, unresolved claims require verification, and new research questions arise. The system has to establish a useful initial agenda while still allowing sections to pull in new evidence as they are written. Prior systems handle this feedback between research and writing in different ways: some interleave reasoning, retrieval, and drafting, some use an evolving report to guide retrieval, and some organize investigation through multiple perspectives before composition. The paper identifies the common failure mode of the evolving-report approach: evidence, intermediate reasoning, and draft text compete for a finite context, compression can discard information whose relevance has not yet become apparent, and repeated full-report rewrites are expensive and destabilizing.

LongCat-DeepResearch's answer is a three-stage workflow with a separation of concerns. In the planning stage, multiple planning agents explore external sources in parallel and refine an actionable research plan that the paper calls a ResearchSpec. The planning is multi-perspective: different agents take different angles on the question, and the perspectives are combined before the spec is finalized. In the investigation stage, research agents are each assigned a section of the report and investigate and draft it in parallel, gathering additional evidence in separate contexts as their analyses develop. This is the key design move: each section gets its own evidence-gathering context, so the section's evidence does not have to survive compression into a shared history. In the assembly and revision stage, a global review identifies cross-section issues, and local editors perform targeted revisions at the section level, so the system never rewrites the whole report. The report-synthesis path retains complete section artifacts through assembly and editing, and external memory and compression are treated as a separate concern for managing research history, which the paper positions against prior context-management work like MemGPT and folding-based methods.

A secondary use of the same stage interfaces is worth noting: the planning, investigation, and revision structure is also used to construct research questions, task-specific rubrics, and trajectories for the mid-training and post-training of Meituan's general-purpose LongCat models. So this is both an inference-time system and a data engine for the model line, which is a common pattern in technical reports from labs that ship open-weight models.

A second structural detail separates the evidence-gathering contexts from the writing context. Each research agent runs its investigation in a separate context, pulling in additional sources as its section's analysis develops, so a section's evidence does not have to be compressed into a shared research history. The report-synthesis path then retains the complete section artifacts through assembly and the global-to-local editing, which the paper contrasts with prior context-management systems like MemGPT, which separate working context from external storage, and folding-based methods that carry structured summaries across research attempts. In LongCat's design, compression is an explicit, separate operation for managing research history, not a side effect of the report growing too long for the context window.

The evaluation compares deployed systems, including their native research tools and budgets, against three commercial deep-research products: Gemini-DeepResearch, ChatGPT-DeepResearch, and Claude-DeepResearch. On DeepResearchBench, LongCat scores 55.25 overall, versus 54.95 for the strongest commercial system, ChatGPT, a margin of 0.30 points. The dimension scores are more informative: LongCat leads on comprehensiveness at 56.21, insight and depth at 56.48, and instruction following at 55.18, but trails on readability at 49.96, where Gemini leads at 51.51. On DeepResearchBench II, LongCat scores 51.35, the strongest of the four, beating the best commercial system by 3.17 points, with the gap concentrated in the analysis dimension, where LongCat reaches 60.69 against a field ranging from 51.15 to 52.26. On ResearchRubrics, LongCat scores 79.83, a 5.62 point lead, driven by explicit requirements at 86.97, implicit requirements at 79.52, and synthesis at 78.92, though it trails on instruction following at 66.27 versus 74.47 for ChatGPT, and on citation quality at 65.00 versus 67.27. On Meituan's in-house benchmark, LongCat scores 76.04, ranking second among the four systems, trailing ChatGPT-DeepResearch's 76.59 by 0.55 points and exceeding Claude-DeepResearch's 61.42 and Gemini-DeepResearch's 42.49. The paper explicitly notes that the point estimates use the scored cohorts listed in the appendix and do not establish statistical significance.

The development-set analyses are the honest part of the report. Combining planning perspectives helps, but further planning refinement has mixed effects, which is the kind of result that does not make the abstract. Additional editing improves average automatic readability preference across two benchmarks, but with different trends on each, meaning the editing stage is not uniformly beneficial. The paper also notes that the scored cohorts used for the point estimates are listed in the appendix, and that the margins, in particular the 0.30-point lead on DeepResearchBench and the 0.55-point deficit on the in-house benchmark, are within the range where cohort selection and judge model choice matter.

Here is the skeptical read. First, the margins on the public benchmarks are small: 0.30 points on DeepResearchBench is within the noise the paper itself acknowledges, and the in-house result, where LongCat loses to ChatGPT by 0.55 points, is the comparison that matters most to the authors and is also not a win. The dimension scores tell the real story: LongCat is strongest on depth and comprehensiveness and weakest on readability and, in one benchmark, instruction following and citation quality. The readability gap is consistent with a system that prioritizes evidence coverage over prose polish, and if you are generating reports for human readers rather than downstream models, that tradeoff is visible. Second, the comparison is system-level and as-deployed, which means the commercial systems' budgets and tooling are not fully specified, and a 0.30 point margin is not a claim of superiority. Third, the in-house benchmark is self-defined, so the second-place result should be read as context, not evidence. The paper's most defensible claim is the 3.17 point lead on DeepResearchBench II's analysis dimension, which is where a section-parallel, separately-sourced architecture should plausibly pay off.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the architectural separation: planning that produces a compact spec, section-parallel investigation with per-section evidence contexts, and section-level editing that avoids full-report rewrites. That decomposition addresses a real cost and stability problem in deep-research systems, and the multi-perspective planning plus per-section contexts are the parts I would want to adopt even in a different stack. Before relying on the benchmark claims, I would re-verify with the exact scored cohorts from the appendix, look at the readability gap on my own report evaluations, and check the wall-clock and tool-call budgets, because the paper compares as-deployed systems without a full budget table in the main text. The engineering gotcha is the cross-section editing stage: targeted local revisions that must keep citations and section interfaces consistent is where this class of system accumulates bugs, and the mixed results from further planning refinement suggest the planning stage has diminishing returns that you will hit in your own tuning.

LongCat-DeepResearch is a competitive open deep-research system whose section-parallel architecture shows its biggest, most plausible edge in analysis depth, with a readable but honest set of small margins against the top commercial products. Treat the 0.30 and 0.55 point gaps as parity, the analysis-dimension lead as the real finding, and the readability weakness as the tradeoff you are signing up for.
