ChronoReplay: One Answer Interface Is Not Enough for Streaming Spatial Intelligence, by Yihao Hu, Yitong Lin, and Pan Wang, from Westlake University, Zhejiang University, and the University of Science and Technology of China, with the remaining authors spanning Singapore Management University, Fudan University, East China Normal University, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University. All three named authors are listed with equal contribution, and two of the senior authors are marked as corresponding.

The problem is a failure mode the authors call interface lock-in, and it is one you will recognize from any multi-task fine-tuning job. Train a spatial vision-language model to answer which side an object lies on, and you are not really forcing it to preserve the metric displacement that determines the answer. Train it instead to estimate a distance, and the categorical relations may stay essentially unconstrained. So when post-training emphasizes one readout, the gains pile up on that one interface while transfer to the others is weak, or even negative. The paper's claim is that this is a supervision-design problem, not a data problem, and its fix is to replay heterogeneous spatial supervision as chronologically ordered episodes, keep the source-native targets intact, and balance the loss mass across supervision families.

The method is the interesting part. They build what they call a causal replay pool from the training data, and the core idea is that every spatial target gets associated with the causal context under which its meaning is defined. A categorical decision, a metric estimate, a trajectory, and an evolving-state judgment all draw from the same observed prefix, but each one carries its own native target format. They do not serialize everything into one token stream by default; the canonical setting keeps the native formats, and a separate control setting does serialize the answers so they can measure what that serialization does to the distribution of gains. The replay is chronological, which matters: they run a shifted-order control where the same episodes are presented in a different temporal order, and chronological order beats that control by about two point four one OST points averaged across two paired seeds. The loss balancing is explicit too. They report the serialized weight of the supervised turns and set the categorical, numerical, and trajectory loss masses to be equal so that no single family dominates the gradient.

The training setup is concrete and worth repeating because it is what makes the comparison fair. They use a cosine learning-rate decay with three percent warmup, run on eight GPUs for the headline checkpoint, and they verify robustness by retraining the three-benchmark mean on four GPUs and reproducing it within point three four points. The preservation family, which holds the metric supervision, is thirty three thousand one hundred eighty single-turn examples. The maximum sequence length, maximum processed pixels, and optimizer details are all tabulated, and the relative trajectory mass is a single hyperparameter they sweep. The whole point of the design is that you are comparing supervision regimes under the same observation access, not under different frame budgets.

On the numbers. Under a fixed observation access, a single ChronoReplay checkpoint improves all three benchmarks together, OVO-S-Bench, OST-Bench, and UCS-Bench, including a thirteen point four one point gain on OST-Bench. The canonical model lands at fifty three point eight nine on OVO-S, sixty three point nine three on OST, and fifty two point seven seven on UCS, which is the highest three-benchmark mean of fifty six point eight six among the supervision settings they compare. That last number is the headline, because it is the one that shows the transfer is real rather than a reshuffle of points between benchmarks. The same-data controls are where I would actually focus, because they separate the ingredients. Answer serialization redistributes gains between OST and UCS. Family-balanced learning beats ordinary joint supervised fine-tuning and also beats natural turn weighting. And the chronological ordering adds the two point four one point advantage over the shifted-order control.

The ablations do the honest work of attribution. They decompose the gain into a training effect and an access effect. Training improves all four OVO-S levels, including the hardest one, level four, by five point zero one points. Adaptive access, which changes which frames the model sees at inference time while keeping the trained model fixed, adds most on the easy level, plus eight point six six points, and also helps level four by two point one three points, using about eighty point five frames on average. They also do a paired-seed replication: across three paired training seeds the mean paired gains are point nine two on OVO-S, one point four three on OST, and one point one seven on UCS, with small standard deviations. The sensitivity analysis is worth a mention because it shows the result is not riding on one hyperparameter. Setting the replay weighting alpha to point five gives the highest observed OVO-S-OST mean of sixty one point two zero versus sixty zero point nine two at alpha one, so the optimum is interior, not at a boundary.

One more detail on the method that earns its keep. The query in this setting is not a single frame but a scope, and the model has to decide how much of its causal history is relevant to that scope. They organize the observation access into local, multi-scale, and global regimes and match the temporal coverage of the observations to the query scope. The result of that matching is an advantage of five point five eight points over a fixed-access baseline at approximately the same visual cost, which is the cleanest single number in the paper because it isolates one design decision, how much history to show, from everything else in the training recipe. That is the kind of ablation you actually want to see, because it tells you which knob to turn if the transfer on your own data does not show up.

Here is where I would push back, and where the skeptical read lives. The gains are real but they are modest in absolute terms, and the three-benchmark mean is an average that can hide which benchmark actually moved. A thirteen point four one point jump on one benchmark is a large single number, but the paired-seed means that back the transfer claim are in the one-to-two-point range, which is the number that should carry the conclusion. The benchmark suite is the authors' own construction plus published references, and the published scores are quoted from the benchmark papers rather than re-run, so a cross-paper comparison on the published rows is only as strong as the evaluation protocols being identical. The adaptive-access row is explicitly flagged by the authors as a system effect rather than a property of the trained weights, and I would want to see that separated more cleanly, because it means part of the reported improvement comes from an inference-time selection procedure that a reader would have to reimplement to reproduce. The serialization control is the right experiment, and it does show the gain is not an artifact of one readout, but the balanced-family weighting is a design choice the authors selected, and I would want a broader sweep before trusting that the equal-mass setting is generally optimal rather than tuned for this particular mix of families.

The bottom line: this is a clean, well-controlled demonstration that how you organize heterogeneous spatial supervision, keeping native targets and balancing families across chronological episodes, beats throwing all of it into one joint objective, and the transfer across three benchmarks is the result I would trust. If you are post-training a spatial model and your gains are concentrating on one readout, this is the specific failure it names and a concrete recipe, with paired-seed numbers, for fixing it. The caveat is that the per-benchmark transfer gains are in the one-to-two point band rather than the headline thirteen, and the adaptive-access improvement is a system effect you would need to rebuild to get, so treat the three-benchmark mean as the honest figure and the single-benchmark jumps as the marketing.
