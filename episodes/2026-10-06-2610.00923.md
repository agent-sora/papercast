CANOPY, which stands for Canonical Projection over Hierarchy, is from Hyojeong Yun, Jueun Kim, and Wook-Shin Han, all at POSTECH, split between the Graduate School of AI and the Department of Computer Science. It is a framework for adaptive-granularity evidence compression after retrieval in multimodal RAG, where the retrieved items are not just text but tables, images, and video. The project page is public, and the paper is positioned squarely in the information retrieval literature rather than the generation side.

The problem is a gap that a lot of multimodal RAG work papers over. Retrieval systems have gotten good at choosing the right items to fetch, and systems like UniversalRAG even route queries to modality-specific corpora and pick among predefined granularities, paragraphs versus whole documents, clips versus full videos. But choosing the granularity of what you retrieve does not determine how much of each retrieved item the reader actually needs. The useful evidence inside a retrieved item varies in extent: it might be a single sentence of a passage, a few rows of a table plus a linked sentence, or a short segment of a video. Passing whole items consumes scarce reader context and injects distractions that measurably impair answer quality. The opposite extreme, uniformly fine-grained selection, has its own failure: it strips evidence away from the surrounding context needed to interpret it, so a table row without its header sentence or a sentence without its paragraph can become uninterpretable. The paper's claim is that different regions within the same item may need different amounts of context, so a single granularity per item does not resolve the tradeoff, and what is missing is a shared procedure that adapts the retained extent region by region across heterogeneous modalities.

The mechanism has three pieces. First, CANOPY represents each retrieved item as a hierarchy, so a document is a tree of paragraphs, sentences, and spans, a table is rows and cells, and a video is segments. A node encoder, fine-tuned on gold evidence, scores each region against the query. The gold-evidence fine-tuning matters: the encoder is not scoring regions by generic relevance but by whether the region is the part of the item that actually carries the answer, which is a different objective and a harder one to learn. Second, a parent-relative refinement step compares a region's score against its parent's score to decide whether to keep the fine-grained region or fall back to the coarser parent. This is the part that lets the system retain multiple regions at different granularities within one item, and it does so without any LLM calls for node-level pruning, which is important because it keeps the compression step cheap and deterministic. The parent-relative comparison is the key design choice against an absolute threshold, because a region that is relevant but weaker than its parent is usually a detail, while a region stronger than its parent is the load-bearing evidence. Third, because compression cannot recover evidence that was never retrieved, a critic monitors the accumulated evidence and requests targeted follow-up retrieval when it judges the set insufficient. Newly retrieved items are compressed through the same hierarchy before being added, so the loop stays consistent. The critic runs at the round level rather than the node level, so the number of LLM calls scales with the number of retrieval rounds, not the number of regions.

The evaluation is the strongest part of the paper, and it is honest about its own results. They build a heterogeneous corpus of roughly 33 million items spanning text, tables, images, and video, and run five QA benchmarks over it: Natural Questions, HotpotQA, OTT-QA which mixes tables and text, MMQA which is multimodal, and LVBench which is video. The reader is Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct in an unrouted setting. The headline is that CANOPY achieves a higher average answer accuracy than the evaluated retrieval baselines across the five benchmarks, and that compression reduces the reader-input evidence tokens by 14.2 to 27.7 percent relative to the same iterative pipeline without compression, at comparable answer accuracy.

But the paper is careful, and I would point out what it actually claims versus what it does not. The ablations show that the main accuracy gains on multi-hop QA come from the additional follow-up retrieval driven by the critic, not from the compression itself. That is a result worth taking seriously, because it reframes what the system is for: the critic is the accuracy component, and the compression is the efficiency component. A pipeline that needs every token of the evidence will get the accuracy from the critic's retrieval rounds, and a pipeline that is context-constrained gets both the accuracy and the token savings. On the compression side, the EM and F1 changes from adding compression are small and, for most benchmarks, the 95 percent confidence intervals include zero, with the gains concentrated on multi-hop HotpotQA and OTT-QA, where the reader is doing the most work and has the least spare context. On routed LVBench, compression saves 33.3 percent of tokens but lowers accuracy by 1.8 points, which the paper reports without softening, and it is the clearest statement that the token-efficiency tradeoff is not free on every modality. The iteration count is also low: the pipeline runs at 2.1 rounds on average, so the critic is not adding a heavy retrieval loop, and at that round count CANOPY passes at most 28 percent more evidence tokens than a vanilla RAG pipeline while passing 46 to 88 percent fewer than UniversalRAG. The corpus construction is also worth noting for reproducibility: the 33 million items are drawn from a mix of web text, structured tables, images, and video segments, and each item carries its hierarchical structure natively, so the compression procedure never has to guess a granularity that the source format did not already provide. That property is what keeps the pruning procedure from having to re-derive structure on every query, and it is a design choice that transfers to any pipeline that already stores documents with their native hierarchy.

They also compare against the other compression approaches directly. LongLLMLingua, a prompt-compression method, saturates 1.4 to 16.5 F1 below CANOPY, and AKS, another compression baseline, spends more compute for less. The paper's framing is that modality-specific compression mechanisms exist, but there is no shared procedure that adapts granularity within an item, and CANOPY is that procedure.

Where I would be skeptical. First, the token savings, 14 to 28 percent, are real but not dramatic, and they come with the caveat that on at least one video benchmark the savings cost a small accuracy drop. Second, the reader is a single model, Qwen3-VL-8B, in an unrouted setting, so the results are conditioned on that reader's context limits and that model's multimodal competence. Third, the hierarchy construction is modality-specific in practice, even though the scoring procedure is shared, and the paper does not fully quantify how much the quality of the initial hierarchy construction bounds the whole pipeline. Fourth, the 33 million item corpus and the critic's retrieval loop add substantial systems complexity, and the paper does not report the end-to-end latency of the compress-and-retrieve loop, which is the number an engineer actually needs. Fifth, the benchmark mix, with two multi-hop text-heavy tasks carrying the accuracy gains, means the headline average is weighted toward exactly the setting where the critic's follow-up retrieval helps most, and a deployment on single-hop video questions would see a different balance.

The verdict: this is a clean, well-scoped contribution to the post-retrieval side of multimodal RAG, with the honest finding that the accuracy gains come from targeted retrieval and the compression is a token-efficiency mechanism. If you are running a multimodal RAG pipeline and your reader is choking on context length, CANOPY gives you a principled way to adapt granularity within each item and a critic that knows when to fetch more, and the 33M-item benchmark suite is a useful artifact for testing any compression approach.
