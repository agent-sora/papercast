Title: PROWBench: Do Video Models Render What the Program Specifies?
Authors: Zheng-Hui Huang, Guixu Lin, Yu-Ju Tsai
Labs: Alaya Lab
Arxiv: 2610.02205
Day: 2026-10-03
Upvotes: 50
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02205
---PROWBench, the first three authors are Zheng-Hui Huang, Guixu Lin, and Yu-Ju Tsai, all from Alaya Lab, with Kaipeng Zhang and Zhixiang Wang as the corresponding leads.

The premise is that the world-model race has been optimizing the wrong axis. We have a pile of video world models, models that generate playable-looking game scenes, and a pile of benchmarks that grade them on visual quality, aesthetic score, motion smoothness, and whether they roughly follow a text instruction. But none of them answer the question that matters for actually using these models as game engines: does the rendered video match the program that generated the ground truth? If the engine says a gate opens between second zero point nine seven and second two point zero seven, and the player walks through it, does the generated video show the gate opening at that time, at that place, with the player passing through? That is a much finer question than plausibility, and it is the one this benchmark targets. The authors frame programmable world models as separating executable dynamics from visual generation, and argue that visual adherence to explicit rules and interactions is the piece that has gone unevaluated.

The benchmark is built on a clean idea that makes the whole thing checkable. They construct 170 programmatically generated episodes, with 600 proxy videos covering diverse scenes and interactions, and they log entity states and timestamped events as replayable world records, including events that happen outside the camera's field of view. From those records they can render synchronized views and proxy representations, and they can render each camera view in different representations, coarse three-dimensional renders, colored oriented bounding boxes, and so on. The key move is that the engine's own record becomes the ground truth, so a generated video can be scored against the observable consequences of a known program execution, not against a human's vague sense of whether it looked right. The benchmark covers first-person and third-person perspectives, with synchronized multi-view observations for a subset.

The metrics are the second clean part. Two are VLM-based. Interaction Success Rate asks whether a registered interaction is actually completed, requiring both the prescribed action and the expected end state. Logic-Render Alignment asks whether the video realizes the engine-defined event logic, depicting each prescribed action in its corresponding time segment. They also report IoU-weighted variants, so an event only counts fully if it happens with the entity in the right place, which stops a model from getting credit for the right event in the wrong location. On the entity-control side they report box IoU and a normalized box-center error, on the camera side rotation and translation error, and a camera multi-view consistency term, plus the usual quality metrics, imaging, aesthetics, warping, smoothness, and a CLIP text-alignment score. So the benchmark separates the question into fidelity to the program, fidelity to the geometry, and raw visual quality, and scores all three. The paper's worked example makes the distinction between the two VLM metrics concrete. In a recorded episode, an entity walks to a gate, the gate slides open between second zero point nine seven and second two point zero seven, and the entity walks beyond it, with the engine logging each of those as a timestamped event. The bad case is a video where the gate visually opens, so a naive plausibility check would pass it, but the timing does not line up with the logged segments, so Logic-Render Alignment fails on the misaligned segments even though the event looks present. The good case shows the same action in the right temporal window, and the metrics pass. That is the whole point of scoring against the program's own record rather than against a sense of plausibility, the failure is a timing and placement error that a quality metric would never flag.

The evaluation has two tracks and four settings. The main track generates five-second clips in two settings that differ in the appearance reference. Verified-FF gives the model an RGB first frame, 40 scene-camera pairs, 31 third-person and 9 first-person, so it is tested on entity control given an appearance. The RGB first frame is itself part of the benchmark, it is generated with MiniMax-H3 from the colored bounding-box proxy and the scene description, checked against the recorded person count, positions, facing, and layout, and every method that accepts a first frame receives this identical image, so the comparison is on control, not on appearance quality. Unverified-FF gives only the world state, the text, and the control signal, 90 pairs, 78 third-person and 12 first-person, forcing the model to jointly solve entity control, appearance generation, and identity binding, because there is no ready-made appearance to anchor to. The challenge track is harder. Long-horizon uses ten 30-second recordings and tests whether entities that leave the field of view reappear at the prescribed location and state, with a reappearance IoU and a state-persistence metric that classifies the returning person as walking or standing from the post-return frames. Multi-view uses ten four-player scenes, each with four synchronized third-person views generated independently, testing whether independently generated views agree on where each participant is, who they are, what they look like, and the scene structure, which is the consistency question that a multiplayer game actually has to answer.

They evaluate 11 models in three families. Camera-conditioned world models like EchoWM, SANA-WM, LingBot-World 2.0, and AlayaWorld receive a first frame, the text, and the camera trajectory, explicit camera control but no entity trajectories. Proxy-video-to-RGB models like Cosmos-Transfer2.5 and C2R translate an engine-rendered control representation into a realistic video. And video foundation models like MiniMax-H3 and Seedance 2.5, used without adaptation, take the colored bounding-box proxy as a reference.

The headline result, on the Verified-FF main track, is that the proxy-grounded models that were specifically built to consume engine control representations outperform the general video models on the fidelity axes. LynnReal-Omni, which builds on MiniMax-H3 and extends it with native support for heterogeneous visual controls including editable 3D renders and game recordings, is the strongest on the program-fidelity metrics, with an entity IoU of 0.457, an interaction success rate of 0.809, and a logic-render alignment of 0.852, well ahead of the general video foundation models on the same axes. That is the central finding: the models that ingest the program's own proxy signals track the prescribed events better than the models that only see a proxy video, which is exactly the axis the benchmark was built to measure. The gap narrows on the raw quality axes, where the general video models remain competitive, which is the honest tension in the result, you can be more faithful to the program or more visually polished, and the current field has not yet found a model that is both. Across the two method classes there is a consistent split: camera-conditioned world models and proxy-to-RGB models differ mainly in how much geometry they are handed, and the ones that receive more of the program's structure, depth, segmentation, or the colored bounding boxes, do better on entity placement, while the video foundation models that see only a reference proxy clip trade placement accuracy for stronger aesthetics and motion smoothness. The long-horizon and multi-view challenge settings push all of the models down, because maintaining one world state across entities that leave and re-enter the view, or keeping four independently generated player views mutually consistent, is a different and harder capability than matching a five-second event, and the spread between the best and worst models widens there, which is where the benchmark is most diagnostic.

The caveats. This is a benchmark, and its ground truth is only as good as the engine records it, so if the engine's event logging has gaps, the scores inherit them. The proxy-to-RGB models are partly trained on engine data like the gunfight scenes for one of them, which is a legitimate training-data advantage and not a pure generalization result, and the paper flags that MiniMax-H3's involvement in curation means its own results should be read with that bias in mind. And 170 episodes across four settings is a real but not exhaustive sample of game dynamics, so the leaderboard is a first snapshot, not a settled ranking.

To sum up, this is a benchmark that moves the world-model conversation from plausibility to verifiability, it builds ground truth from the executable program itself, and it shows that the models designed to consume that program's proxy signals are the ones that actually render what the program specifies, while the general video models still win on polish. For anyone building playable world models, the practical lesson is that fidelity to the specified event timeline is a distinct capability you should measure, and right now it is not the same thing as a high aesthetic score.
