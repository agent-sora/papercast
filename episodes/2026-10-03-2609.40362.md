Title: Multimodal Flow: Unified Flow Modeling of Language and Vision in Embedding Spaces
Authors: Hongyuan Tao, Xinggang Wang, Lianghui Zhu
Labs: Huazhong University of Science and Technology; Beijing Jiaotong University; Horizon Robotics
Arxiv: 2609.40362
Day: 2026-10-03
Upvotes: 20
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40362
---Multimodal Flow is from Hongyuan Tao, Xinggang Wang, and Lianghui Zhu, working across Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Beijing Jiaotong University, and Horizon Robotics, and it is a new fully-continuous foundation-model architecture for unified language and vision.

This one qualifies for coverage on its own terms, because it is a new foundation-model architecture, not a fine-tune or an orchestration layer on top of an existing model. The question it asks is the one the unified-multimodal race keeps running into. Every current unified model that generates both text and images resolves a tension in one of two ways. The fully-discrete paradigm tokenizes the image into discrete visual tokens and predicts them like words, which is clean but pays a visual quantization tax. The concrete cost is that the image is mapped into a codebook of a fixed size, and any detail in the continuous image space that the codebook cannot represent is gone before the model ever sees it, and it is not available at inference, so the model is generating over a lossy alphabet rather than over the image. The hybrid paradigm keeps the image continuous, uses discrete language prediction for the text, and runs a separate diffusion or flow process for the image, which avoids the quantization loss but means the model is really two generative processes glued together, not one, and the text and image share parameters and context but not a single generative objective, so the claim that it is one unified model is doing a lot of work. The paper's position is that there is a third option that has been underexplored for pretraining: model both language and vision as fully continuous, so they genuinely share one generative process, one vector field, one objective, with no quantization on either side.

The architecture is a shared chunk-causal flow backbone over continuous representations. Both text and image are represented continuously, the text as continuous token embeddings rather than a discrete vocabulary, the image in a continuous visual space, and a single backbone learns one vector field over both, respecting the textual token order and the visual spatial structure. Concretely, frozen modality-specific encoders map text blocks and images to continuous hyperchunks, which are then normalized and projected into a shared space. The backbone applies a joint attention across the ordered hyperchunks, so a text hyperchunk and a vision hyperchunk attend to each other under one attention, with modality-specific feed-forward networks handling the modality-specific computation, and multimodal decoders map the generated hyperchunks back to text or images. The training scheme predicts multiple target chunks in parallel, which amortizes the cost of the flow objective, and inference generates hyperchunks sequentially. The Flow Matching objective is the same one used for continuous image generation, applied here to the text hyperchunks as well, which is the step that makes the language side fully continuous rather than a discrete next-token prediction. They instantiate it as MF-1, with variants from 0.6 billion to 1.6 billion parameters, and the main model is a 1.6 billion parameter flow backbone trained from scratch on 150 billion pretraining tokens, with all the reported results coming from the same checkpoint after 5 billion tokens of joint finetuning. The evaluation spans language modeling, image captioning, multimodal understanding, and image generation. The headline numbers, with only 150 billion tokens, are 0.821 on GenEval for image generation, 83.44 on DPG-Bench, and an average of 75.3 across VQAv2, MMBench, and POPE for understanding, for an average of 82.8 across GenEval and DPG-Bench. The scaling result is that across the 0.6, 1.2, and 1.6 billion variants, longer pretraining and larger models generally improve language modeling, image captioning, and text-to-image generation, which is the smooth scaling curve you want from a from-scratch base model. There is also a mixed-pretraining result that is worth flagging, because it speaks to the transfer question. Under the same training-token budget for the 1.6 billion architecture, mixed multimodal pretraining raises SeedBench from 31.6 to 62.4 and MMBench from 36.0 to 67.2 relative to a randomly initialized flow backbone, which is the argument that the flow backbone is not just a generation model, it learns representations that transfer to understanding. The paper's own framing is that this is competitive with unified models trained on substantially more data, and that under matched data, optimization, and parameter budgets, the fully continuous Multimodal Flow outperforms representative hybrid and discrete models.

The honest caveats, and there are real ones here. One point six billion parameters is small by foundation-model standards, so the competitive-with-bigger-models claim is a small-model claim, and it is the scaling curve that matters for whether this architecture holds up as you go to tens of billions of parameters, and 150 billion tokens is a single point on that curve. The fully-continuous text representation is the more novel and more uncertain part, replacing the discrete vocabulary with continuous token embeddings, and the paper reports that it works at this scale, but it is the piece most likely to change behavior as the model grows, because discrete tokens give you a hard inductive bias that continuous embeddings do not. And the understanding scores are in the mid-seventies, which is respectable but not a claim to be made at frontier scale, so this is a paradigm paper, an argument that fully continuous unified modeling is a viable pretraining target, demonstrated at a scale where it is cheap to test, not a state-of-the-art claim at the frontier. The code and model are publicly released, which is the right thing for a paradigm paper, because the whole point is whether the recipe generalizes, and that is something the community has to be able to run. One implementation detail is worth flagging because it shapes how you should read the results. The encoders and decoders are frozen, SigLIP 2 for the image representation and a T5-small text encoder with a T5-small text decoder, and both decoders stay frozen during flow pretraining. So the thing that is actually being trained from scratch is the shared chunk-causal flow backbone, and the continuous representations it operates over come from frozen pretrained encoders. That is a strong inductive prior, it means the model inherits the geometry of SigLIP and T5 rather than learning it, and it is also the honest reason the small backbone gets competitive numbers at all, the hard representational work was done by the encoders, and the backbone is learning to route between them. The task-defined multimodal sequences, where you arrange text and image hyperchunks into the order a task requires, is what lets one backbone do text-to-image, image-conditioned text generation, and the understanding tasks without separate heads, and the downstream finetuning for visual question answering and text-to-image is the transfer test that the paper uses to argue the representations carry over.

To sum up, this is a from-scratch foundation model that argues the fully-continuous route, one shared flow process over continuous representations of both language and vision, is a legitimate unified pretraining paradigm, and shows that at one point six billion parameters and 150 billion tokens it is competitive with bigger unified models and beats hybrid and discrete baselines under matched budgets. If you are thinking about unified multimodal architectures, the practical takeaway is that the quantization-versus-hybrid choice has a third option worth testing, and the public release means you can test it yourself rather than trusting a single scaling point. The way to use this paper is as a design argument, not as a benchmark to beat. The claim to hold it to is the architecture-level one: that a single continuous flow objective can carry both modalities without either side paying the other's representational tax, and the evidence for it is the matched-budget comparison and the public checkpoint, which is enough to run the next experiment, the one that takes this recipe to a scale where the scaling curve, not the single point, is what you are reading.
