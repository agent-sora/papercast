Spatial-Interactor: Learning Spatial Reasoning through Interaction with the Observable Physical World. The first three authors, two of them equal contributors, are Kaixiang Yao, Xu Wang, and Miao Pan, from the OmniAI Group at Zhejiang University, with Weishi Wang and Daniel Dahlmeier from SAP and the rest of the roster at Zhejiang.

The diagnosis comes first, and it is two sharp little experiments. The first is a frame-shuffling diagnostic. Take a video spatial reasoning benchmark, sample thirty-two frames from each video, and randomly shuffle their order before showing them to the model. If a model actually understands how the scene evolves over time, shuffling the temporal order should hurt it badly. It barely does: Qwen's three-billion and seven-billion vision-language models drop by only zero point nine and zero point eight points, with no subtask moving more than one point four. The models are not modeling temporal transitions at all; they are reading order-invariant visual cues, basically describing the scene and pattern-matching. The second experiment separates local perception from long-horizon integration. On a local interaction task, the seven-billion model scores fifty-four point seven. On a long-horizon camera-displacement task, which requires accumulating motion information across the whole trajectory, the same model collapses to eight point six. Even a frontier model, GPT-5.5, drops from eighty-eight point seven to twenty-three point nine. And when the authors feed GPT-5.5 its own generated descriptions of the local state transitions between consecutive segments as extra text, its camera-displacement score climbs back up from twenty-three point nine to thirty-five point five. The read is blunt: the models can perceive a local change, they just cannot integrate consecutive changes into a maintained spatial state.

The fix is to train on exactly the thing the models lack: state transitions, learned from interaction. An interaction trajectory is a chain of observation, action, observation, and each link gives direct supervision for the local transition, while the whole trajectory reveals how transitions depend on each other. The authors build a dataset, LSI-108K, one hundred seven thousand five hundred eighteen question-answer pairs from simulated and real interaction trajectories, organized into a three-level curriculum. Level one is passive world-state transitions: the world changes and you have to describe the change. Level two is active self-state transitions: your own viewpoint or position moves and you have to track the change in your relation to the scene. Level three is long-horizon interaction trajectories, where you must integrate many transitions in sequence.

The training is two stages. Stage one is supervised fine-tuning on levels one and two, teaching the model to model local transitions. Stage two is on-policy distillation, and it is the interesting mechanism. A teacher branch of the same model is given privileged context, segment-level transition descriptions, the kind of explicit text that the diagnosis showed helps. It supervises the student's own on-policy chain of thought on level three, so the student learns to generate those transition descriptions itself rather than being handed them. In effect, the teacher shows the student what to say about each segment, and the student practices saying it while rolling out on the real long trajectories. The dataset splits into eighty-four thousand five hundred ninety-six level one and two examples and twenty-two thousand nine hundred twenty-two level three examples.

The results, across four spatial benchmarks and multiple backbones, show the gains are consistent and the long-horizon part is where it bites. On the seven-billion Qwen backbone, overall accuracy across the four benchmarks goes from forty-four point one to fifty-one point one with fine-tuning alone and fifty-two point five with the distillation added, and the big number is the real-world interaction benchmark, which climbs from fifty-four point seven to seventy-one point three with fine-tuning and seventy-four point zero with the distillation, a jump of nineteen point three points from the base model. The smaller backbones show the same pattern in miniature, and the distillation stage, not the fine-tuning, is what drives the long-horizon gains, which matches the diagnosis that integration, not perception, was the missing skill.

The skeptical read. The two diagnostic experiments are the best part of the paper, because they are cheap, reproducible, and they establish a specific, falsifiable claim that a lot of spatial-reasoning papers quietly assume is true: that current vision-language models actually use temporal order in video, and the shuffling result says they largely do not. That is a useful negative result in its own right. The on-policy distillation mechanism is a reasonable way to teach the integration skill, and the fact that it transfers across backbones and that the gains concentrate on the long-horizon benchmark is exactly what you would predict if the diagnosis were right. What I would want before believing the magnitude is more on the data side. The dataset is built from simulated trajectories plus real ones, and the generalization from simulation to the real-world interaction benchmark is the whole claim, so the composition and the domain gap matter a lot, and a benchmark that overlaps with the training distribution would flatter the numbers. The frame-shuffling diagnostic is also a bit blunt, because a model could in principle use other temporal cues, and the small drop could mean the models have a coarse temporal signal rather than none. But as a training recipe, SFT on local transitions plus privileged self-distillation on long trajectories is a clean, reusable pattern. Verdict: a well-diagnosed, well-matched training framework that shows interaction trajectories can teach what static spatial QA cannot, with the long-horizon integration gains the most defensible result and the simulation-to-real transfer the number to interrogate.

The curriculum design is more deliberate than it first looks, and the reason matters. Level one, passive transitions, is the easiest and the most common failure: the world changes, an object moves, a door opens, and the model has to report the change. It is pure perception of state transition, no self-motion, so it isolates the skill the frame-shuffling diagnostic showed is missing. Level two adds the agent's own motion, the camera or the robot moves, and now the model has to update its relation to the scene, which is a different cognitive operation, egocentric state tracking, and the data has to come from trajectories where the observer moves. Level three is the composition, long sequences of both, where the real test is not any single transition but the accumulation, holding a spatial state across many steps and using it to answer a question about the trajectory as a whole. The dataset construction draws from simulated environments, where the ground-truth state is exact and can be used to label transitions, and real interaction trajectories, where the labels are harder and the domain gap is the whole point. The split, roughly four to one in favor of level one and two, reflects that local transition modeling has to be solid before long-horizon integration can build on it.

The on-policy distillation stage deserves a careful look, because it is where the paper's cleverness is, and where its assumptions hide. The privileged teacher is the same backbone, but conditioned on segment-level transition descriptions that the student does not have access to during its own rollout. The teacher reads those descriptions and produces a chain of thought; the student, rolling out on-policy on the real trajectory without the privilege, is trained to match the teacher's reasoning. The effect is that the student internalizes the habit of narrating its own local transitions as it goes, and that narration is precisely the extra text that the diagnostic showed lifts GPT-5.5's long-horizon score. So the method is, in one sentence, teaching the student to do for itself what the privileged trace does for a frontier model. The training details are standard, a single epoch of reinforcement with a rule-based reward that is mostly task accuracy plus a small format term, standardized within each rollout group, which is a GRPO-style setup applied to reasoning over trajectories. The gains concentrate on the real-world long-horizon benchmark, which is the strongest evidence that the method is teaching integration rather than overfitting a benchmark, because that is the column the diagnosis predicted would be weak and the one that improved the most.

The honest limitations are the domain gap and the diagnostic's bluntness. The simulation-to-real transfer is the load-bearing claim, and if any of the real benchmark shares structure with the simulated training trajectories, the long-horizon gains are partly memorization of the distribution rather than learned integration. The frame-shuffling result, a small drop under random reordering, is strong evidence against temporal modeling but not proof of none, because a model could retain a coarse sense of order from cues the shuffling does not break, and the authors do not probe that. The method is also tied to a specific recipe, privileged self-distillation with a rule-based reward, and whether the same integration skill transfers to a different training signal, or to a model that does not have a clean teacher branch to condition, is open. As a reusable pattern, though, the diagnosis-plus-curriculum-plus-privileged-distillation structure is clean enough to port.
