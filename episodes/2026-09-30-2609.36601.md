SAKI, Maximal-Coupling-Routed Teacher Supervision for On-Policy Distillation. The first three authors are Miteto Wei, Xiaohan Wang, and Zehao Chen, from Meituan, with a collaboration with the KTH Royal Institute of Technology.

The setting is on-policy distillation, or OPD: a small student model is trained on its own generated trajectories, with a larger teacher model providing token-level supervision at the prefixes the student actually visits. This reduces the train-test mismatch that plagues fixed-trajectory distillation. The problem SAKI targets sits inside the teacher-guided variant of OPD. A weak student initially visits prefixes the teacher would rarely generate, so the teacher's supervision lands on states that are unrepresentative of the teacher's own behavior. A prior method, TRB, addresses this by rolling out from a teacher-guided behavior distribution constrained by a student-centered KL trust region. But TRB keeps the same per-prefix reverse-KL objective everywhere: every token gets the same kind of supervision, regardless of how much the student and teacher disagree at that position. SAKI's observation is that the rollout mechanism itself, when realized through maximal coupling, produces a natural routing signal, and that signal should be used to allocate supervision.

The method has three pieces, and they are tightly coupled. First, the behavior policy. At each decoding step, the student distribution and the teacher distribution define a geometrically interpolated distribution, which the paper calls the geometric bridge. At every position, the system picks the largest interpolation coefficient that keeps the KL divergence from the student within a trust region. At zero trust region the behavior policy is the student itself; at a large enough trust region it is the teacher. This is the same construction as TRB.

Second, maximal coupling. To sample exactly from that interpolated distribution while keeping track of where the student's proposal survived, SAKI uses maximal coupling. The student proposes a token. The proposal is accepted with probability equal to the minimum of one and the ratio of the guided distribution's probability of that token to the student's. If accepted, the rollout takes the student's token and the correction indicator is zero. If rejected, the system draws a correction token from the positive residual distribution, the part of the guided distribution the student under-weights, and the correction indicator is one. Two propositions carry the theory. The first says the final output has exactly the guided marginal distribution, and the correction probability is exactly the total variation distance between the student and the guided distribution. The second says maximal coupling achieves the minimum possible intervention probability over all couplings with those marginals. A corollary, using Pinsker's inequality, shows the trust-region radius directly upper-bounds the correction probability: the same single hyperparameter controls both how far the rollout distribution drifts from the student and how often the rollout must override a student proposal.

Third, the supervision routing. Accepted positions retain the standard sampled-token reverse-KL update, the log-ratio advantage times the log probability of the sampled token. Correction positions switch to a different objective: the system queries the teacher at the same prefix and takes the negative log-likelihood of the teacher's top token. The residual correction token determines the subsequent rollout prefix, while the teacher's top token determines the local parameter update. So the same coupling process controls both trajectory construction and supervision routing, and the supervision is sparse and stochastic by construction: corrections happen with probability equal to the total variation distance, which is small when the student and teacher agree and grows where they conflict.

The implementation detail worth implementing yourself is the engine-resident speculative verification. Exact teacher-guided rollout requires the teacher's distribution at every visited prefix, which is expensive. SAKI amortizes this with speculative block verification inside the inference engine, with exact residual correction and a commit-or-rollback step at the first rejection. This preserves the exact trajectory distribution and the coupling semantics while giving a 4.22 times matched-workload speedup over an external-loop implementation. That is a systems contribution with a direct training-cost payoff.

One empirical detail ties the theory to the runs. Appendix J.2 reports the correction trajectory under the annealed trust-region schedule: the observed correction probability stays below the Pinsker upper bound at every step, and it vanishes as the trust region reaches zero, exactly as the corollary predicts. That is a useful invariant to monitor in your own implementation, because a correction rate that tracks the theoretical bound tells you the coupling semantics are intact, while a rate that drifts above the bound usually means the speculative verifier has broken the exact-q distribution.

The evaluation is on seven mathematical reasoning benchmarks, including MATH-500, HMMT, AIME for 2025 and 2026, AMC 2023, Minerva, and an Olympiad set, with two student scales, 1.7 billion and 0.6 billion parameters, distilled from a 4 billion-parameter teacher. The main table reports Mean@8 and Pass@8. Relative to the teacher-guided baseline TRB, the 1.7 billion student improves from 27.9 to 29.0 on Mean@8 and from 44.6 to 47.5 on Pass@8. The 0.6 billion student improves from 17.2 to 18.4 and from 33.6 to 35.6. Relative to the SKD baseline, the gains are larger: 4.5 and 6.3 points for the 1.7 billion student. Mean@8 improves in 13 of the 14 student-benchmark pairs over TRB.

The ablations do real work. Placement controls compare correction-triggered routing against two alternatives with the same supervision budget: random placement of the teacher-mode objective, and TV-weighted placement, where positions are chosen proportionally to their total-variation conflict. Correction-triggered routing beats both, which is the paper's central empirical claim: the realized coupling event is a better router than a static conflict measure. A fixed-prefix analysis shows the teacher alignment is persistent across training, and the gains over random placement grow at higher initial student-teacher conflict, which is exactly the regime where a weak student needs the most help.

The skeptical read. The gains over TRB are real but modest in absolute terms: roughly 1 point on Mean@8 and 2 to 3 points on Pass@8. The paper's own framing is that SAKI improves the matched teacher-guided baseline, and it does, consistently, but the headline comparisons against plain OPD are inherited from the teacher-guided setup rather than contributed here. The 4.22 times throughput number is matched-workload, so it is the right comparison, but the external-loop baseline is the authors' own implementation, not a production system. The theory is clean and the propositions are the kind that survive contact with practice, because maximal coupling's minimal-intervention property is a fact about couplings, not about this particular training setup. The engineering cost is nontrivial: you need the teacher's full distribution at visited prefixes, speculative verification inside the engine, and the commit-rollback logic, and the paper uses the exact GRPO hyperparameter recipe from the baseline, which helps make the comparison fair but means the method's hyperparameter sensitivity is not independently characterized.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the routing principle: reusing realized intervention events to place expensive supervision is a generally useful pattern, and the placement controls are the right way to demonstrate it. Before adopting, I would re-verify the 1.7 billion results with the named teacher, measure wall-clock training time end to end including the speculative verification overhead at my own batch sizes, and check the Pass@8 versus Mean@8 gap, because Pass@8 gains can come from variance reduction rather than capability, and the two metrics tell different stories here. The gotcha is the trust-region schedule: the correction probability anneals as the trust region changes, and getting the schedule wrong either starves the correction signal or floods the student with teacher-mode supervision it cannot absorb.

SAKI is a precise, well-theorized refinement of teacher-guided on-policy distillation, with the routing signal coming for free from the coupling mechanism that was already in the rollout. The gains are consistent across models and benchmarks but small in absolute terms, so treat this as a quality-of-training improvement rather than a capability unlock.
