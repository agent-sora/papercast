MathForm: Scaling Mathematical Autoformalization with Knowledge Retrieval and
Verification-Guided Refinement, by Lushi Pu, Weiming Zhang, Xinheng Xie, and a
longer co-author list, from ModelBest and Tsinghua University.

Autoformalization is the step that sits between a mathematician's prose and a
proof assistant's formal language: take a natural-language statement, like
"for every even number greater than two there exist two odd primes that sum to
it," and produce the corresponding Lean 4 declaration, with the right types, the
right definitions from Mathlib, and the right quantifier structure. The paper's
opening observation is that this is not really translation in the linguistic
sense. It is a mapping from informal concepts onto a specific library's type
hierarchy, and the difficulty is that the library is huge, the naming is
idiosyncratic, and the model has to know which of a hundred near-synonymous
definitions the source statement actually intends. Getting that wrong does not
produce an error message in the same way a typo does; it produces a statement
that type-checks but means something subtly different from the original, which
is the failure mode that matters most for downstream proof automation.

The framework they build, MathForm, addresses this in two phases, and the
structure is the part worth understanding. Before generation, a retrieval
planner queries Mathlib for the relevant definitions and any existing
formalizations of closely related statements, and feeds those into the
formalization generator. This is the knowledge-retrieval half, and the point is
that instead of relying on the model's parametric memory to remember which
Mathlib definition corresponds to which informal term, the system looks it up.
The retrieval planner does this in a structured way: it first identifies the
mathematical concepts in the source statement, then issues queries against the
Mathlib index for each concept, and finally assembles a context window from the
top-ranked definitions and any structurally similar formal statements it finds.
The assembled context is what the generator sees, so the quality of the
retrieval directly bounds the quality of the generation. After generation, the
second phase is verification-guided refinement: the generated Lean statement is
handed to the compiler, the diagnostics come back, and the model is given both
the compiler output and a semantic-consistency signal, which is a separate
check that the formal statement actually says the same thing as the informal
one. The model then revises. This is an iterative loop, not a single-shot
generate-and-filter, and that distinction is the
paper's methodological claim. The data-construction pipeline that most prior
work uses, they argue, filters single-pass outputs and throws away anything
that does not type-check on the first try, which means the training data is
biased toward the easy cases and never teaches the model how to repair a hard
one. By contrast, the iterative refinement loop in the data-construction
pipeline produces statements that required multiple correction rounds before
they passed, so the training set contains harder examples than a single-pass
filter would allow, and the model is exposed to the kind of statement that
takes several passes to get right rather than only the ones that are right on
the first attempt.

The dataset they produce from this pipeline is FormalVerse, a Lean 4 corpus of
roughly three hundred and sixty-seven thousand verified examples, across a range
of mathematical domains and source types. The scale of the dataset is the second
pillar of the contribution, because the retrieval-plus-refinement pipeline only
pays off if there is enough verified material to train on, and three hundred and
sixty-seven thousand is large enough that the supervised fine-tuning stage is not
starved for signal. The composition matters as much as the count: the examples
come from diverse sources, so the model is not overfit to a single notation
convention, and they are all verified, meaning each one passed both the type
check and the consistency check before it entered the training set, which is
what makes the training data clean rather than merely large. The training recipe
is supervised fine-tuning on FormalVerse followed by reinforcement learning, and
the result is MathForm-8B, an eight-billion-parameter model. The choice of
reinforcement learning after the supervised stage is worth noting, because the
verifiable reward in this setting is the compiler: a formalized statement that
type-checks and passes the consistency check is a positive signal, which gives
the RL stage a clean, non-hallucinated objective that does not require a reward
model. The headline numbers are Pass@8
averages across six benchmarks: eighty-eight point zero-six percent under a
syntax-check gate, which only requires the statement to type-check, and seventy-
two point three-seven percent under a consistency-check gate, which additionally
requires the formal statement to mean the same thing as the informal source.
They report that this outperforms several specialized autoformalizers at thirty-
two billion parameters, which is the scale comparison that makes the result
interesting, because it says the retrieval-plus-refinement pipeline is buying
back a lot of the gap that raw model size would otherwise fill. On the harder
subsets they name specifically, the FATE-H and FATE-X benchmarks, the
consistency-check pass rates are sixty-three percent and thirty-seven percent,
and they claim both exceed the strongest specialized baselines on those two.
The consistency check itself deserves a sentence of explanation, because it is
the less standard of the two gates. It is not just asking whether the Lean
statement type-checks; it is asking whether the formal statement and the
informal source statement are semantically equivalent, and the way the system
approaches that is to treat the consistency check as its own model-based
judgment, which can flag a statement that is well-formed Lean but that has
silently dropped a quantifier, swapped a definition for a near-neighbor, or
changed the domain of a variable. That is a stricter bar than the syntax check,
and it is the one that a downstream prover actually depends on, because a
type-correct statement about the wrong proposition is useless to a proof
search that is trying to prove the proposition the mathematician actually
stated.

Now the skeptical read. The consistency-check number is the one to interrogate,
and the gap between the syntax-check and consistency-check rates tells you how
much of the apparent success is just "produces valid Lean" versus "produces Lean
that says the right thing." A seventy-two percent consistency pass rate at
Pass@8 means that in roughly a quarter of cases, none of the eight samples is
both type-correct and semantically faithful, and for a downstream proof
automation pipeline that consumes these formalizations, a semantically wrong
statement is worse than no statement, because it will produce proofs of the
wrong thing. The retrieval component is doing a lot of work, and its quality is
only as good as the retriever's ability to surface the right Mathlib definition
from among near-names; the paper reports the end-to-end gain but the ablation
that isolates retrieval from refinement is what you would want to read carefully.
The FATE-H and FATE-X claims are the most specific, and the most testable, so
they are also the ones a reader should reproduce first, because if the
consistency-checker used to score the baseline is stricter or looser than the
one used to score MathForm, the comparison is not clean. And the scale
comparison against thirty-two-billion specialized models is a single point on a
trade-off curve: retrieval adds inference cost, and the paper's framing is that
this cost is worth it, which is a reasonable claim at eight billion but would
need re-examination if the baseline were a much larger general model rather
than a specialized one.

Bottom line: this is a well-structured contribution to the autoformalization
problem, with a clear two-phase design, a substantial verified dataset, and a
scale comparison that says retrieval plus verification-guided refinement can
partially substitute for raw model size. The practical value is also worth
stating plainly: for anyone building a pipeline that feeds natural-language
mathematical statements into a Lean 4 prover, the question is no longer whether
the model can produce valid Lean, it is whether the Lean it produces means what
the mathematician meant, and this paper's consistency-check gate is the
specific mechanism that addresses that second, harder question. The open
question is how much of the reported gain is the retrieval, how much is the
refinement loop, and whether the
consistency-checker used for scoring is strong enough to make the consistency-
check numbers a meaningful gate rather than a generous one.
