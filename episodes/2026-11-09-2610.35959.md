The paper is called "AgentBaton: Efficient Model Collaboration in Agentic Tasks." The first three authors are Junlong Li, Jindong Jiang, and Yonggan Fu. The work comes from the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, with project leads at NVIDIA and a corresponding author at UIUC.

The question the paper asks is a cost question about long-horizon agentic tasks. Reliable performance on these tasks usually comes from expensive, capable frontier models available through cloud providers. At the same time, cheaper efficient models are emerging, lower-tier cloud models and smaller open-weight models you can run on local hardware, and they are worse but far less expensive. The paper asks whether a capable model and an efficient model can collaborate within a single long-horizon task to cut cost substantially while keeping task accuracy. This is a different question from the more familiar query-level routing, where you route each individual prompt to a model. Here the collaboration happens inside one task that takes many steps.

The mechanism they study is model handoff, where one model takes over from another partway through a task. They isolate two design choices. The first is handoff direction. You can go from efficient to capable, starting cheap and escalating, or from capable to efficient, starting with the strong model and handing off to the cheap one. The second is context transfer, how the interaction history moves to the receiving model. Transferring the full history directly lets the new model understand prior progress and reuse existing ideas, but it lengthens the prefill sequence at the handoff point and keeps a long input around for the rest of the run, which has a cost. They compare these two transfer methods, in-context transfer and a summarized or compressed variant, across the handoff directions.

The central empirical finding is an asymmetry, and it is the most useful thing in the paper. Efficient-to-capable handoffs generally underperform running the capable model alone, with little or no cost reduction. That makes sense: the cheap model struggles early, and by the time you escalate to the capable model you have spent the budget on the weak phase without having saved anything. Capable-to-efficient handoffs at a suitable stage of task progress, by contrast, largely preserve or even improve accuracy while cutting cost significantly. The intuition the case studies support is that the capable model often does the hard, creative, or exploratory work up front and establishes enough of the correct trajectory that the remaining steps are more mechanical, so a cheaper model can finish them. The handoff direction and the best context-transfer method both vary across model pairs, so there is no single universal setting.

From that finding they build AgentBaton, which is a lightweight learned detector for when to hand off. The capable model starts the task, and after each turn AgentBaton evaluates the progress and predicts whether the current point is a good handoff point, based on recurring patterns it has learned in the agent's execution history. Once it predicts a suitable handoff point, the efficient model takes over and continues until the task terminates. The name is a metaphor for a relay baton pass: the lead model carries the work to the right moment and hands it to the cheaper runner. It is a one-time handoff, not repeated switching back and forth, and the detector is deliberately small so it does not add much overhead of its own.

The trigger decision itself rests on a few learned signals rather than a fixed rule. The detector looks at progress, which measures how the task state is evolving turn by turn, at context transfer, which assesses how well the accumulated state can be picked up by the receiving model, and at a cost-awareness threshold that keeps the handoff economical rather than firing at the first plausible moment. Because the detector is trained on execution history from model pairs that have already been run, and it uses no task-specific information at inference time, it is general in the sense that you do not write a new handoff rule for each task. But that generalization is learned from a finite set of pairs, which is exactly where the transferability question bites, and it is the weakest link in the deployment story.

The evaluation runs across 11 capable-to-efficient model pairs and two agentic benchmarks, Terminal-Bench 2.1 and DeepSWE version 1.1. On average, AgentBaton reduces API costs by 65.9 percent while largely preserving task accuracy, with accuracy gains of up to 8.8 percentage points over running the capable model alone. So in some configurations the collaboration is not just cheaper but actually more accurate, which is the interesting part, because it suggests the capable model's early work sets up the later steps in a way that the capable model alone, running the full trajectory without a structured handoff, does not always achieve. They also run the method across a range of reasoning-effort settings, low, medium, high, and maximum, and report that AgentBaton extends the empirical accuracy-cost Pareto frontier, meaning that for a given cost it reaches at least the accuracy of the single-model baselines and often better.

The reasoning-effort sweep is worth unpacking because it is where the Pareto claim comes from. The capable source model can run at different effort levels, and the efficient target model, in their examples a large open-weight model like Qwen or a cheaper cloud model like a lower-tier GPT variant, can too. The paper plots accuracy against cost per task for each combination, and the AgentBaton configurations, where the effort settings are tuned to the handoff, form a frontier that the single-model baselines do not dominate. That is a stronger claim than "it saves money," because it says there are cost-accuracy operating points you simply cannot reach with any single model at any single effort setting. The practical implication is that if you are already spending money on a capable model, the cheapest way to get more accuracy per dollar is not to run it longer, but to run it to the right point and hand off.

Now the skeptical read. The framing is clean and the asymmetry result is the kind of finding that is genuinely useful for anyone budgeting agentic workloads. But a few caveats matter. The two benchmarks are coding and software-engineering agentic tasks, so the finding that the early phase is the hard phase and the later phase is finishable by a weaker model may be specific to that task shape; it is not obviously true for tasks where difficulty is spread evenly or front-loaded differently. The cost figure of 65.9 percent is an average across 11 pairs, which hides how much the savings vary, and the 8.8 point accuracy gain is a maximum, so the typical accuracy delta is the number to look for in the per-pair table before you trust the headline. The handoff detector is trained on execution history from particular model pairs, so transferring the detector to a new pair or a new task distribution is an open question the paper does not fully resolve. The in-context versus summarized transfer comparison is interesting but the best choice varies by model, which means adopting this is a per-pair tuning job, not a set-and-forget deployment. What I would trust is the directional result, that capable-first with a well-timed one-time handoff to a cheaper model beats both efficient-first and capable-only on cost, and often on accuracy too, on coding-style agentic tasks. What I would re-verify is the per-pair cost and accuracy spread, whether the detector generalizes across model pairs without retraining, and how the method behaves on task families where the hard work is not concentrated in the opening turns.

The bottom line: AgentBaton turns a simple observation, that strong models do the hard part early and weak models can finish, into a learned one-time handoff that roughly halves API cost while holding or improving accuracy on agentic coding benchmarks. It is a practical, low-overhead cost-reduction technique, and its limits are the task-shape and per-pair tuning assumptions behind the headline numbers.
