RSIHub: A Composable Framework for Agent-Evolution Methods, by Zimu Wang, Xiaobo Wang, and Siyu Ye, from UC Berkeley, the University of Science and Technology of China, Douyin Group, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Tongji University, the Beijing Institute for General Artificial Intelligence, and Simple Agent Lab.

There is a growing family of methods that improve agents by evolving their prompts, skills, tools, or orchestration in response to execution feedback: AHE, Hyperagents, A-Evolve, GEPA, and a long tail of similar systems. The practical problem is that every one of these methods ships with its own experimental machinery. Each paper rebuilds the same scaffolding from scratch: a workspace, an execution loop, an evaluator, checkpointing, failure recovery, and bookkeeping that ties a result to the candidate that produced it and the conditions under which it was evaluated. The result is that comparing one evolution method against another is hard, because the comparison is confounded by the harness, and nobody can inspect what candidates a run actually explored. RSIHub is a framework that factors this out. It is open source, and the contribution is the factorization: a composable substrate on which evolution methods are recipes rather than monorepos.

The design is a split between recipes and the framework core. A recipe binds one implementation to each enabled operator interface, declares the mutable surface, and specifies the run configuration: the target agent, the evaluator, the runtime, and the data splits. The mutable surface is the key concept. It is the set of resources the evolution policy is allowed to modify, prompts, skills, tool definitions, orchestration logic. Everything else, notably the evaluation itself, stays outside the mutable surface. That separation is what makes the comparison protocol honest: the evolution policy can change the agent, but it cannot change how the agent is scored. The framework core then supplies the parts every method needs: execution of candidates, evaluation against the declared evaluator, recovery from failed runs, and lineage, a persistent record of every candidate, its parent, its mutation, and its evaluation outcome. A framework-managed evolution loop invokes the selected operators to choose the next candidate from the lineage, run it, score it, and record it.

The lifecycle is git-like, and that is a deliberate analogy. The verified archive stores parent commits, evaluation records, and artifacts with provenance, so the candidate history of a run is a versioned log you can diff. At each iteration, the loop selects a candidate to extend, gathers evidence from its behavior, proposes and evaluates a revision, and writes the outcome to the archive. At each iteration boundary, the framework validates and snapshots the active recipe before running the guarded lifecycle. The target definition, evaluator, runtime, data splits, and mutable surface stay fixed across the run; surface enforcement, protected evaluation, and verified archive writes all remain under framework control, not recipe control. There is also an outer agent that can revise the recipe's operator bindings and policy settings between iterations, using permitted feedback from completed iterations. That outer loop is how you would tune the evolution policy itself without touching the protected surfaces. The design point to internalize: recipes own what gets mutated, the framework owns how mutation is executed, scored, and recorded. When those two concerns are entangled, as they are in most single-paper implementations, a bug in the harness can silently rewrite what the method is actually claiming to have optimized.

To show the factorization is real, they instantiate four existing methods, AHE, Hyperagents, A-Evolve, and GEPA, without modifying the framework core. Each method's specific logic lives in its recipe and operators; the shared substrate is untouched. That is the strongest possible demonstration that the abstraction is expressive enough, because it is the methods themselves, not toy examples, that are being ported.

The four methods being ported span different strategies, which is what makes the comparison interesting. AHE and Hyperagents are on the agentic side of the family, evolving an agent's tools and orchestration from execution feedback. A-Evolve and GEPA are on the prompt and textual-component side, where the feedback loop revises language-model prompts and other textual artifacts. All four share the recurring lifecycle, select a candidate to extend, gather evidence, propose a revision, evaluate it, retain the outcome, which is exactly the lifecycle the framework's guarded loop implements once. The evaluation uses two seed agents, MiniSWE and Codex, so the framework's claims are not tied to one underlying model family, and the fixed model and evaluator configurations mean that when a method's score moves, it is the method's evolution policy doing the moving, not a swap of the seed agent or the scorer.

The evaluation protocol is where the framework pays off. They evaluate the four instantiated methods with two seed agents, MiniSWE and Codex, on two benchmarks, Terminal Bench 2 and tau-3 Banking, under fixed model and evaluator configurations. The benchmark splits are worth describing because they are designed to catch overfitting. Terminal Bench 2 has 89 tasks, split into 50 train, 19 gate, and 20 sealed. Tau-3 Banking, the knowledge-grounded tau-Banking domain, has 97 tasks, split into 50 train, 20 gate, and 27 sealed. The train trajectories provide the evolution feedback, the gate tasks support candidate admission, and the sealed tasks are excluded from mutation feedback entirely, used only for held-out reporting. One trial per task, pass@1 reported. That three-way split, with sealed tasks the evolution loop never sees, is exactly the discipline that most agent-evolution papers skip, and it is built into the protocol rather than left to the reader.

The headline result is modest, and that is the honest part of the paper. The largest observed overall pass@1 gain across the settings is 13.5 percentage points. But the more important finding is a negative one: method rankings vary across settings, and optimization gains do not always transfer to held-out tasks. In other words, the best evolution method on one seed agent and benchmark is not reliably the best on the next, and a method that improves on the train and gate splits does not always keep that improvement on the sealed split. That is a useful and slightly deflating result for the field, because it means there is no single dominant evolution policy, and any claim that one method is best needs to be read as best-under-these-conditions. The framework itself does not add modeling power; it does not make AHE or GEPA better than they were in their own papers. What it adds is comparability, inspectable candidate histories, and a protected evaluation surface, so that when you do compare methods, the comparison is about the methods and not about whose harness leaked evaluation signal into the loop.

The skeptical read. This is a framework paper, so the bar for it is different from a model paper. The right question is not whether RSIHub beats SOTA, it is whether the factorization is clean enough that you would actually build on it. The four-method port is strong evidence for that, and the sealed-split protocol is genuinely good practice that the field should copy. The caveats: the empirical section is a validation of the protocol, not a contribution to agent evolution itself, so if you are looking for a better evolution algorithm, this is not it. The 13.5 point headline gain is the maximum over settings, and the paper's own finding that gains do not reliably transfer is a warning label on any single number you pull from the table. And as with any framework, the real test is maintenance: operator interfaces and mutable-surface declarations are the API you will live with, and the paper does not yet have the scar tissue of a long user base to vouch for them. If your team is running agent-evolution experiments and keeps rebuilding the same workspace, evaluator, and lineage code, this is worth adopting as a substrate, and the sealed-split evaluation design is worth stealing even if you never use the rest. The framework is a protocol, not a model. Trust the protocol. Do not expect it to change which evolution method wins.
