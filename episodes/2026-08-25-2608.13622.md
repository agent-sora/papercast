ARC: Fair Relative Advantage Comparison in Open-Ended Real-World Interaction, by Yongqi Tong, Tan Li Hui Faith, and Choy Zhen Wen Marcus, equal-contribution first authors at Ant International, with Zhou Jin, Kewei Fu, Jiang-Ming Yang, Jianshe Li, and Xin Zhang as coauthors. The paper addresses a specific failure mode in group-based reinforcement learning for agents: when one rollout group mixes different conversation strategies, relative advantages stop measuring response quality and start measuring whatever interaction style the reward model happens to prefer.

First the problem, because the mechanism only makes sense against it. Group-based methods such as GRPO sample several responses per prompt, subtract the group mean reward, and use the centered value as the advantage. That signal is interpretable only when the compared rollouts are behaviorally comparable. In open-ended interaction they often are not: for the same dialogue state the agent may answer directly from memory, ask for clarification first, stream progress updates while tools run, or restate intent and confirm before an irreversible action. All of these can be task-appropriate. Reward models carry exposure, length, and style bias, so when a group spans qualitatively different behaviors, the centered reward absorbs region-specific reward preference along with genuine quality differences. The authors formalize this as a fairness property: a reward model is delta-fair if two equally good responses following different strategies score within delta of each other, and they argue real reward models violate it systematically in agent settings where interaction appropriateness lacks ground truth. Guidance-augmented RL methods such as SAGE, Scaf-GRPO, LUFFY, and ExGRPO attach auxiliary information to make answers easier to find. ARC attaches one for a different purpose: to define which rollouts may be compared at all.

The substrate is INTER3, their channel-separated interaction runtime, because standard think-then-act or ReAct traces do not expose strategy as an annotatable variable. In INTER3, plain text outside answer tags is latent reasoning; answer-tagged spans stream to the user; tool calls remain structured internal actions whose results return to context. The implementation footprint is deliberately small: add the answer tags to the tokenizer, and post-process generated text to extract user-visible spans. Because answer spans can be emitted before hidden execution finishes, time-to-first-token drops from 4.91 seconds for the think-style baseline to 1.27 seconds, which the authors treat as an architectural property of the runtime, not evidence for their training method. Behavior organizes into four high-level families, Progress Update, Clarify First, Alignment Check, and Direct Answer, refined into nine concrete strategies including parallel tool calls, silent execution, error recovery, and decision support. The families serve as training-time comparison classes only; nothing constrains inference.

The training corpus, INTER3-86K, holds 86,796 examples from two sources. One is de-identified online traces collected from customer service on a large-scale global payment platform already running the INTER3 runtime, capturing interruptions, redirections, and multi-step tool use. The other combines curated public benchmarks with teacher-driven rewriting: a 397B mixture-of-experts model, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, rewrites each assistant turn into the interleaved format, and malformed dialogues with invalid turn orderings or empty answer blocks are filtered out. The supervised split of 57.9 thousand examples mixes 34.2 thousand ToolMind function-calling examples, 59.1 percent, with 17.2 thousand Musique Long Content multi-hop questions, 29.7 percent, and 6.4 thousand KnightsAndKnaves logic puzzles plus distilled high-quality conversations, 11.1 percent. The reinforcement-learning split of 28.9 thousand examples is entirely tool-use data, and every conversation receives a strategy annotation through a two-model pipeline using Qwen3-235B-Instruct and Qwen3.5-27B as independent labelers. Both models predict a strategy with a confidence score; agreement above 0.85 confidence is accepted directly; disagreement with a confidence gap above 0.15 resolves to the more confident label; anything else goes to three human annotators for majority vote. The selected instruction is injected into the system prompt. The resulting distribution is heavily skewed: Progress Update covers 75.3 percent of examples, Direct Answer 14.3 percent, Clarify First 7.9 percent, Alignment Check 2.5 percent. Corpus-wide, inputs average 702 tokens and outputs 265; 63 percent of examples contain tool calls averaging 4.9 tools; 36 percent are multi-turn with 12.5 turns on average; answer segments appear 3.48 times per example at roughly 25 tokens each.

The method itself is four steps. Assign one strategy instruction to each training example. Sample M rollouts conditioned on the prompt plus that instruction. Compute advantages strictly within each strategy-conditioned group. Update the policy with an entropy-regularized objective. At inference the instruction is removed and the policy chooses strategies autonomously. The supporting analysis decomposes each reward into a strategy-dependent mean plus noise. Under standard sampling the advantage variance equals the within-strategy variance plus the between-strategy variance, scaled by one minus one over group size; the between-strategy term reflects reward-model bias and persists no matter how much you oversample. Conditioning the group on one strategy removes that term under perfect compliance, and an idealized calculation says the rollout budget needed for a given gradient accuracy shrinks by a factor of one plus the ratio of between-strategy to within-strategy variance. The authors flag this repeatedly as an estimator-level characterization, not a convergence guarantee. The entropy term matters for a mechanical reason: without it the policy collapses to emitting redundant answer blocks that collect format reward without content, so the bonus maintains stochasticity across the reasoning, answer, and tool channels. The reward sums three parts: a binary format check for at least one well-formed block with properly matched, non-nested tags; a tool reward of minus one, zero, or one requiring exact matches on tool identifier, argument key set, and argument values; and an answer reward of zero, 0.5, or one from an LLM judge over the extracted answer text, gated so semantic credit pays out only when tool execution was exactly right, with a structural-agreement score of one when neither prediction nor reference contains answer spans.

Setup: Qwen3-8B trained with GRPO in no-think mode, all reinforcement-learning runs warm-started from the same supervised checkpoint, and the same final reward everywhere unless a variant says otherwise. Evaluation pairs in-domain agentic suites, tau-bench airline and retail plus tau2-bench airline, retail, and telecom, with out-of-domain reasoning and instruction tests: AIME 2026, GPQA-Diamond, HMMT 2025, IFBench, and Arena-Hard. Reference points: the base no-think model averages 22.85 across the eleven metrics; the think variant reaches 32.82 at a 4.91-second latency cost. Against backbones, PPO improves from 27.49 to 28.57 with ARC, DAPO from 28.61 to 29.92, and GRPO from 28.09 to 33.46, a gain of 5.37 points. On the GRPO backbone, tau-airline climbs from 31.33 to 44.00, tau-retail from 40.29 to 50.00, and tau2-airline from 36.67 to 48.00, with smaller moves on tau2-retail and tau2-telecom; AIME improves from 31.67 to 40.83 while IFBench slips from 18.71 to 15.99. Time-to-first-token under ARC rises to 1.27 seconds from GRPO's 0.61, still far under the think baseline. The PPO pairing is genuinely mixed: AIME drops from 30.83 to 19.17 and IFBench from 19.73 to 14.29, while DAPO with ARC gains on AIME and GPQA but loses on Arena-Hard and HMMT. Training curves reinforce the bias story: vanilla GRPO peaks mid-training and then declines on tool-call and answer rewards even as format reward saturates for both methods, whereas ARC holds or improves across the same budget, suggesting the baseline starts exploiting reward-model preferences. A 4-byte-scale case study shows the effect is not capacity-bound: at 4B parameters, no-think averages 22.38, think 29.54, plain GRPO 22.33, and GRPO with ARC 34.23, roughly 53 percent above no-think on the five-task tool-use average.

Ablations carry most of the credibility here. Instruction-removal curricula do not help: keeping instructions always scores 29.59 average, a linearly increasing drop schedule scores 27.28, and a constant 0.20 drop probability is weakest at 26.97, so annealing away the conditioning weakens within-group comparability instead of teaching autonomy. Scaling the strategy set monotonically helps: Progress Update alone reaches 21.74 average, adding Direct Answer 24.91, adding Clarify First 25.60, and the full four-family suite 29.59, with tau-suite performance rising 99 percent and tau2 71 percent from one strategy to four; Alignment Check, 2.5 percent of the data, still contributes measurably. Hint diagnostics at inference show the behavior is internalized: the deployed checkpoint scores highest with no hints at 41.73 average, versus 39.85 with matched hints and 39.58 with randomized ones, and matched hints help tau-airline while hurting tau2-airline. The ingredient ladder, run under a fixed reward family so its numbers are not comparable to the main table, isolates the mechanism: GRPO alone 25.43, GRPO plus entropy alone 24.23, GRPO plus strategy-conditioned grouping 27.80, grouping plus entropy 29.59. An entropy sweep shows narrow tolerance: a coefficient of 0.01 collapses training outright, 0.001 is optimal, and 0.0001 falls to 27.48.

Four limitations are stated plainly: the taxonomy is coarse relative to real behavior; strongest results sit in open-ended tool use with generality untested; the method depends on normalized traces and strategy labels that may encode annotation or deployment bias; and the theory is estimator-level only. Add your own skepticism. The headline gain concentrates on one backbone, GRPO at plus 5.37 against roughly plus one elsewhere, and PPO regresses badly on reasoning. Baselines train on identical data without the injected strategy prompts, so part of the margin could be prompt-side supervision rather than fairer comparison; an instruction-prompted GRPO control without grouped advantages would settle it. The in-domain suites resemble the ToolMind-heavy training distribution, and tau2-telecom stays below 22 points in every configuration.

Bottom line: ARC is a narrow, well-diagnosed correction, change which rollouts get compared rather than how the policy updates, and the negative results on removal curricula, entropy alone, and inference-time hints make the mechanism claim more believable than most recipe papers manage. Before adopting, verify that your own reward model actually exhibits cross-strategy bias on your traffic, and rerun the missing instruction-prompted baseline control yourself.
