VFlash, Fast, Accurate Visual Planning via Flash Primitive Decoding, is from Yu-Cheng Chou, Shihao Wang, and Jin Huang, working at NVIDIA, with collaborators at Johns Hopkins University, the Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Purdue University, and the University of Hong Kong.

If a vision-language model is going to act in the physical world, it does not just produce words. It produces spatial structure: a bounding box around the object to grab, a two-dimensional or three-dimensional trajectory for the end effector to follow, a set of points describing where a scene is about to move. The problem the paper tackles is that the standard way of generating all of that is extremely slow. An autoregressive model emits one coordinate token at a time. A single bounding box is already a handful of tokens; a ten-point trajectory for two arms in both a two-dimensional image and a three-dimensional space is a long sequence of coordinate tokens, and every one of them costs a sequential forward pass. For a physical agent that has to keep re-planning as new observations arrive, that sequential cost is the whole bottleneck.

The core idea is that visual primitives have a known structure that you can exploit at decode time. A bounding box is four or eight coordinates of a known type and a known length. A trajectory is a known number of points in a known dimensionality. So instead of decoding the coordinates one token at a time, VFlash uses a lightweight drafter to generate an entire primitive in a single forward pass. The drafter's output layout adapts to the primitive's type, dimensionality, and length. Compact boxes get a compact block; a long variable-length trajectory gets a longer block. The drafter is small enough that this extra forward pass is cheap relative to the many autoregressive passes it replaces.

There is one detail that makes this different from ordinary speculative decoding, and it is the part worth understanding before you try to reproduce it. In standard speculative decoding, a draft token is only accepted if it exactly matches what the target model would have emitted. The draft has to reproduce the autoregressive token sequence. VFlash argues that spatial accuracy does not require that. You do not need the drafter to hit the exact token the target would produce; you need the decoded primitive to be structurally valid and spatially close to the ground truth. So VFlash trains on annotated primitives and directly accepts structurally valid predictions without target-token agreement. The verification step is checking the geometry of the plan, not checking token identity. For text and sub-task planning, where there is no such structural prior, the paper keeps verified speculative decoding with the usual target check. The framework is a generalist: different primitives route to different verification regimes depending on whether their structure is known.

On the speed side the headline numbers are large. The paper reports action-predicting latency on NVIDIA hardware, specifically Jetson Thor, DGX Spark, and H100. On Jetson Thor, autoregressive decoding takes about 3,553 milliseconds for the measured planning workload, DFlash, the prior block-diffusion drafting method, brings that down to about 1,828 milliseconds, and VFlash gets to about 703 milliseconds, which the paper labels an 80.2 percent reduction over the autoregressive baseline. On DGX Spark the same pattern holds: 3,357 autoregressive, 1,809 for DFlash, 650 for VFlash, an 80.6 percent reduction. The preliminary single-rollout measurement for three-dimensional point motion forecasting is roughly twenty-five times faster than autoregressive generation. These are the numbers to hold the paper to, and they are the reason a robot control loop can actually afford to re-plan every observation instead of every few observations.

On the accuracy side the claim is that VFlash does not just trade speed for a small drop in quality; it is at least as good and often better. Across the evaluated two-dimensional and three-dimensional robot trajectory tasks, VFlash consistently reduces prediction error relative to autoregressive decoding. The most concrete downstream result is a world action model conditioned on VFlash-generated plans, which reaches a 70.2 percent average success rate across four MolmoSpaces task suites, compared with 62.6 percent for the baseline. That is a meaningful gap, and it is the result that matters most if you are actually building a manipulation stack: the plan generator is not a neutral speedup, it changes the downstream policy's behavior.

The driving results are the more interesting of the two application domains, and the more worth checking if you are in autonomous driving. On NAVSIM and the Waymo test split, the policy is a ReCogDrive-derived diffusion transformer that reads the VLM's final-layer states plus the generated plan through cross-attention and outputs a continuous trajectory. The VLM is frozen. With supervised training, VFlash reaches 87.1 PDMS on NAVSIM, slightly above the ReCogDrive baseline at 86.8, and 7.79 RFS on Waymo, essentially tied with the Qwen-Drive-1.0 supervised row at 7.78. After reinforcement learning on the action head, using PDMS and RFS as rewards, the supervised row moves to 90.7 PDMS and 7.86 RFS, matching the strongest listed NAVSIM result and coming within a fifth of a point of the best Waymo number. The important structural point is that the plans are identical between the supervised and the reinforcement-learning rows; only the action head is trained by RL. So the driving gains are not coming from a better plan, they are coming from the action head learning to use a fast, accurate plan under a frozen planner.

A skeptical read has a few things I would verify before adopting this. First, the speedups are batch-size-one, latency-sensitive physical AI numbers, which is exactly the regime where they matter, but it is also a narrow regime. The single-rollout twenty-five-fold figure is described as preliminary, so I would treat it as a direction rather than a guarantee, and I would re-run the multi-rollout and batched numbers myself before putting a robot in the field. Second, the accuracy comparisons mix several baselines from different papers, and the LIBERO-PRO gains are the largest, at 6.8 percentage points over baseline, which is encouraging, but I would want to see the variance across seeds because the paper is reporting a single configuration. The 3D-accuracy-improves-while-latency-drops combination is the part that would normally be suspicious, and the explanation is plausible: structurally-validated decoding removes a class of coordinate-token errors that pure autoregressive decoding can make, so faster does not here mean less careful. The drafter is trained on real annotated primitives, not on the target's token distribution, which is a nontrivial training cost that the abstract does not fully price in.

The engineering gotcha to watch is the layout adaptation. The drafter has to handle a box, a two-arm trajectory, a variable-length trajectory, dense point motion, and tool-call text, all with different block shapes. If you are dropping this into your own stack, the primitive taxonomy and the boundary-token format are the surface you will be maintaining, and every new primitive type is a new layout and a new verification rule. The verified-speculative path for text is standard, but the structural-acceptance path for spatial primitives is a new contract between drafter and verifier, and the failure mode is a plan that is structurally valid but quietly off by a few centimeters, which is exactly the kind of error that looks fine in a log and shows up as a miss in the real world.

One more mechanism is worth being precise about because it is the reason the accuracy story holds together. The drafter is a small block-diffusion model that predicts an entire primitive layout in one pass, and the layout is a function of the primitive's metadata: its type, its dimensionality, and its length. A two-arm trajectory in a three-dimensional space with ten points gets a different block shape than a single bounding box, and the drafter conditions on that metadata so the block is the right size before it starts. The verifier then checks the decoded primitive against the structural prior and, for the spatial cases, against the annotated training distribution rather than against the target's token stream. That is a deliberate departure from DFlash, the prior method, which uses block-diffusion drafting but still verifies against the target model's output distribution. VFlash's claim is that dropping the target-token agreement requirement is safe specifically for structured spatial output, because the structure itself is the constraint, and it is a claim that does not generalize to free text, which is why the framework keeps the standard path for text and sub-task planning.

Bottom line: VFlash is a clean, well-motivated decoding change that turns a sequential coordinate-token bottleneck into a parallel, structure-aware one, with large measured latency reductions on real NVIDIA hardware and no accuracy loss on the evaluated planning tasks. The main caution is that the headline speedups are single-rollout and the accuracy gains lean on cross-paper baselines, so reproduce the multi-rollout and multi-seed numbers before you build a control loop on top of it.
