Ovis-Embedding: Pushing the Frontiers of Universal Omni-Modal Embeddings. The byline is the Ovis Team at Ant Group, and the paper is built on the pretrained Qwen-omni backbone.

Multi-modal embedding models have followed a familiar path: you train a model to map text, images, and video into a shared space where similarity means relatedness, and you measure success on retrieval and classification benchmarks. The problem with most of them is that they are not actually omni-modal. They handle text and images, maybe video, but they do not handle audio, and they do not handle the mixture of all four in a single embedding. That means if you want to retrieve a video given a spoken query, or match an image to a description that was transcribed from audio, you are stuck with a system that was never trained to sit at the intersection of all the modalities it is supposed to connect. Ovis-Embedding is a family of models, in multiple sizes, trained to produce a single embedding from any combination of text, image, audio, and video, so that the embedding space is genuinely shared across all four. The training recipe is where the technical contribution sits. They start from the Qwen-omni backbone and use a low-rank adapter, LoRA, initialized at a small rank, rather than fine-tuning the full model, which keeps the pretrained multi-modal representations intact while teaching the model to produce the embedding objective.

The contrastive training pipeline has three ingredients the paper highlights. First, homogeneous-source sampling: when building a positive pair, the model is trained on pairs where the query and the target come from the same source modality distribution, which the authors argue reduces the modality gap that normally lets a strong text side dominate the shared space while the audio and video sides stay under-trained. Second, a focal-loss variant on the contrastive objective, which down-weights the easy negatives that make up most of a contrastive batch and forces the model to spend capacity on the hard ones, the ones that are truly confusable. Third, a similarity-based embedding distillation step, in which a larger teacher embedding model, also from the Qwen-omni family, is used to supervise the smaller student models in the family, so the small models inherit the teacher's geometry rather than learning it from scratch at low capacity. On top of this they add a low-rank feature decomposition that separates the embedding into components, which the paper uses to analyze what each component is encoding. The models are evaluated on the current frontier of omni-modal embedding benchmarks: MMEB v3 and MMEB v2 for multi-modal embedding, MVEB for video, MAEB for audio, and RTEB for retrieval, and the headline claim is state-of-the-art across all five at the corresponding model sizes.

The headline results are across the family, and the omni-modal flagship is the 3B model, initialized from Qwen2.5-Omni-3B, which is the interesting result because it is small. On MMEB-v3, the primary omni-modal benchmark covering image, video, visual document, text, audio, and agent tasks, Ovis-Embedding-Omni-3B scores 58.46 overall, beating the strongest baseline, Tianmu-Emb-Uni at 8B, by 5.19 points, and beating e5-omni at 7B by 11.3 points and Omni-Embed-Nemotron-3B by 14.9 points, despite being the same size as the latter and far smaller than the former two. The consistency is the notable part: it ranks first on every one of the six evaluation groups, with margins over the second-best of 3.72 on image, 5.62 on video, 2.89 on visual documents, 3.53 on text, 7.04 on audio, and 6.10 on agent tasks, so the overall lead is not carried by a single dominant modality. The vision-language variants, trained on the same recipe, also lead their comparison groups on MMEB-v2: the 9B model scores 81.13 overall against 80.09 for the next-best, Octen-VL-Embedding-Large, and the 2B model scores 77.46 against 75.42. The family also reports state-of-the-art on the focused audio and video benchmarks, MAEB and MVEB, and on RTEB for text retrieval.

The architectural choice is the paper's most defensible claim, and it is worth stating precisely. The competing omni-modal systems in the paper's related-work analysis, e5-omni, Conan-embedding-v3, jina-embeddings-v5, LCO-Embedding-Omni, all follow one of two patterns: either bolt modality-specific adapters onto a text-centric backbone, or fuse modalities late, after each has been encoded separately. The paper's argument is that this produces a shared space that is only shared in name: the geometry is really the text space, with the other modalities projected into it, and the projection quality for audio and video is bounded by the adapter. By starting from Qwen-omni, a model that already processes text, image, audio, and video in a single transformer stack, the embedding is extracted from a representation that was built jointly, and the paper's MMEB-v3 results, particularly the 7 point margin on the audio group, are offered as evidence that the joint representation is what buys the audio performance that the adapter-based systems give up. The low-rank training discipline, small-rank LoRA initialization plus distillation from a larger teacher, is the standard recipe for protecting a pretrained backbone while teaching it a new output head, and the Matryoshka-style flexible embedding dimensions, 2048 or 4096 depending on the model, mean you can trade embedding storage for quality at deployment time, which matters for a retrieval system indexing millions of items.

The skeptical read. The benchmark numbers are strong, but the comparison set needs a closer look before you trust the state-of-the-art framing. The MMEB-v3 comparison, where the 3B model scores 58.46 against 53.27 for the next-best, is a clean win, but the baselines in that table are a mixed bag in terms of scale and recency. Tianmu-Emb-Uni is an 8B model, so beating it by 5 points at 3B is the genuinely impressive result, but e5-omni is also 7B, and the paper does not include every recent omni-modal model in the same table, so the 5.19 point margin over the strongest baseline is a margin over the strongest baseline in this particular comparison set, not over every model ever trained for this task. The MMEB-v2 results for the vision-language variants are more crowded. The 9B model's 81.13 is only 1.04 points above Octen-VL-Embedding-Large, and the 2B model's 77.46 is 2.04 points above Octen-VL-Embedding, which are meaningful but not large margins, and on individual sub-tasks the picture is less one-sided than the aggregate suggests: the 9B model's video QA score of 72.50 is well below Octen's 82.14, and its visual-document ViDoRe-V2 of 61.00 is below Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B's 69.86, so the overall lead is built on image and video-classification strength while the model gives up ground on the harder video QA and some document retrieval sub-tasks. The paper is honest about the text-retrieval weakness: on MultiConIR, the 3B model trails Omni-Embed-Nemotron-3B by 7.94 points, and the authors flag multi-condition retrieval as an open direction. The audio and video focused benchmarks, MAEB and MVEB, are where the paper claims state-of-the-art, but the extracted text does not surface the specific numeric tables for those two suites in the main body, so the audio claim rests on the MMEB-v3 audio group result of 50.08, which is the strongest single-modality margin in the table, and on the paper's own statement that the family leads MAEB and MVEB. The low-rank training recipe is the standard, well-understood approach, which is a good thing for reproducibility and a modest thing for novelty: the contribution is in the data and the backbone choice, not in a new training method.

Bottom line: Ovis-Embedding is a credible, well-executed push on the universal-embedding frontier, and the 3B omni model's consistent first place across all six MMEB-v3 modality groups, including a 7 point margin on audio, is the strongest evidence yet that a natively omni-modal backbone beats bolt-on adapters for cross-modal retrieval. The caveats are that the MMEB-v2 vision-language margins are thin and sub-task-specific weaknesses exist on video QA and multi-condition text retrieval, and the MAEB and MVEB state-of-the-art claims should be checked against the full tables before you build on them, but for anyone running a retrieval stack that needs to sit at the intersection of audio, video, and text in one embedding space, this is the model to evaluate first.
