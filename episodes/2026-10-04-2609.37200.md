Adaptive Reward Routing is from Songlin Yang, Xiaotong Zhao, and Jiacheng Zhang, with the authors at MMLab at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Tencent Video, and the University of Hong Kong. It is a method for reinforcement learning of joint audio-video diffusion models against multiple competing rewards at once.

The setting is multi-reward reinforcement learning for joint audio-video diffusion models. A single model must generate video and audio together from a text prompt, and the quality of the result depends on several objectives at once: the visual quality of the video, the audio quality, semantic alignment between the two modalities, and temporal synchronization between them. Reward-guided diffusion RL is a natural way to improve along all of these, and the authors use a forward-process framework called DiffusionNFT, which gives stable direct supervision over sampled flow-matching timesteps without needing reverse-process likelihood estimation. The problem the paper targets is that two quantities change during training, and existing methods treat both as fixed. First, where reward-driven updates should act in the network: which layers and tokens actually mediate cross-modal influence. Second, how competing rewards should be combined: the relative weights between, say, audio quality and synchronization drift as the model changes. Fixed routing and fixed reward weights fail to track the evolving model, and that mismatch is what the method is designed to remove.

The method has two components, and it helps to think of them as answering the two questions separately. The first is Cross-Modal Influence-Guided Routing, which localizes where the updates should act. The trick is using bidirectional cross-attention responses inside the model as an efficient proxy for evolving cross-modal influence. Instead of probing the network with extra interventions, the method reads the attention responses that the model is already computing, and uses them to dynamically reweight the token-aware losses and to scale gradients across the cross-modal layers. So the set of layers and tokens that the reward signal pushes on changes as training progresses, tracking where the cross-modal influence actually lives at each stage. The second component is Preference-Preserving Modality-Aware Reweighting, which coordinates the competing rewards. Here the predefined reward weights are preserved as preference priors, and branch-specific reward-gradient interactions are used as residual corrections applied after a warm-up period. The design choice is deliberate: rather than replacing the hand-set weights with gradient-derived coefficients, the method treats them as a prior and adds corrections on top. The warm-up matters because the gradient relationships are not reliable early in training, so dynamic reweighting is delayed until the estimates stabilize. The stated goal is to resolve conflicts as they emerge without letting a dominant reward suppress weak but essential objectives, for instance audio quality quietly collapsing because the synchronization reward is driving most of the gradient.

The experiments are on JavisBench with two backbones, LTX-2 and LTX-2.3, and the evaluation is a panel of metrics grouped into four families: audio-video quality, text consistency, audio-video consistency, and synchronization, reported as means over three seeds. The headline comparison is against the base model and three RL baselines, GDPO, MARBLE, and OmniNFT. On the LTX-2 backbone, the base model scores a video quality of 1.883 and an audio quality of 5.201, with a DeSync synchronization score of 0.604, lower being better. OmniNFT is the strongest baseline: it raises video quality to 3.136 and audio quality to 5.614 and cuts DeSync to 0.390. The proposed method beats it on the quality and consistency metrics, reaching video quality 3.336 and audio quality 5.868, the highest text-audio consistency and CLAP scores in the table, and the lowest DeSync, 0.341. The gains over OmniNFT are modest in absolute terms on each metric, but they are consistent across the quality and consistency columns, and the method is the only one that improves both modalities' quality and the synchronization simultaneously. On the larger LTX-2.3 backbone the picture is similar: the base model starts at 2.032 video quality with DeSync 0.504, OmniNFT reaches 3.489 with DeSync around 0.369, and the proposed method continues to lead the consistency metrics while remaining competitive on quality.

A few setup details that matter for interpretation. The backbones are LTX-2 and LTX-2.3, the latter a 22 billion parameter model, and both use separate audio and video streams connected through bidirectional cross-attention, which is exactly the structure the routing mechanism reads. Training uses 19,487 audio-video prompts from a VGGSound-derived corpus, each carrying modality-specific descriptions plus a joint prompt. The reward stack has four families: video quality, combining VideoAlign and HPSv3; audio quality, from AudioBox aesthetics; text-audio alignment, from CLAP; and audio-video synchronization, from DeSync, which is converted to a higher-is-better form during training and reported in its original lower-is-better form at evaluation. The ablations on JavisBench decompose the two components. Token weighting alone lifts video quality from the routing baseline, adding layer scaling pushes it further to about 3.3, and the full combination of token weighting plus layer scaling plus the preference-preserving reweighting with warm-up reaches the top row. The most instructive row is the branch-aware reweighting without the preference-prior preservation: it produces the worst video quality in the table, 2.612, which supports the authors' claim that letting gradient-derived coefficients replace the hand-set prior lets one modality's reward dominate and suppress the other. The mechanism analysis adds a check that the cross-attention proxy is not just a decorative statistic: when they block specific token groups, the layers the proxy identifies as influential are the ones whose ablation actually changes the cross-modal scores, and the proxy's layer ranking tracks the true influence ranking across training checkpoints rather than going stale.

Now the skeptical read. The margins over the strongest RL baseline are real but small on most columns: video quality 3.336 versus 3.136 on LTX-2, audio quality 5.868 versus 5.614, and the synchronization gain of about 5 hundredths of a DeSync point. Against a 22 billion parameter backbone and a 19,000 prompt training corpus, those are the numbers you would expect from a refinement rather than a breakthrough, and the paper is honest about the framing: it is a dynamic-routing fix for a problem, fixed weights, that the baselines demonstrably have. Two caveats deserve emphasis. First, the evaluation is a single benchmark, JavisBench, with a panel of learned reward models, and the training rewards overlap heavily with the evaluation metrics, so part of the measured improvement is the model getting closer to the optimizers it was trained with. Second, the routing mechanism is tied to bidirectional cross-attention layers, which is a property of these particular dual-stream backbones; the authors note the idea extends to other diffusion objectives through their token losses, but the evidence for that generality is argument, not experiment. The compute cost of reading cross-attention responses is negligible, which is the method's strongest practical selling point: it adds no parameters, no auxiliary models, and no extra forward passes.

Verdict: a well-constructed, low-overhead refinement of multi-reward diffusion RL, with ablations that isolate each component and a mechanism check that the attention proxy tracks real influence rather than noise. The gains over strong baselines are consistent but modest, and the single-benchmark, reward-overlapping evaluation limits how much weight you should put on the absolute numbers. It is a useful piece of the multi-objective generation stack, not a redefinition of it.

For someone implementing this on their own multi-reward setup, the practical path is straightforward. You need three things: a way to read the cross-attention responses between your modalities during the forward pass, a per-token and per-layer loss reweighting step driven by those responses, and a reward-aggregation step that keeps your hand-set weights as a prior and applies gradient-interaction corrections only after the warm-up window. The warm-up length is the hyperparameter to watch, because too short a warm-up means the gradient estimates are noisy and the residual corrections will fight the prior, while too long a warm-up wastes the early training where the imbalance is worst. The paper does not report a sensitivity sweep over the warm-up boundary, which is a gap if you are planning to reuse the recipe. The other implementation decision is how coarse your routing should be: the ablations show token weighting and layer scaling are complementary, so if your compute budget forces a choice, token weighting gives you most of the routing benefit, and the layer scaling buys the remaining quality points.
