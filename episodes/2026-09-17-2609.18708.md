Rethinking Critic Learning in PPO: Understanding and Mitigating Value Flattening. The first three authors are Yizhuo Li, Jianhao Yan, and Yun Luo, and the work spans Shanghai Jiao Tong University, the Shanghai AI Laboratory, Westlake University, Nanjing University, Tsinghua University, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, and Nanyang Technological University. It is a focused method paper on a specific failure mode in one of the most widely used reinforcement-learning algorithms for language models.

The context. In reinforcement learning for large language models, Proximal Policy Optimization commonly uses a critic to estimate state values and reduce the variance of the policy updates. The critic is supposed to tell the policy, at each token of a response, roughly what the expected return is from that point forward, so that the advantage, the return minus the baseline, has lower variance than the raw return alone. The paper uncovers a systematic failure mode in these critics that the authors call Value Flattening. The state values, estimated from multiple Monte Carlo returns, collapse toward a flat function: instead of the critic's value changing meaningfully across the tokens of a response, it ends up nearly constant, so the advantage it induces is weak and the policy updates carry less of the signal they are supposed to carry. The claim is that this is not noise in a particular run, it is a structural tendency of how the critic is trained.

The mechanism. The paper formalizes the baseline invariance that usually makes the choice of critic seem low-stakes. For any action-independent baseline, the expectation of the policy gradient is unchanged, so a value function and a constant function have the same expected gradient. That invariance is in expectation, though, and it does not hold for the clipped PPO objective, where whether the clipped branch or the unclipped branch is active depends on the actual magnitude of the advantage. A flattened critic changes the magnitude of the advantages, and therefore changes which branch of the clip fires, and therefore changes the actual update. So the flattening is not harmless even though the textbook invariance argument would suggest it is. That gap between the in-expectation invariance and the clipped-objective reality is the core of the paper's argument.

The mitigation is deliberately simple, and that is the point. Rather than a new architecture or a new loss, the authors use sparse supervision: instead of supervising the critic at every token of every response, they supervise it at a small, well-separated set of states, a handful of anchor points per response. They study where to place the anchors, and find that structured placement outperforms random placement. Adding a tail anchor, for example, improves the downstream policy accuracy in their reported runs, moving from 44.10 to 45.57 on their metric. The method, which they call SP3O, is evaluated on the Qwen3 8B base model and shown to mitigate Value Flattening and improve the actor across their benchmarks, while maintaining more stable policy updates, higher validation accuracy, and higher rollout reward over the PPO baseline. The ablations show that the number of supervised states is not the whole story, and that the placement of the anchors is what drives the gain, not merely the count.

The intuition for why sparse supervision helps is the core of the fix, and it follows directly from the diagnosis. If you supervise the critic at every token, each supervised target is a Monte Carlo estimate of the return from that token onward, and those estimates share a large amount of the same future reward, so the critic is effectively being trained on a highly correlated set of targets that all point at roughly the same value. That correlation is what pulls the value function flat: there is not enough independent information across the token positions to pin down a value that varies meaningfully along the response. By supervising only at a few well-separated anchors, you break that correlation. The anchors are far enough apart that their Monte Carlo targets capture genuinely different return estimates, and the critic is then free to interpolate a value function that varies between the anchors instead of being dragged toward a constant. The placement matters because you want the anchors to span the parts of the response where the return actually changes, and the structured placement rules are designed to do exactly that, which is why putting an anchor at the tail, where the final outcome is realized, gives a measurable bump over placing anchors randomly.

A skeptical read. What I trust is the diagnosis and the mechanism, specifically the argument that baseline invariance in expectation does not survive the clipped objective. That is the kind of subtle point that is easy to hand-wave in a PPO paper and hard to get right, and if their formalization is correct it reframes how much you can trust a flattened critic to be doing real work. The fix being "just supervise fewer, better-placed states" is exactly the right kind of contribution for this area: cheap, explainable, and a drop-in change to an existing training loop rather than a new framework you have to adopt wholesale.

It is also worth noting what the paper is not claiming, because the framing matters. This is not a claim that the critic is unimportant, or that you can drop it and use a return-only baseline. The critic is still doing its job of variance reduction, and the point is that the way it is supervised determines whether it collapses into a flat, low-information function or stays a useful, varying one. That distinction is the whole value of the paper: it does not change the role of the critic, it changes how you train it so that the role is actually filled. For a practitioner running PPO at scale, that is a much lower bar to clear than adopting a new algorithm, which is probably why the contribution, while modest in absolute points, has a good chance of actually getting used. The stability results, showing more stable policy updates and higher validation accuracy alongside the accuracy gain, are the part that would matter most in a long training run, because a slightly higher final accuracy that comes with a more stable trajectory is often worth more than the raw number suggests.

What I would re-verify before adopting. First, the margin. The headline improvement is in the range of a point or two on their internal metric, which is real but small, and I would want to see it hold across more seeds and more base models before treating it as a free lunch. The paper reports the half-width of the 95 percent Wilson confidence interval on the empirical success rate is at most 0.04, which is honest about the uncertainty, but a 1.5 point gain sitting inside a 0.04 half-width is a result you should replicate, not cite. Second, the anchor-placement rule is the load-bearing part, and the paper's ablation says structured beats random, but the practical question for an implementer is how robust the placement heuristic is to response-length distributions you have not seen. A third concern is the evaluation substrate: the results are on the Qwen3 8B base model, and the whole value-flattening story is a property of how the critic is supervised under a specific clipping schedule and a specific reward, so the size and location of the gain could shift if you change the clip range, the number of Monte Carlo samples, or the reward scale, none of which the paper sweeps exhaustively. The bottom line is that this is a tight, well-argued paper on a real failure mode in PPO critics, and the sparse-supervision fix is a low-cost change worth trying. But the gains are modest and model-specific, so treat it as a targeted improvement to an existing PPO loop, not as a replacement for the algorithm.
