StreamVibe: Proactive Video Agents for Evolving User Intent, by Chengzhi Liu, Yuxiang Lai, and Yuzhe Yang, from Google, the University of California Santa Barbara, and Stanford University, with the full author list also including researchers from the University of California Santa Cruz, the University of Oxford, Emory University, and Columbia University.

The premise here is a shift in what a video model is for. The dominant paradigm is offline and passive: you give the model a video, it watches, you ask a question, it answers. The paper argues the interesting deployment is online and proactive, where the model watches a continuous stream of what a user is doing, and has to decide not just what to say but when to say it, without being asked. The example they use is a user navigating a website: nobody is going to ask the assistant where they should click next, but the assistant could figure out that the user is stuck and offer a pointer at the right moment. Two failure modes of current systems motivate the paper. First, most methods only respond to explicit requests, so they miss the implicit intent, the help the user needs but does not ask for. Second, user intent does not sit still. A request can update mid-task, the user can change direction, and a system that computes an answer once at the start is answering a question the user no longer has. The paper's three contributions are a benchmark, a streaming model, and a harness that keeps intent state alive over time.

StreamVibe-Bench is the first piece, and it is the part most people will reuse. It has 4,100 evaluation samples and 85,000 training samples, and it organizes intent into three types: explicit, where the user states what they want and the model must watch for the trigger, like notify me when the download finishes; implicit, where the user is doing something and the model must infer what help would be useful, like trim the crusts before spreading the jam on a bread roll; and dynamic, where the intent itself evolves over the course of the interaction. The benchmark is built from roughly 183 hours of video, with over 20,000 annotated help points, and the construction pipeline uses a frontier model from Google DeepMind to clean the data and identify candidate help points. The evaluation has two settings that are worth understanding. In pointwise evaluation, the benchmark samples timestamps around annotated help points and asks whether the model would respond at the right one; that is scored with precision, recall, F1, and false positive rate, plus a content F1 that measures whether the response actually contains the key content of the reference answer. In trajectory evaluation, the model runs over the full video and must cover all of a sample's evidence points, each with its own valid response window, and the interaction ends when every evidence point has been covered or the video ends. That second setting is the harder one, because it tests sustained interaction rather than isolated trigger detection. A useful way to think about the difference: pointwise scoring asks whether the model would have spoken at the right second in isolation, while trajectory scoring asks whether, over minutes of continuous watching, the model tracks which help points have been delivered, which are still pending, and when the user's goal has moved. The false positive rate is the metric to watch in deployment, because a proactive assistant that speaks too often becomes noise, and the benchmark's negative timestamps around help points are specifically there to measure that failure.

StreamVibe-VL is the streaming model. It infers implicit intent from continuous observations and changes its interaction behavior as the intent evolves. The training setup is the most implementation-relevant detail in the paper: the model is optimized with GRPO, the group relative policy optimization algorithm, on localized trajectory rollouts sampled around help points. Localized here means the rollouts are causal segments centered on a help point rather than the entire video, which keeps the compute cost tractable while still covering the decision window where the timing judgment actually happens. The reward has two parts. The point-level reward matches predicted responses to ground-truth evidence points, with each point matchable at most once, and aggregates the matched scores while penalizing missed evidence points and excessive responses, so the model is trained on both the timing and the content of its responses. The global reward scores the rollout as a whole, because point-level timing accuracy by itself does not guarantee coherent interaction over a long stream. The result is a model that improves response timing and answer quality by 10.7 percent over the baselines, while the paper checks that it has not degraded general video understanding on standard benchmarks, which is the usual cost of a targeted training objective. The general-capability check matters here because a streaming model that learns to speak up at the right moments can easily do so by becoming a worse passive viewer, and the paper's claim is that the two are decoupled in their setup.

StreamVibe-Harness is the third piece, and it is the part that addresses the dynamic-intent problem directly. It maintains user intents as persistent state with a flexible lifecycle, using a hierarchical memory that tracks the latest intent and the current environmental state across the interaction. The point is that a model call is stateless by default: the harness is what carries the intent forward, decides when an intent is stale and should be retired, and updates it when the user's behavior changes. Over sustained interaction, the harness gives a further 12.3 percent gain in proactive interaction, improving response consistency and adaptation.

The findings the paper draws from the benchmark are worth stating plainly, because they are negative results for the field. Finding two is the most important: existing proactive models remain limited in understanding user intent and deciding when to respond, and the gap is largest under implicit and dynamic intents, exactly the settings that matter for real deployment. Explicit intent, watch for the download to finish, is comparatively tractable; inferring unasked help and tracking a shifting goal is where current models fall over.

The skeptical read. The benchmark is the durable contribution: 183 hours of video with 20,000 help points and a two-setting evaluation protocol is a resource that will outlive any one model, and the explicit/implicit/dynamic taxonomy is a clean way to organize what "proactive" means. The model results, a 10.7 percent improvement on timing and answer quality, are meaningful but the comparison class is limited to other proactive models, most of which the paper shows are weak, so part of the margin is measured against a low bar. The harness gain is reported as a percentage of proactive interaction quality, and the hierarchical memory design is where I would want the most detail before adopting it, because intent lifecycle management, deciding when a user's goal has actually changed versus when they are just being slow, is a genuinely hard policy and the paper treats it somewhat lightly. There is also the standing question of who defines a help point during construction: the annotation pipeline uses a frontier model to identify candidate help points, which means the benchmark encodes that model's theory of when assistance is useful, and a different annotator might have drawn the windows differently. The 85,000 training samples against 4,100 evaluation samples is a large train-to-test ratio that should make you check the evaluation split is genuinely held out and not leaking help-point windows from the training distribution. The bottom line: this is the most complete treatment of proactive video interaction to date, with a benchmark that will become a standard and a streaming model that sets the current state of the art. If you are building assistants that watch what users do, the implicit and dynamic intent gap is the real finding, and it is a gap worth benchmarking against before you claim your system is proactive.
