Today's paper is FuseReg: Regularizing Layer Fusion Mitigates the Reconstruction-Generation Gap in Representation Autoencoders, from Hongyang Du, Yunfei Xie, and Junjie Ye, working across the USC PSI Lab, Brown University, Rice University, and a spread of other US and UK universities. The paper's core claim is that the biggest weakness in representation autoencoders is not the encoder or the generator, but the thin interface between them: the choice of which encoder layers get fused into the latent space.

The setting is representation autoencoders, or RAEs. You take a frozen pretrained visual encoder, in their experiments DINOv3-Large with 23 candidate transformer layers, and you build a generative stack on top of its features. A pixel decoder turns the fused latent back into an image, and a diffusion transformer, a DiT, learns to generate latents. The latent space is defined by fusing features from several encoder layers, and the authors note the tradeoff that has plagued this design: shallow layers preserve fine pixel detail, so they favor reconstruction, while deeper layers carry more semantically compressed information, so they favor generation metrics. Every prior method, including RAEv2 and learned gating schemes, commits to one fixed fusion, either a heuristic sum of the last few layers or a set of globally learned weights. FuseReg's argument is that a fixed fusion is the wrong target. You do not want to find a better single fusion. You want the decoder and the generator to stay effective across a whole family of fusions.

The mechanism is a dropout-style regularizer applied at the fusion interface. During training, for each example you draw a Bernoulli mask over the K candidate layers, conditioned on keeping at least one layer, and form the latent as the mean of the retained layers. The normalization is doing real work here. The paper proves that, by linearity of expectation, the expected latent under this sampling is exactly the full-layer mean, so you are not shifting the deployment representation at all. What the sampling changes is the variance around that mean, and the proof shows the induced covariance is proportional to a scalar times the covariance of the layer deviations. In plain terms: the random subset only perturbs the directions in which the encoder layers disagree with each other. Layers that agree are untouched. That is precisely the brittleness the method is targeting, and the identity is exact, with no linearity assumption on the downstream model.

There is a theoretical section worth respecting. Under a linear squared-error surrogate, subset sampling adds a penalty term to the decoder objective that is the trace of the decoder map times the layer-disagreement covariance, and the same structure appears in the DiT objective scaled by the time weight and the noise level. The paper also proves that no deterministic input-independent fusion can match both the first and second moments of the random fusion weights, because the second moment of a fixed weight vector has rank at most 1 while the random one has full rank. This is the cleanest statement in the paper: randomization is not just another learned gate, it is a different object.

Two design choices matter if you want to reproduce this. First, the decoder and the generator get separate drop rates, p for the decoder and p for the DiT, because the two stages solve different prediction problems. The decoder learns to reconstruct pixels from a subset latent. The DiT learns to map a noisy subset latent to the full-layer target, which is the actual deployment latent, using a flow-matching loss with a time weight that peaks at high noise. Setting the DiT rate to zero recovers the standard objective exactly, so this is a pure training-time modification, no architectural change. Second, the frozen encoder, the decoder architecture, parameter counts, and training budgets are all held constant across variants, which is the right way to isolate the effect of the regularization.

The experiments are structured in three stages, and the numbers are specific. The setup is ImageNet-256, a ViT decoder trained from scratch on top of the frozen DINOv3-Large encoder with 23 candidate layers, all decoder variants matched in architecture, parameter count, and training-step budget. Reconstruction is scored with PSNR, SSIM, and reconstruction FID on 50,000 images; generation is scored with FID and Inception Score on 50,000 samples from a class-conditional DiT run with 50 Euler steps, with and without the official guidance scale of 1.78. Stage one: can one decoder handle arbitrary fusions? A decoder trained at drop rate 0.95 reconstructs from the 7-layer fusion, the 23-layer fusion, and a single mid layer, with peak signal-to-noise ratios of 23.77, 27.52, and 25.13 decibels, and reconstruction FID below 0.6 in all three cases. A decoder trained on the 23-layer fusion alone hits 27.04 decibels on its home fusion but collapses to 12.51 decibels on the 7-layer fusion. That is the headline: fixed-fusion decoders are specialists, the regularized decoder is a generalist, and the generalist also wins on the native fusion.

It is worth locating this against the prior art, because the framing matters. RAEv2 sums the last few encoder layers with fixed weights. DRoRAE and attentive multi-layer probing learn deterministic fusion weights across depths. FuseReg explicitly positions itself as orthogonal to that question: it is not asking which single fusion is best, it is asking how to make the downstream models robust to the choice. The closest relatives in the deep learning canon are dropout, nested dropout, LayerDrop, and Matryoshka representation learning, all of which stochastically vary the available computation or representation during training. FuseReg is that idea, pointed at the layer-fusion interface of an autoencoder. One practical detail the paper reports: a learned global gate, given the chance to find its own fixed fusion, concentrates on a single-layer shortcut. That is direct evidence that the optimization landscape favors lazy specializations, which strengthens the case for forcing exposure to many configurations.

Stage two isolates the generation side. Take a fixed generator, sample its latents once, and render them with decoders trained at increasing drop rates. On the native 23-layer fusion, swapping the plain decoder for the regularized one drops generation FID from 3.01 to 2.21. Under a shifted 7-layer fusion, where a plain pipeline produces nearly garbage, it drops from 27.73 to 1.92. The curves are not uniformly monotone, which the authors are honest about: with guidance, intermediate drop rates temporarily regress generation FID from 1.25 to as high as 1.49 before recovering at the top rate. If you are adopting this, sweep the rate, do not assume monotonicity.

Stage three scales up. On a DiT-Base generator with a 13.96 baseline FID, regularizing the decoder alone gives 12.96, regularizing the generator alone gives 12.09, and doing both together gives 9.93, which beats the sum of the individual gains, so the two axes are genuinely complementary. Scaling to DiT-XL, with a 2.91 baseline, the picture gets more nuanced: regularizing the generator alone no longer helps FID, but joint regularization still does. The benefit shrinks as the generator gets stronger, which is the kind of result you should expect, and which the paper reports without flinching.

Where I would be skeptical. The theoretical guarantees are all for a linear squared-error surrogate, and the paper says so plainly. The nonlinear benefits are real in their experiments, but the theory is describing the direction of the incentive, not the magnitude of the gain. The method is also validated on ImageNet-256 with a single encoder family. Whether the drop rates transfer across encoders, resolutions, or video generation, where this line of work is heading, is not shown. And the practical cost is real: training with randomized fusions means the decoder sees different latent statistics every step, so convergence may need more steps at the top of the sweep, and the two-rate grid search doubles your hyperparameter surface. My bottom line: this is a clean, well-controlled demonstration that the layer-fusion interface is an under-regularized weak point in RAE pipelines, and the fix is cheap to try. But I would re-run the sweep on my own encoder and resolution before trusting the 9.93 FID, and I would not extrapolate the linear theory to claim why it works, only that it is a plausible mechanism.
