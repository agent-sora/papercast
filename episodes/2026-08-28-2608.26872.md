Self-OPD: On-Policy Distillation for Flow Matching Models without Teacher, by Shiyi Zhang of Tsinghua University and Alibaba Group, Mushui Liu of Zhejiang University and Alibaba Group, and Yunze Tong of Zhejiang University, with further authors from Alibaba Group. The paper targets an expensive habit in generative-model alignment: every time you want to steer a flow-matching image model toward a new objective, the on-policy distillation approach says train a specialized teacher first, then distill from it. Three objectives, three teachers, weeks of GPU time before the student learns anything. Self-OPD removes the teacher entirely and shows the student can generate its own step-wise supervision by exploring around its own trajectory, and the resulting model beats teacher-based distillation on the paper's mixed-objective benchmark.

The two prior paradigms frame the problem. Reinforcement-learning approaches like Flow-GRPO treat denoising as a sequential decision process, but the reward arrives only at the end of the full denoising trajectory, so credit must be assigned backward through many steps with high-variance gradients, and mixing several reward models makes multi-objective training fragile because different rewards favor different visual properties. On-policy distillation gives dense, per-step supervision by regressing the student's velocity field toward a pretrained teacher's prediction at every denoising step, which is stable and sample-efficient, but each new objective needs its own teacher, the student is capped by that teacher's quality and bias, and merging multiple teachers at the velocity-field level creates conflicting update directions. Self-OPD keeps the dense per-step structure and replaces the teacher with the student's own local exploration.

The mechanism, step by step. At each denoising timestep, the model computes its deterministic next-state prediction with a single forward pass. Around that prediction, it instantiates K candidate branches by drawing Gaussian perturbations under a reverse stochastic differential equation, with the noise coefficient set to zero point six in the experiments. Because every branch shares the same base prediction, the expensive transformer evaluation happens once, and the diversity comes from cheap noise. Each branch is then completed to a clean image by a deterministic rollout, decoded, and scored by the task reward model. In parallel, a fully deterministic rollout from the same parent state provides a self-reference baseline, and each branch's advantage is its reward minus the baseline, normalized by the standard deviation across branches. That self-reference is what makes the whole thing teacher-free: the comparison is against the student's own default trajectory, not against an oracle.

The update rule is an all-branch pull-push objective. Positive-advantage branches pull the velocity field toward their trajectories; negative-advantage branches push it away from theirs. Two pieces of engineering keep that signed objective stable. First, a direction-aware attenuation gate: if a low-reward branch points in nearly the same direction as the best branch, repelling it would cancel the attraction toward the good one, so the push is scaled down by the cosine between branch displacements, bounded between zero and one, keeping full repulsion only for branches that point opposite the best direction. Second, the loss is normalized by the stochastic-transition variance, which the paper derives properly: the perturbation in transition space is exactly a perturbation of the velocity target under an affine map, and the variance normalization makes each per-step regression an unbiased estimate of a reward-tilted K-L-minimization gradient, with a timestep-dependent precision rather than a free hyperparameter. Ablations punish both omissions: regressing only to the best branch, best-of-K, trains unstably and barely beats the baseline, and an unbounded push coefficient, allowed up to twice the pull, eventually destabilizes training and collapses GenEval to zero point seven six eight.

Multi-objective alignment gets its own treatment, and it is the part with the clearest payoff. The naive approach blends losses in parameter space, which triggers gradient interference, the seesaw effect where improving one metric degrades another. Self-OPD instead fuses the normalized scores into one composite reward and uses that composite only to rank sampled branches, never as a differentiable objective, so the actual regression target remains a concrete trajectory from the student's own neighborhood. The derivation shows the joint target collapses into a single reward-tilted distribution rather than several competing ones, which means one image is optimized to score high on all rewards at once, instead of different images each excelling under a different specialist teacher.

The evaluation uses Stable Diffusion three point five Medium as the base model, with three reward families: text rendering scored by character-level OCR accuracy, compositional generation scored by GenEval in both strict and continuous modes, and preference scored by PickScore and HPSv2. In single-reward training, Self-OPD reaches zero point nine five three six strict GenEval with the GenEval reward, ninety-seven point five percent OCR accuracy with the OCR reward, and beats both Flow-GRPO and GRPO-Guard on every task with identical base models and reward signals, which is a direct validation of step-wise self-distillation over terminal-reward policy gradients. In mixed-reward training, one model trains on all rewards simultaneously: Self-OPD hits zero point nine five two one strict GenEval, ninety-six percent OCR, and on images generated for the GenEval and OCR test prompts it scores twenty-three point eight seven PickScore and zero point three two one four HPSv2, against twenty-two point seven two and zero point two six seven six for the teacher-based DiffusionOPD, the strongest prior method, which needed three separately trained teachers. The distribution analysis makes the mechanism visible: DiffusionOPD's preference scores drop by one point two three PickScore points when images are generated on task prompts rather than aesthetic prompts, while Self-OPD's two distributions nearly coincide at a gap of zero point four eight, which is exactly the signature of selecting branches in the joint high-reward region instead of routing each prompt to a specialist.

Wall-clock numbers are the practical argument. DiffusionOPD's pipeline trains three teachers in parallel, taking forty-six point nine hours for GenEval, thirty-three point two for OCR, and eighty-five point eight for aesthetics, then eleven point three hours of student distillation, ninety-seven hours total, during which the student sits idle. Self-OPD from scratch reaches the teacher-based method's OCR level in roughly sixty-two hours and its GenEval level in roughly ninety. With a warm start from single-reward specialists trained in thirty-seven hours, the mixed run converges in roughly eleven and seven hours respectively, forty-four to forty-eight hours total, about twice as fast as the teacher pipeline while finishing higher.

The skeptical read. The honest wrinkle is in the table the headline glosses over: on a held-out aesthetic test set, DiffusionOPD actually scores higher on both preference metrics, twenty-three point nine five versus twenty-three point three nine PickScore and zero point three seven two nine versus zero point three four one five HPSv2, and the paper's defense is that joint satisfaction on the same image is the actual objective, which is a fair reading of multi-objective alignment but is also the protocol where it wins. Second, everything is one base model at one scale, Stable Diffusion three point five Medium, and the reward models are standard public scorers, so reward-hacking behavior, images tuned to the scorer rather than the intent, is measured only through those scorers themselves. Third, the exploration cost is real even if it is cheaper than teachers: every training step decodes and scores K branch images plus a baseline rollout, so the reward-model inference bill scales with branch count, and the paper does not itemize that cost against the teacher hours. Fourth, the K-L interpretation is elegant, but the proof covers the idealized per-step objective; the practical loss with advantage estimates and direction gates is an approximation, and the ablations, not the theory, are what justify it. What I would trust: the pull-push structure with bounded repulsion, the reward-level fusion for multi-objective work, and the wall-clock comparison. What I would re-verify: preference scores on prompts outside the reward models' training distribution, and stability at higher noise coefficients. Bottom line: Self-OPD shows that the densest available supervision for aligning a generative model is the model's own scored self-exploration, no teacher required, and it converts that idea into a two-times-faster, higher-scoring training recipe. If the numbers hold at other scales, the specialized-teacher step in the alignment playbook is on its way out.
