PUBG Ally: A Conversational Embodied Agent as an AI Teammate. The first three authors are Beomsoo Kim, Byeongju Kim, and Dohyun Kim, and the work is credited to the PUBG Ally team at Krafton, the company that publishes the game. This is a deployed system report, not a research prototype, and it is the closest thing to a production case study I have seen on the intersection of real-time embodied control and voice interaction.

The core difficulty, and the one the authors state up front, is that the two capabilities they want, real-time gameplay and natural voice conversation, do not merely add together, they have to stay synchronized. The agent must keep its speech consistent with what it observes, decides, and does as the match evolves, because a teammate that says it is covering you while the control layer does something else is useless. The setting is a scoped duo on the Sanhok map in battle-royale matches, one human player paired with the agent. The architectural answer is a strict separation into two layers, framed explicitly as System 1 and System 2 after Kahneman. A language-model agent is the System 2: it interprets player intent, coordinates with the player, produces speech, and selects high-level actions through a bounded tool interface that converts raw, rapidly changing game signals into compact textual observations and restricts the actions available to the agent. A deterministic behavior-tree layer is the System 1, translating those high-level decisions into movement, combat, recovery, and other latency-critical behaviors that have to run at the game's tick rate. The two layers are coupled, not independent: the language model sets the current intent, and the behavior tree carries it out while reacting immediately to changes in the game. The key engineering decision is that language-model inference is kept out of the real-time control loop entirely, which is the only way the whole thing runs at the game's cadence.
The data story is where this report is most unusual. The agent's speech and the human player's speech continually shape each other's behavior and the course of the match, so synthetic data cannot capture the loop. They rented a gaming cafe in Korea and organized full matches between real players and the agent. Across 28 collection days, 1,046 participants played 38,956 sessions, and the recordings include player speech, game events, the information the agent requested, tool results, the agent's responses, and the executed actions. They then used that corpus to iteratively train a small language model for on-device deployment. The training loop is DAgger-inspired: for the first two weeks, a 31-billion-parameter teacher model with a GEPA-optimized prompt played alongside humans to collect initial demonstrations. For the following two weeks, successive student versions played, so the collected rollouts captured situations arising from the students' own behavior. At each iteration they used the teacher to generate corrections for selected student interactions, trained an intermediate 8-billion-parameter teacher, distilled a deployable 2-billion-parameter student, and ran on-policy knowledge distillation. That 2-billion on-device model is what ships, and the latency number is the reason the whole architecture exists: a single spoken exchange completes in about 1.6 seconds on-device versus 3.4 seconds with the cloud configuration.
The evaluation section is the part I find most instructive, because it confronts a problem that most agent papers hand-wave: how do you measure the quality of a teammate? Combat performance and command completion do not capture whether players experience the agent as responsive, useful, natural, and cooperative. So they used player preferences, survey responses, and interaction records to refine an evaluation framework, specifically by hunting down the cases where their internal evaluations disagreed with what players actually preferred, and using those discrepancies to identify missing or poorly specified criteria. That iterative process led them to revise their measures of conversational quality, gameplay behavior, and cooperation, and to introduce evaluation scenarios grounded in situations observed during actual matches. That is a legitimately better methodology than picking a benchmark and calling it done, and it is the kind of thing that does not transfer cleanly to a paper but transfers well to a team.

The scoped setting is worth being explicit about, because it bounds the claim. This is a duo, one human plus the agent, on a single map in a single mode, with a bounded tool interface and a hand-built behavior tree for the low-level control. That is a deliberate narrowing, and it is what makes the whole thing shippable on a 2-billion on-device model within the game's latency budget. It also means the transferability of the specific control layer is limited, while the transferability of the architecture, the data loop, and the evaluation methodology is broad.

For deployment they had to solve a contextual safety problem that is specific to a game with a player-facing voice channel. Ordinary in-game combat language has to remain playable, while speech targeting real-world people or groups, unsafe escalation across turns, and unsafe information entering persistent memory have to be handled. They address it with model compression, context compaction, safety specifications, targeted safety training, runtime guardrails, and memory redaction. The live-service result: a two-week beta in English, Korean, and Chinese with locale-specific on-device language and speech models, surveyed across 141 countries. Among respondents whose play with the agent was confirmed in game records, positive responses exceeded negative by 25.1 percentage points on whether they would recommend it, and half of respondents framed the agent as a teammate or a companion rather than a tool.

One more detail on the interface that I think is the quiet load-bearing piece. Rather than giving the language model a fixed set of observations, the way most game agents do, the agent observes only the game information relevant to the current situation. It can query relevant game state, retrieve memory and game knowledge, access the player's communication, and issue high-level actions, all through the same bounded tool interface. That interface does two jobs at once: it keeps the perception context compact so a small model can reason over it within the latency budget, and it keeps the action space constrained so the agent cannot issue something the behavior tree cannot safely execute. The coupling between the two layers is what lets a deliberate language-model decision guide moment-to-moment behavior without ever placing an inference call directly in the control loop, which is the whole reason the 2-billion on-device model can hold the game's tick rate at all.

What I would trust here is the architecture and the data loop. The System 1 / System 2 split, with language-model inference kept out of the real-time path, is the right decomposition for any conversational agent that also has to react in real time, and the DAgger-style distillation from a 31-billion teacher down to a 2-billion on-device student is a clean, reproducible recipe for getting a capable small model out of a lot of real interaction data. What I would be cautious about is the evaluation. The 25.1-point recommendation margin is a real signal, but it is a self-selected survey of players who already opted in to play with the agent, and the paper does not give me enough of the capability-evaluation internals to judge how much of the teammate quality comes from the agent's game skill versus the polish of its voice. The bounded tool interface is also a strong assumption, and the whole approach depends on being able to convert the game's raw state into compact, faithful textual observations, which is a lot of hidden engineering that the report does not fully open up. If you are building any voice-enabled agent that shares a world with a human in real time, the layering and the distillation loop are worth stealing directly.

Bottom line: this is a rare, honest deployment report that pairs a well-motivated System 1 / System 2 architecture with a real data flywheel and a player-preference-driven evaluation loop, and the 38,956-session corpus is the asset that is hardest to replicate. Treat the survey numbers as a positive but self-selected signal, and steal the layering and the on-device distillation, which are the transferable parts.
