Human-to-Robot Transfer via Bridging Representation and Action Space in VLAs, is from Junhyeong Park, Wook Jung, and Taeyoung Kim, with the remaining authors at KAIST and the startup RLWRLD, which is where the OpenArm experiments are run.

Pretrained vision-language-action policies are a strong starting point for deployment, but adapting one to your specific robot and environment still costs you a pile of embodiment-specific robot demonstrations, and collecting those is the bottleneck. It requires the physical platform, a teleoperation pipeline, and repeated environment resets. Human demonstrations are dramatically easier to collect, anyone can do them with a lightweight camera, but they are hard to use directly because of the embodiment gap, and that gap is worst exactly when the underlying vision-language-action model was pretrained primarily on robot data, which is the common case. So you are in a bind: the cheap data is human, the expensive data is robot, and the model was trained on the expensive kind.

The prior work on bridging this gap mostly attacked the action side. You infer latent actions from frame transitions, or you reconstruct world-space hand motion, and you place human and robot demonstrations in a shared action space. The authors make a specific and, I think, correct observation that this only solves half the problem. A shared action space does not make the human demonstrations compatible with a robot-pretrained model, because the perceptual gap is still there. The human and robot observations are visually misaligned, and even when you have forced the actions into one space, the model still has to map distinct, embodiment-dependent observations into that shared space, which is exactly where reliable action prediction breaks down. They argue you have to bridge both interfaces, the representation and the action, not just the action.

Their framework is called H2RB, Human-to-Robot Bridge, and it is a co-training recipe that takes unpaired human and robot demonstrations, unpaired in the sense that they are not aligned shot-for-shot, and uses both to adapt a pretrained policy. It has two components, one per interface. The first is geometry-anchored alignment, which grounds human and robot observations in a compatible representation. The idea is to anchor both domains to a shared geometric reference so that a human hand reaching for a cup and a robot arm reaching for the same cup land in a perceptually comparable place, rather than forcing the model to learn that mapping implicitly from noisy co-training. The second component is a decoupled-decoder tokenizer that shares action latents across the two domains without forcing both through a single action mapping. A single shared action head is the standard choice, but it assumes the human and robot motions have the same realization dynamics, and they do not. A human reach and a robot reach that accomplish the same task are different control problems, so the tokenizer uses separate decoders, one per source domain, that decode from a shared latent action space.

The training is staged. The tokenizer is trained first, in its own stage, and then the policy fine-tunes on top of it, with the optimizer settings and trainable modules specified in their tables. The point of the staging is that the representation and action bridging are learned before the task-specific policy, so the policy is not trying to solve the embodiment alignment and the task simultaneously. They also report which parameters update at each stage, which is the kind of detail you want when you are trying to reproduce a multi-stage recipe.

So the two components, together, address the two halves of the embodiment gap. The geometry-anchored alignment handles perception: human and robot views of the same world become comparable inputs to the policy. The decoupled-decoder tokenizer handles action: the shared latent captures what the task requires, while each domain's decoder handles how its embodiment realizes it. Prior work treated these as one problem and solved it with one mechanism, the shared action space, and the paper's evidence is that one mechanism is not enough.
The results are real-robot, which is what gives this paper its weight. They use an OpenArm humanoid with Inspire hands, and all policies are initialized from a pretrained model. On the object-transfer task, where the target behavior is provided only through human demonstrations, H2RB improves performance from 8.0 to 51.8. That is the headline number, and it is a large jump from a near-zero baseline. On the skill-transfer task, it reaches 62.7, in settings where the co-training baselines, the ones that only bridge the action space, fail to transfer the human-demonstrated skills at all. And the gain holds under distribution shift: when they perturb the object placement, the out-of-distribution transfer improves from 6.0 to 45.3. So it is not an in-distribution fluke, the bridged representation generalizes to object placements the model has not seen.

The result I find most important is actually the negative one, that the shared-action-space baselines fail completely on skill transfer. In their skill-transfer table, the robot-in-domain tasks are fine, the Ego-Pi shared-action baseline reaches 70.5 in-domain, but on the human-transfer tasks, the ones whose target behavior comes only from human demonstrations, every shared-action method scores exactly zero, including HaWoR and Ego-Pi, while H2RB gets 50.7 on the open task and 74.7 on the close task, an average of 62.7, and holds 81.0 in-domain. On object transfer the comparison is less stark but still clear: the best shared-action baseline averages 27.2 against H2RB's 51.8, and H2RB does it while retaining 49.0 in-domain performance. That is the empirical confirmation of their core claim: bridging the action space is not enough, you also need the representation bridge, and the failure is not gradual, it is total, on the skill-transfer axis. If you have been doing human-to-robot transfer by forcing both domains through one action mapping and wondering why it does not work, this is the paper that explains why, and it gives you a concrete fix.

What would I trust and what would I re-verify? I trust the real-robot object and skill transfer numbers, because they are large, they are on a physical platform with real hands, and the negative baseline result is internally consistent with the method's motivation, which is a good sign that the numbers are not an artifact of a lucky baseline. What I would re-verify is the scale of the claim. These are results on a single humanoid, the OpenArm, with Inspire hands, and the magnitude of the 8 to 51.8 jump is the kind of result I would want to see on a second embodiment before I generalized it, because the geometry-anchored alignment is doing a lot of the work and its quality depends on how well the geometric reference transfers across morphologies, and a second arm with a different reach or a different hand would stress exactly that component. I would also re-verify the out-of-distribution number, 6 to 45.3, because the placement shift is a specific kind of perturbation and I would want to see how the method behaves under object-identity changes, not just placement changes. The engineering gotcha is the tokenizer staging: you have to train the tokenizer to convergence before the policy, and if the geometry anchoring is off, the whole downstream policy inherits the error, so the geometric alignment step is where I would spend my debugging time.

Two-sentence verdict. H2RB is a clean, well-motivated co-training framework that bridges both the representation and the action space for human-to-robot transfer, and it turns near-zero real-robot baselines into strong object and skill transfer while the action-only baselines fail outright. I would trust the real-robot results and the negative baseline, re-verify the gains on a second embodiment and under object-identity shifts, and treat the geometry-anchored alignment as the step most likely to need the most work in a new deployment.
