The paper is Meta Expert: Cross-Layer Expert Sharing for Storage- and Retrieval-Efficient MoE, from Ermo Hua, Xiangyu Hong, and Baiting Wu of Tsinghua University, with the broader team of fourteen authors including Che Jiang, Haozheng Hou, Yicheng Gu, and Hanjing Wang, splitting time with Shanghai AI Laboratory. This is an efficiency paper for mixture-of-experts models, and the problem it attacks is one of the quiet costs of the MoE architecture that has powered a lot of the recent open models. A standard MoE has an expert per layer, and the experts are not shared: a model with thirty-two layers and eight experts per layer stores thirty-two times eight separate expert networks. The redundancy is real, because neighboring layers learn similar routing behavior, and the cost shows up in two places. Storage: the total parameter count is the product of layers and experts, which is why MoE models are so large on disk even when the active parameters per token are modest. And retrieval: at inference, the router for each layer fetches the right experts, and if every layer has its own distinct set, the memory traffic does not benefit from any reuse across layers. Meta Expert proposes cross-layer expert sharing: instead of each layer owning its own hierarchically arranged local experts, the model adds inter-layer shared meta-experts that multiple layers draw from, so that the model matches the quality of a vanilla MoE while storing only seventy to eighty percent of the experts.

The mechanism deserves care, because there are a few ways you could do cross-layer sharing, and the paper's choice is specific. The baseline in modern MoE models is what the authors call the local expert: each layer has its own hierarchically arranged set of experts, and nothing is shared between layers. Meta Expert instead introduces inter-layer shared experts that sit alongside the local ones, so a subset of the expert pool is drawn from across layers rather than owned by a single layer. The storage arithmetic is the selling point: with the activated expert quantity and the expert granularity held fixed, the model matches vanilla MoE quality while using only seventy to eighty percent of the experts. The paper's framing is redundancy removal: the inter-layer comparison shows the shared model has lower parameter redundancy, a lower cross-entropy loss, and higher downstream accuracy than the local-expert arrangement at matched compute. There is also a theoretical analysis in the paper arguing the shared structure improves information retrieval efficiency, which is the second half of the title.

The retrieval side is the second half of the title, and it follows from the same design. In a vanilla MoE at inference, each token activates a small number of experts per layer, and the memory system has to fetch those experts from storage, which for a large model means substantial off-chip traffic. With cross-layer sharing, a given expert is fetched once and can be reused by the layers that share it within the same forward pass, so the distinct expert fetches per token go down even though the activations per token stay the same. The paper quantifies this as a reduction in the unique expert loads, which translates to lower memory bandwidth demand at fixed throughput, and the theoretical analysis in the paper connects the shared structure to more flexible residual pathways between hidden states. The combination is the point of the paper: the same architectural change that cuts the parameter count also cuts the inference-time memory traffic, and neither benefit requires any change to the training objective beyond adding the shared experts.

The evaluation compares Meta Expert against vanilla MoE with the same number of active parameters, and against other expert-sharing or pruning baselines, on language modeling perplexity and on downstream tasks in the general, math, and code families. The main result is that the shared model matches the vanilla model at the reduced expert count, and the ablations vary the sharing factor to show the quality-storage tradeoff curve, with the sweet spot in that seventy-to-eighty percent band where the accuracy loss is within noise of the vanilla model. The paper is twenty pages, and the training recipe is standard pretraining with the shared experts learned jointly with the rest of the model, so there is no exotic training procedure to replicate, which makes the result more reproducible than it might otherwise look.

The scaling framing of the paper is worth stating, because it is what the title's efficiency claim is really about. The authors study a family of MoE models across a range of sizes, from a model on the order of a few billion FLOPs of compute per token up through much larger ones, and in each case they compare the shared-expert model against the vanilla arrangement at iso-FLOPs, holding the activated quantity and the expert granularity fixed. The loss-ratio curve across the expert budget is the central plot: as the total expert count drops from one hundred percent of vanilla down toward seventy percent, the loss ratio of the shared model against vanilla stays within a narrow band around parity, with the smallest sizes showing the tightest match. The practical reading is that the redundancy they are removing is real redundancy, the vanilla architecture is paying for experts it does not need, and the shared design recovers most of that budget without a corresponding quality loss. That is a scaling story, not a one-off tuning result, which is why the authors frame it as improving the model's scaling potential: the same architecture can grow to the next size with a proportionally smaller expert store.

A concrete walk-through of the inference path makes the retrieval benefit tangible. In a vanilla MoE, a single token's forward pass touches, for each layer, the router plus the two or so experts the router selects, and because every layer's expert set is disjoint, the set of distinct expert weights streamed from storage is the sum over all layers. Under Meta Expert, a chunk of the selected experts is shared across the layers that draw from the meta-pool, so those weights are loaded once and reused within the pass. The paper's theory section ties this to the residual pathway: with inter-layer shared experts, the hidden state has more flexible routes between layers, and the retrieval cost per token drops without touching the activation count, which is why the title can promise both storage and retrieval efficiency from one change. The iso-FLOPs framing in the comparison is the right one to hold the authors to, because a naive expert reduction would also cut compute, and matching at iso-FLOPs means the quality comparison is not buying back parameters that the vanilla model spent on extra activation.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the storage reduction, because it is a direct arithmetic consequence of the sharing design, and I would trust the general-ability match at the reduced count, because that is the kind of result that shows up robustly across seeds in this literature. I would re-verify the retrieval claim in a real deployment, because the paper's metric is unique expert loads in the model, and the actual memory bandwidth of a serving system depends on the hardware, the batching strategy, and whether the experts fit in fast memory or have to come from the slow tier. The theoretical reduction in distinct fetches does not automatically become a measured latency win, and the gap between those two is where I would spend my time. I would also look at the math and code task results with extra care, because those are the benchmarks most sensitive to subtle capacity changes, and a within-noise perplexity result can still show a small but consistent drop on hard reasoning tasks; the paper reports gains in downstream accuracy, but I would want the per-task deltas at the seventy percent expert setting, the most aggressive one, before I would cut my own experts by a third on that strength of evidence.

Bottom line: Meta Expert is a clean, low-risk efficiency result for MoE models: inter-layer shared experts alongside the local ones, matching vanilla quality at seventy to eighty percent of the expert count, with a credible secondary benefit in inference-time memory traffic. The adoption case is strongest for teams serving large MoE models where disk and bandwidth are the binding constraint, and the main thing to verify before deploying is the measured serving latency, not the architecture, which the paper makes a convincing case for.
