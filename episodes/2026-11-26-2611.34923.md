Mixture-of-Loops Transformer, from Lianghui Zhu, Shijie Wang, and Xinggang Wang, at Huazhong University of Science and Technology, ByteDance Seed, and the University of Toronto. The claim: looped Transformers, which reuse shared layers across repeated loops to boost expressivity, can be made more efficient by rethinking what is passed from one loop to the next, and a new attention mechanism that treats keys and values as cross-loop memory closes the efficiency gap without adding parameters.

The problem is the expressivity-efficiency frontier of looped Transformers. A looped model reuses a small stack of shared layers many times, so it gets more effective depth without more parameters. The cost is that every loop reprocesses the full sequence, so inference time grows with the number of loops, and the naive way of connecting loops, the residual stream, carries the entire recurrent state forward. The paper reframes the design question as: what should be passed as a Transformer loops? The answer is Mixture-of-Loops Attention, abbreviated MoLA, which uses keys and values as a memory for selective reuse across depths and loops, rather than passing the full residual. There are two variants. MoLT-R keeps the loop residual for expressivity, and adds MoLA so each loop can read local keys and values from earlier loops. MoLT-KV drops the residual and instead restarts each loop from the embeddings, reading the current loop's local keys and values plus a memory of preceding loops' keys and values at earlier positions, which is the more efficient design.

The unification comes in a third variant, U-MoLT, which splits the stack into a looped encoder and a looped decoder. The encoder builds the memory across loops, and the decoder is scheduled independently of the encoder's recurrence. This separation has a concrete payoff at inference: U-MoLT can omit the intermediate-loop decoder traversals and run the decoder only at the final prompt position without changing the next-token prediction, because the strict-prefix memory reads at positions before the current one remove same-position cross-loop dependence. Every feedback loop reduces to a strictly triangular recurrence across token positions, which is what makes parallel prefill possible. Under a fixed two-loop execution, an equal-split U-MoLT matches a non-looped Transformer of the same total depth on full-prompt prefill block depth and quadratic prefill attention, while keeping decoding traversals and persistent key-value storage at the looped model's lower cost. That is the core of the efficiency claim: it closes the inference gap in two steps, first matching the non-looped baseline on the dominant prefill costs, and then adding only a small, predictable decoding overhead that scales with the fraction of the sequence length.

The training results are the empirical support. The scaling studies use a one hundred thirteen million parameter backbone, then 700 million and 1.5 billion dense models and a 3 billion mixture-of-experts model with 660 million active parameters, all trained on 400 billion tokens with the OLMo3 engineering pipeline. Relative to the OLMo3 baseline, average downstream accuracy increases by 2.5, 1.4, and 2.1 percentage points at 700 million dense, 1.5 billion dense, and 3 billion mixture-of-experts, respectively, and perplexity improves in each of ten domains at every scale, including a drop in mean perplexity from 13.99 to 13.24 for the mixture-of-experts model. On the 10-task suite, U-MoLT improves nine tasks and ties the remaining one at 1.5 billion, and improves all ten at the 3 billion scale. The component ablations are extensive: thirty-two small-model configurations are compared under a common training protocol, varying the feedback form, the depth source that the memory keys and values are read from, the dense-versus-sparse placement of the memory attention across layers, and the decoder design. The consistent ordering across those blocks supports separating encoder memory construction from decoder updates, and the sparse placement of the memory attention at alternating layers retains most of the quality at a fraction of the extra compute. There is also a continued-pretraining study on Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base over 100 billion tokens, where the U-MoLT adaptation lowers the cross-entropy on all eleven domains over the matched baseline with a mean token efficiency of 1.4 times, meaning it reaches the same quality with about a quarter fewer tokens.

The inference numbers are the payoff, and they are unusually clean. On the small model, a plain non-looped OLMo3 takes about 1,000 milliseconds for the full prefill-plus-decode test. A looped model running a fixed two-loop execution roughly doubles that, landing near 1,974 to 1,980 milliseconds, which is exactly the cost the loop topology was supposed to avoid. The MoL-Hybrid schedules bring it back down: with one-loop prefill the hybrid lands at 989.5 milliseconds, faster than the non-looped baseline, and with two-loop prefill it lands at 996.6 to 1,006.6, essentially matching the non-looped latency while carrying the looped model's extra effective depth. The hybrid schedule is what does the work: fixed-loop parallel prefill builds the prompt memory, and then decoding uses a single-traversal update per token, so the per-token cost stays at one traversal regardless of loop count. The training side uses a weighted sum of the per-loop cross-entropy losses, with normalized weights that let each loop contribute proportionally, so all loops are supervised rather than only the final one.

The skeptical pass. First, the quality gains are real but modest: 2.1 percentage points at the 3 billion scale is a meaningful edge, but it is the kind of margin that can shift with the data mixture, and the evaluation is on the OLMo3 pipeline with standard benchmarks, so the claim is about this recipe on this data, not a universal property of the architecture. Second, the efficiency claim is conditional on the hybrid inference schedule, which is a nontrivial systems contribution in its own right; if you run the model with fixed-loop execution you pay the doubled latency, so the architecture and the serving schedule are coupled, and the benefit disappears if you cannot implement the single-traversal decoding update. Third, the parameter and FLOP accounting in the ablations shows the dense memory variants add parameter overhead relative to the shared variants, with the shared projections saving over a million parameters at a small cross-entropy cost, so there is a real design space here and the paper's chosen point is a trade, not a free lunch. The Qwen continued-pretraining result, with 1.4 times token efficiency, is the most transferable number, because it says the adaptation works on a different tokenizer and data mixture, not just the OLMo3 stack. One more caution: the models are trained from scratch on the OLMo3 pipeline, so the result does not by itself establish that converting an existing large checkpoint to the looped form is easy, and the continued-pretraining study on a 0.8 billion model, not a frontier-scale one, is the only evidence on the adaptation path. The design is also a real systems burden: the memory attention, the hybrid prefill schedule, and the single-traversal decoding update have to be implemented together, and the paper's own code release is the main thing standing between the result and anyone trying to reproduce it, and until a second group reproduces the matched-latency inference numbers on their own hardware, the efficiency claim should be read as architecture-plus-schedule, not architecture alone.

Bottom line: a careful architectural contribution that fixes the main weakness of looped Transformers, the doubled inference cost, by moving cross-loop memory into keys and values and scheduling the decoder outside the loop. The matched-latency result with better quality is the headline, and the token-efficiency result on a different model family is the one I would trust enough to test in my own pretraining pipeline. The practical read is that looped models stop being a research curiosity the moment the serving schedule is in place, and the open question is whether the community adopts the KV-as-memory pattern or keeps paying the full residual cost for depth.
