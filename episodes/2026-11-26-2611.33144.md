PyRUA-Dex: Learning Programmatic Policies for Dexterous Bimanual Manipulation with Coding Agents, from Feixiang Ruan, Guodong Lv, and Kewei Zhang, at Peking University, the National University of Singapore, NVIDIA, Tongji University, and the University of Chicago. The claim: a coding agent, with no demonstrations at all, can write a program that drives a pair of twenty-two degree-of-freedom hands to play piano and do calligraphy, and the resulting policy beats every trained baseline on two dexterity benchmarks.

The problem is the data bottleneck in dexterous manipulation. Every standard route to high-quality demonstrations is broken. Retargeting from human video is imprecise, teleoperation gives the operator no force feedback, and exoskeleton-based collection is hard to scale. So the team's move is to stop collecting demonstrations entirely and instead have coding agents search for a program that does the task. The programmatic policy is learned in simulation in two stages. In exploration, multiple agents explore in parallel, looking for individual task skills and ways to compose them into longer sequences. A distilling agent then reads the successful attempts and condenses them into a task library plus a single program. In refinement, that program is improved against randomized layouts to buy robustness and execution efficiency. The finished program is frozen and shipped to the real robot, where it runs open-loop as a fixed sequence of skill calls.

There is a sim-to-real loop that is worth being precise about. The system is bidirectional: real-world execution feeds back into the simulation, so a program that fails on the physical hands can be repaired in the simulator and redeployed. The real platform has tactile sensing at the fingertips, which is the one physical signal the simulator has to approximate. In practice the authors validate a set of tasks in simulation, of thirty-two validation layouts, before moving them to the bimanual rig. That is the honest core of the contribution: a search procedure over program space, not a learned policy network, and a sim-to-real bridge that lets the program self-repair.

The benchmark results are where the programmatic approach earns its keep. On Bench2Dex, a bimanual dexterity leaderboard, PyRUA-Dex takes state of the art with a score of seventy-two point nine, beating the nine strongest four-channel entries. On DexJoCo, a different simulator with a different hand and no predefined task stages, it reaches an eighty-one point zero percent success rate under full randomization, with objects, cameras, lighting, and textures all varied. The margins are not uniform, and the paper is upfront about that. The widest wins are on the bimanual piano melody, ninety-nine point five percent against a thirty-two point zero percent baseline, plus ball sorting and baking-tray preparation. On eleven other tasks, a trained policy wins, and on three tasks the programs almost never succeed at all, because no agent solved one of their stages during development. That honesty matters: the method is not a universal replacement for imitation learning, it is a tool that shines where a task decomposes cleanly into composable skills.

The real-world numbers are the more interesting result, because they are the ones that survive contact with physics. On the bimanual platform, the frozen programs completed all thirty-two validation layouts for cube-to-basket and for the Ode to Joy piano piece, thirty of thirty-two for stacking cubes, twenty-nine of thirty-two for a third piano piece, and fourteen of thirty-two for the writing task. The writing task is the weak one, and it is the one that most resembles the tasks a real user would actually want, sustained fine motor control with a tool in the hand. On the DexJoCo side of the comparison table, the numbers put the programmatic policy in a class of its own: eighty-eight point five percent success with randomized objects and eighty-one point zero with full randomization, against a trained open-source policy at fifty-four point one and forty point three, and even a ten-times-budget online language model agent at eighty-seven point three with object randomization only. The program wins even against a reasoning agent with ten times the step budget, which is the cleanest possible statement of the development-time-versus-test-time trade.

The cost analysis is the part I would actually reuse. The headline framing is that the search moves the model's reasoning from test time to development time. Once a program is built, it runs open-loop for essentially free, whereas an online agent that reasons at every step burns thirty-three point seven million tokens per episode just to match the offline policy's full-channel completion rate of sixty-one point five percent. Building a task costs a median of four hundred twenty-seven million tokens and twenty-one point six agent hours in exploration, plus two hundred fourteen million tokens and fourteen point eight agent hours in refinement. Across all twenty-six tasks, total development consumed less than a third of the tokens the online agent would spend over the same episodes. The refinement stage is doing real work, too. The authors classify three hundred forty-one failed validation runs after exploration, and sixty-eight percent of them had returned with at least thirty percent of their step budget still unused, which means the failures were not compute-bound but logic-bound, a single stage function failed and every downstream stage was skipped. Refinement, by re-testing against randomized layouts, is where those single-stage bugs get found and fixed.

Now the skeptical pass, because there are real gotchas here. First, the sim-to-real bridge is the load-bearing assumption and it is only partially characterized. The system has tactile sensing on the physical hands, but the simulator's approximation of contact and slip is not the headline of the paper, and the real-world task set is small. Thirty-two validation layouts is a sample, not a distribution. If your task involves sustained contact, deformable objects, or precise force control, the open-loop frozen program has no corrective mechanism once it leaves the scripted trajectory. Second, the token cost is real even if it is amortized. Four hundred twenty-seven million tokens per task is a lot of frontier-model inference, and the median hides the tail: the hardest tasks are the ones where exploration runs long or never converges. The three tasks where no agent solved a stage are the honest estimate of the failure rate, and on those you are no better off than not having tried. Third, generalization is task-level, not skill-level. A program learned for piano does not transfer to a new piece without re-running the pipeline, though the paper does report that learning a new piece from sheet music takes as little as thirty minutes, which is a genuine and useful number. The task library is the real asset, and it is the part that would compound over time if you kept adding skills, which is the pitch that would convince me to look at this again when it scales beyond a single bimanual platform.

The engineering question I would ask before adopting this is whether your tasks decompose into discrete, verifiable stages. The method's success is tightly coupled to the existence of a stage boundary that a distilling agent can name and an executor can check. Where the decomposition is clean, the programmatic policy is cheaper and more robust than an imitation policy. Where it is not, you are paying a large development cost for a result that a trained policy gets for free. That is the honest boundary of the contribution. The practical takeaway is narrower and more useful: if you have a fleet of dexterous hands and a set of tasks that decompose into stages, the programmatic pipeline is a credible alternative to collecting demonstrations, and the task library it produces is an asset that compounds.

Bottom line: a well-executed demonstration that coding agents can replace demonstration collection for dexterous manipulation, with strong numbers on two benchmarks and a credible cost model. The sim-to-real bridge and the task-decomposition assumption are the two things I would re-verify on my own hardware before betting a deployment on it.
