CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements, by Xinlei Yu, Gen Li, and Ji Zhao, from MMLab at CUHK together with Tsinghua University, HKUST Guangzhou, and the Explore Academy at JD, is a data-fabric for computer-use agents, and the model it produces, CUA-Master-27B, is the evidence that the data-fabric works. The problem it starts from is the one that has constrained the whole field: computer-use agents are limited not by model capacity but by interaction data. The data is narrow in diversity, hard to control, and unreliable, because collecting it means running an agent in a live environment and hoping the trajectories are good. On top of that, the standard optimization pipeline has no iterative feedback, and the recursive-improvement approaches that do exist, where an agent generates its own experience and trains on it, tend to run into diminishing returns because self-generated data only ever contains what the agent already almost knows how to do. CUA-Master's answer is a nested double loop. The outer loop is a world-data-model flywheel at the system level: it keeps pulling in open-world corpus, and it uses the current model's evolving weaknesses to steer what data gets constructed next. The inner loop is at the data level, coupling open-world seeding with what the authors call autocatalytic propagation: seeding mines real workflows from instructional videos and reconstructs trajectories through a visual-semantic dual-path process, and propagation turns those seeds into reusable primitives from which diverse, complex, verifiable tasks can be synthesized on demand.

The most distinctive piece is the retry mechanism, a feedback-guided snapshot-rollback scheme. When an agent trajectory goes wrong in a real environment, instead of discarding the whole attempt, the system rolls the environment state back to a snapshot and retries from there, guided by the feedback about what failed. That turns a binary succeed-or-waste signal into a graded one, and it makes trajectory collection in live, non-reversible-by-default environments practical at scale. It is an unglamorous but load-bearing engineering decision, because most of the value in a trajectory dataset is in the near-miss trajectories, the ones that got ninety percent of the way there, and snapshot rollback is what lets you collect those.

The results are where the double loop pays off. CUA-Master-27B scores 80.2 on OSWorld Verified, 36.9 on OSWorld 2.0, 43.9 on WeaveBench, and 25.0 on Agents' Last Exam. Against UI-Mate-27B, a specialist of similar size, it improves by 2.6, 12.9, 25.6, and 11.8 points across those four benchmarks respectively, and the big margin is on WeaveBench, the hybrid GUI-plus-CLI benchmark, which is exactly where a data-fabric built from real open-world workflows should show its advantage, because those tasks combine desktop and command-line work the way actual software work does. The paper's own framing of the capability result is the right one: against the Qwen-3.5-27B-derived models, CUA-Master matches Qwen-3.8-27B on GUI-only tasks and then pulls far ahead in hybrid scenarios. And on the hardest benchmark, Agents' Last Exam, a 27B open model sits behind only GPT-5.6-Sol and Claude-Opus-5, which is a statement about where open computer-use models stand relative to the frontier. The efficiency numbers are a second, underrated contribution: on OSWorld Verified it reaches 80.2 in eight minutes of execution time, on WeaveBench it matches GPT-5.6-Luna with 28 percent less runtime, and on Agents' Last Exam it matches Kimi-K3 and Qwen-3.8-Max with 34 to 36 percent less time. A computer-use agent that is both stronger and measurably faster is a better product than either alone, and the speed comes from the data, not from a bigger model, which is the point the paper is making all along.

Cross-platform generalization is the third result, and it matters less in the headline but more in the practice: 56.2 on WindowsAgentArena, 43.0 on MacOSWorld, and 59.9 on MacAgentBench. A computer-use agent that only works on one operating system is a benchmark entry; one that transfers across Windows and macOS is a system, and the transfer here is across different window managers, different input conventions, and different screenshot scales, which is a genuinely harder ask than a single-platform score implies. The open-world data fabric, which is not tied to a single platform's screenshots, is the most plausible explanation for the transfer, and it is the part of the method a reader should take most seriously.

There is a second thing to notice about the numbers, which is that the efficiency and the quality improvements come from the same source. The model decides faster because it was trained on trajectories that are clean, verified, and short of dead ends, and it performs better for the same reason. A computer-use agent trained on noisy, self-generated data learns to hedge, to take extra verification steps, to re-read the screen before acting, and all of that habit is latency. Strip the noise out of the training distribution and the model stops hedging, which is why the runtime drops by a quarter to a third at the same or better accuracy. That coupling is the quiet argument of the paper, and it is the reason the data-fabric framing is not just a story about data quality but a story about the economics of deployment: a faster agent is a cheaper agent, and at the scale where computer-use agents are being deployed, the latency number is a cost number. It is also the reason I would weight the WeaveBench and Agents' Last Exam results more than the OSWorld Verified one. The verified benchmark is close to saturated for the strong models, so a 2.6-point gain there is real but modest, while the hybrid benchmarks are where the data diversity actually shows up, and the 25.6-point gain over the same-size specialist is the number that says the flywheel is producing something the specialist's data did not.

The honest caveats. The double-loop description is dense, and the paper's ablation of which loop contributes what is the thing I would want to read most carefully: a world-data-model flywheel plus an inner data flywheel is two compounding effects, and attributing the gains to the right one matters if you are deciding which piece to copy. The OSWorld 2.0 margin over the same-size baseline is small, 36.9 versus 36.5, so the headline story is really the hybrid benchmarks and the efficiency, and the paper is careful enough to say that. The data pipeline itself, mining workflows from instructional videos and reconstructing them visually and semantically, is the part that is hardest to reproduce, because it depends on the open-world corpus and the reconstruction quality, and the paper's numbers on data-quality improvements, like the reduction in execution steps of 23.8 percent over GUI-only training and 43.8 percent on OSWorld 2.0, are the proxy for that quality rather than a direct measurement of it. And Agents' Last Exam at 25 percent is still a quarter, which is a reminder that even the best computer-use agent, open or closed, fails three out of four of the hardest tasks.

My verdict is that CUA-Master is the strongest argument yet that computer-use agents are a data problem, and the 27B model that beats a 397B specialist on hybrid benchmarks while running a third faster is hard to argue with. The nested flywheel is the real contribution and the part worth stealing, the snapshot-rollback retry is the unglamorous piece that makes the whole thing work, and the open-model-versus-frontier gap on the hardest benchmark is the number that will age well. The main open question is how much of the gain is the outer flywheel versus the inner one, and the paper gives you the tools to find out. The field implication is that the next generation of computer-use agents will be defined by their data engines rather than their architectures, and this paper is the clearest statement of that bet yet, with a model that carries the argument.
