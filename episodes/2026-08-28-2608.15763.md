Training Agents to Evolve with Their Harness: TaoLive Digital Avatar Agent Technical Report, by the TaoLive AIGC LLM team at Alibaba, with Yuhan Sun and Wenhao Lin among the listed authors. The setting is live-stream e-commerce: an AI avatar answers product questions and runs marketing playbooks on air, where every reply is public, the latency budget is seconds, and the business rules change weekly. The engineering dilemma is real and worth stating plainly. Big models adapt to new rules zero-shot but are too slow; in their tests a frontier model's median end-to-end latency exceeded eleven seconds. Compact models meet the latency target but, trained on one fixed configuration, they memorize skill names, tool names, and prompt templates, and then fail exactly when the harness they memorized changes. Fixed-harness supervised fine-tuning costs seven point seven points on IFEval, a general instruction-following benchmark, which is the measurable signature of that overfitting.

Their production answer is an evolvable harness with four independently versioned modules: Skills, which are reply rules and strategies; Hooks, which are validation checkpoints that can force retries; a system prompt pipeline; and a tool registry wired to commerce services. Behavior changes ship as harness edits, not retraining. A human-in-the-loop process they call harness evolution diagnoses failures, proposes edits, and evaluates: on a development set of four hundred eighty-two cases annotated by three people, the first evolution round lifted accuracy from eighty-two point four to ninety-two point one but dropped effectiveness to eighty-four point two, the second round reached ninety-two point six and ninety-two point eight and was selected as the engineering stopping point, and further long-tail rules regressed one or both dimensions. That curve is a quiet warning worth the price of the paper: harness tuning has its own overfitting problem.

The training contribution is Harness-Aware Training, which makes the harness state part of the training distribution instead of a deployment constant. The core mechanism, harness-state augmentation, perturbs five surface dimensions while preserving task semantics: skill identifiers get renamed, masked, or padded with synthetic and noise skills; skill rules get paraphrased, masked, and reordered; tool names and descriptions get rewritten; system prompt blocks get reordered with numeric constraints perturbed within valid ranges; and hook retry behaviors get controlled variants. The hypothesis is that if names are unreliable, the model has to read the functional content. Training runs in three stages. First, supervised fine-tuning on teacher-generated trajectories under both original and augmented harnesses, ten thousand examples, filtered by a rejection-sampling judge, with one detail that shows production maturity: for most trajectories containing hook-triggered retries they strip the intermediate failures and keep only correct final behavior, but retain a small fraction of complete retry trajectories so the model still learns to interpret a hook nudge. Second, general on-policy distillation from the base model on the Tulu3 instruction dataset, explicitly to recover the general capability that domain fine-tuning damaged. Third, reinforcement learning in a production-informed live-room simulator with sandboxed service replicas and injected failures like timeouts and permission denials, using grouped optimization with four decomposed reward dimensions, accuracy, effectiveness, tool rationality, and skill selection, computed separately for tool-call and reply segments, plus a chain-of-thought length penalty that contributes zero to minus zero point one per state beyond one hundred tokens and saturates at two hundred. The simulator itself is built in four components worth copying: input simulation sampling from live-streaming scenario pools with simulated room state including product listings and viewer action signals; full harness-agent scheduling that executes skill routing and triggers hooks at the same checkpoints as production; tool execution against sandboxed service replicas returning product details and inventory from production catalog snapshots, with controlled failures like timeouts, malformed responses, and permission denials injected at calibrated rates; and augmented harness configuration aligned to the same declared change envelope as the offline stages, so offline supervision and on-policy rollouts never drift apart. A few more implementation details matter for anyone replicating this. The traffic is dominated by product questions at roughly forty-six percent, casual chat at nineteen, and clarification follow-ups at sixteen, with after-sales and discount queries filling most of the rest, so the reward weights were tuned against a real intent distribution, not a synthetic one. The reinforcement-learning objective uses grouped rollouts with advantages computed per reward dimension within tool and reply segments separately, sequence-level importance sampling borrowed from GSPO for stability under the sparse expert routing of the mixture-of-experts architecture, and zero-advantage trajectories discarded before the update because they dilute the batch without contributing gradient. The chain-of-thought penalty actually worked as a diagnostic: mean tool-call reasoning length fell from two hundred ninety point six to one hundred seventy-one point two tokens across training, and final-reply reasoning from ninety-three point six to thirty-seven, though the paper is careful to label this a training diagnostic, not evidence of causal latency gains. One more deployment trick: the speculative-decoding draft head was transplanted from the base model's native multi-token-prediction module and adapted on rollout sequences from the task-trained policy, which the paper shows converges, so RL and serving-speed engineering could proceed independently.

The results, across four evaluation sets totaling over four thousand five hundred cases. On real live-stream QA, the trained model scores ninety-four point eight average against eighty point three for the untuned compact base and ninety-three point zero for the best frontier model under the same judge. On harness-variant QA, the robustness test, it scores ninety-four point six against seventy-five point four for the base, and the gap between original and variant harnesses is only zero point two points, versus four point nine for the base and one point three for fixed-harness fine-tuning. On tool robustness it reaches eighty-four and prompt robustness seventy-seven point six, and IFEval lands at eighty-three point five prompt-level and eighty-eight point seven instruction-level, slightly above the base model rather than seven points below. The ablation table is the most instructive part: without augmentation, plain SFT raises task scores but drops prompt robustness four point six and IFEval seven point seven; adding general distillation recovers most of it; but plain reinforcement learning in a fixed environment then drags prompt robustness from seventy-two point six to sixty-six point seven, below the base model. With augmentation at every stage, the same reinforcement-learning stage instead nudges prompt robustness up. Deployment runs on a single NVIDIA H20 with speculative decoding: median latency three point four seconds, ninety-fifth percentile eight point one, every measured request inside the fifteen-second bound. And the online A/B test against a ReAct-style control recorded four point three three percent uplift in confirmed-receipt gross merchandise value, normalized per unique visitor, and zero point nine one percent in item-page views.

The skeptical read. The judge is the load-bearing wall: reply quality is scored by a harness-based model-as-judge, calibrated against four hundred eighty-two human-labeled cases, which is a real calibration effort but still a circular instrument when the same harness stack defines both the reward during training and the metric afterward. Second, the headline comparisons use the same protocol for all models, but the top models are accessed through an API whose P95 exceeded twenty-one seconds in their own replay, so the quality comparison is fair while the latency comparison is structural. Third, the A/B test has no stated duration, traffic share, or confidence interval, and GMV uplifts in live commerce drift with campaign calendars, so treat four point three three percent as the operator's claim, encouraging but unverified. Fourth, the training-dynamics figures are single-run diagnostics, as the paper itself says, not seed-averaged evidence. What I would trust: the harness-evolution regression curve, the fixed-harness versus harness-aware ablation, which is a clean controlled comparison of the central claim, and the latency engineering. What I would re-verify: judge robustness to adversarial replies, and whether the augmentation gains survive a second domain with different update cadences. Bottom line: this is a serious production report showing that an agent trained over a distribution of harness states, not a single configuration, keeps its general ability while gaining domain skill, and the seven-point IFEval recovery is the number that should make anyone fine-tuning against a fixed prompt stack uneasy. The general lesson for agent builders is to treat the harness the way domain randomization treats physics: train against its variability, not against one instantiation of it.
