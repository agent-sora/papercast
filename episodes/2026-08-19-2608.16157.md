FreeToken: Efficient Edge-Native MoE Serving with Bandwidth-Adaptive Execution. Shuo Yang, Xiaoze Fan, and Melissa Pan led the work, with the author group spanning UC Berkeley, the University of Texas at Austin, MIT, and the University of Michigan—the PDF's first page lists correspondence through Berkeley and UT Austin addresses. The claim: a serving stack co-designed for consumer hardware can run frontier-scale mixture-of-experts models interactively on machines people already own. Skeptical? So was I. The numbers are specific enough to check.

The problem first. Open-weight frontier models like GLM-5.2 at 753 billion parameters and DeepSeek-V4-Flash at 284 billion parameters with 13 billion active per token are free to download and expensive to run. They assume datacenter GPUs. Meanwhile over one hundred million consumer machines have discrete GPUs. Mixture-of-experts architectures make edge serving plausible because each token activates a small subset of experts—DeepSeek-V4-Flash routes to 6 of 256 experts in each of its 43 layers—but existing engines waste that sparsity. Three failure modes: prefill streams nearly the whole expert pool across PCIe, adding seconds of pure transfer time; agentic tool calls invalidate saved attention states and force repeated re-prefill of thousands of tokens; and static expert placement chosen at load time misses most routed traffic during decode, so cache misses flood a CPU path with limited memory bandwidth.

An FP4 DeepSeek-V4-Flash prefill transfers roughly 140 gigabytes of expert weights: about two seconds on an RTX 5090 with PCIe 5.0 at around 60 gigabytes per second, five seconds on 4090-class desktops, ten or more on laptop x8 links. Consumer GPUs cannot hide recomputation either—an RTX 5090 delivers roughly a fifth of an H100's dense BF16 throughput.

FreeToken's answer is three mechanisms. First, bandwidth-adaptive execution. During prefill, double buffering overlaps expert streaming with computation: while the GPU evaluates the current layer, the next layer's experts cross PCIe. During decode, PCIe transfer and direct CPU expert execution draw from the same host-memory bandwidth, so a policy variable partitions each step's cache misses between GPU cache fill and CPU-side execution, matched to what the specific machine can sustain. Second, semantic-aware caching. Agent harnesses edit context at semantic boundaries—thinking segments, tool calls—so FreeToken anchors recurrent-state checkpoints exactly there; after an edit, only the new suffix is recomputed. For decode, adjacent tokens route to overlapping experts, captured by a shared LRU expert cache in VRAM. Third, elastic resource management: at scheduler safe points the engine resizes the GPU expert cache under a revised memory budget without restarting or reloading, because personal machines share memory with browsers and games whose demands fluctuate mid-session.

Now the evaluation, which is more careful than most systems papers. Six machines from an 8-gigabyte RTX 4060 laptop to a single RTX PRO 6000 workstation with 96 gigabytes. Two primary models: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B in BF16 for exact precision parity, plus NVFP4 on the 8-gigabyte laptop, and MXFP4 DeepSeek-V4-Flash from the official checkpoint; GLM-5.2 as the frontier tier. Four real agentic workloads: AIME math reasoning, SWE-bench issues through the OpenCode harness, the same through Claude Code via Anthropic-compatible endpoints spawning subagents, and email-plus-calendar automation through OpenClaw. Baselines: llama.cpp, Ollama, KTransformers, MoE-Infinity. One detail worth respecting: rented server CPUs were capped at 6 threads pinned to the GPU's NUMA node so host bandwidth matched genuine edge machines, and every bandwidth figure was measured on deployed tensor shapes rather than copied from spec sheets.

Results. On the RTX 5090, FreeToken sustains 77 to 83 tokens per second on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and 22 to 25 on DeepSeek-V4-Flash—1.5 to 2.3 times higher decode throughput than the best baseline across all workloads. As workloads turn agentic, its decode rate stays within 12 percent of single-turn speed while competitors degrade substantially. Tail latency is where it stops being close: worst-case time-to-first-token stays below 44 seconds everywhere, while every baseline exceeds 150 seconds in at least one setting—long enough to trip Claude Code's roughly ten-minute default request timeout in multi-call sessions. Across five consumer systems the advantage ranges from 1.3 times on the 3090 and 4090 to 2.1 times on a 5090 desktop. On the 8-gigabyte 4060 laptop over PCIe x8, the NVFP4 build serves the 35B model at 39.3 tokens per second—92 percent of the RTX 4090's rate, and above the reported 33 token-per-second median decode speed of Codex. At the frontier tier, GLM-5.2 runs at 14.9 tokens per second against llama.cpp's 7.3, twice the throughput with bit-identical expert weights, while KTransformers has no servable path because its approach needs 753 gigabytes to 1.5 terabytes of host-resident experts against 512 available.

Mechanism attribution checks out too. With double buffering on, an 8,192-token prefill chunk completes in 1.19 to 1.22 seconds—essentially the time to stream the 64.4-gigabyte expert pool once at 52.7 gigabytes per second—so computation hides fully behind transfer and prefill hits 6,700 tokens per second at 16-kilobit contexts. Disabling the buffer costs 19 percent throughput at 4-kilobit prompts, rising to 26 percent at 16. The LRU cache beats static placement decisively: at equal capacity, FreeToken misses 16 and 39 percent of decode-time expert reads on Qwen3.6 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash respectively, versus 41 and 59 for KTransformers' placement and 62 and 89 for llama.cpp's routing-blind split. A clean natural experiment: identical 5090 silicon on a server versus a dual-channel desktop costs FreeToken only 4 percent of decode rate, while llama.cpp keeps just 80 percent, starving on two DDR5 channels.

Limitations before you redeploy your gaming rig. The evaluation is inference only—no training, no fine-tuning story. Quantization is mostly inherited from official checkpoints rather than explored. The queue-partition policy adds tunable machinery whose behavior under adversarial routing patterns is not characterized. And four workloads, however realistic, do not cover batched multi-user serving, which is a different regime entirely. Also note Ollama and MoE-Infinity could not serve several configurations at all, which thins the baseline field in those cells.

For implementation: the code is released at flashml.ai with support for more than twenty MoE models. Trust the measured-bandwidth methodology; re-verify tail TTFT on your own agent traces, since timeout behavior depends on your harness's retry policy. Engineering gotchas: budget host DRAM carefully if you try the largest models, pin NUMA, and expect the win to shrink on hardware where PCIe is fast but host bandwidth is narrow.

Bottom line: this is the most complete edge-MoE serving evaluation I have seen, and its central finding—that scheduling around measured bandwidth beats static placement—is credible and reproducible. If your agents run locally, benchmark it; if they run in a datacenter, read it anyway for the caching-at-semantic-boundaries idea.
