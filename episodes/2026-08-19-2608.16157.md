FreeToken: Efficient Edge-Native MoE Serving with Bandwidth-Adaptive Execution. Shuo Yang, Xiaoze Fan, and Melissa Pan led the work, with the author group spanning UC Berkeley, the University of Texas at Austin, MIT, and the University of Michigan—the PDF's first page lists correspondence through Berkeley and UT Austin addresses. The claim: a serving stack co-designed for consumer hardware can run frontier-scale mixture-of-experts models interactively on machines people already own. Skeptical? So was I. The numbers are specific enough to check.

The problem first. Open-weight frontier models like GLM-5.2 at 753 billion parameters and DeepSeek-V4-Flash at 284 billion parameters with 13 billion active per token are free to download and expensive to run. They assume datacenter GPUs whose clusters cost millions of dollars, and hosted APIs remain burdensome for individuals as agentic applications multiply inference demand. Meanwhile over one hundred million consumer machines have discrete GPUs—Steam alone reports more than 200 million monthly active users with discrete NVIDIA GPUs on roughly 72 percent of surveyed systems. Mixture-of-experts architectures make edge serving plausible because each token activates a small subset of experts—DeepSeek-V4-Flash routes to 6 of 256 experts in each of its 43 layers—but existing engines waste that sparsity in three distinct ways.

Failure mode one is prefill transfer. Although decode touches few experts per token, a prefill pass over thousands of tokens activates nearly the entire expert set, so the engine streams almost the whole pool across PCIe. An FP4 DeepSeek-V4-Flash prefill transfers roughly 140 gigabytes of expert weights: about two seconds on an RTX 5090 with PCIe 5.0 at around 60 gigabytes per second, five seconds on 4090-class desktops at PCIe 4.0, ten or more on laptop x8 links. On-demand fetching exposes that entire window as GPU idle time. Failure mode two is recomputation. Hybrid-attention and recurrent layers—gated DeltaNet in Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Kimi Delta Attention in Kimi-K3—compress context into single states worth hundreds of KV-cache tokens, so engines keep few checkpoints. Agent harnesses edit context almost every turn by deleting thinking segments or old tool outputs, invalidating every checkpoint after the edit point and forcing re-prefill of thousands of tokens on a GPU that delivers roughly a fifth of an H100's dense BF16 throughput. Failure mode three is static placement: llama.cpp assigns MoE tensors at load time and KTransformers pins a hot expert subset, but routing shifts with every token, so frozen placement misses most routed traffic and floods the CPU path, which has limited memory bandwidth.

FreeToken's answer is three mechanisms. First, bandwidth-adaptive execution. During prefill, double buffering overlaps expert streaming with computation: while the GPU evaluates the current layer, the next layer's experts cross PCIe. During decode, PCIe transfer and direct CPU expert execution draw from the same host-memory bandwidth, so a policy variable partitions each step's cache misses between GPU cache fill and CPU-side execution, matched to what the specific machine can sustain rather than a fixed offloading strategy. The partition matters because both paths compete for one resource: whatever host bandwidth GPU cache filling does not consume is precisely what concurrent CPU expert execution can use, so the split is set against measured host bandwidth, not CPU core counts. Second, semantic-aware caching. Agent harnesses edit context at semantic boundaries—thinking segments, tool calls—so FreeToken anchors recurrent-state checkpoints exactly there; after an edit, only the new suffix is recomputed. For decode, adjacent tokens route to overlapping experts, captured by a shared LRU expert cache in VRAM that absorbs most routed reads and leaves residual misses to the partition policy. Third, elastic resource management: at scheduler safe points the engine resizes and rebuilds the GPU expert cache under a revised memory budget without restarting or reloading, because personal machines share memory with browsers and games whose demands fluctuate mid-session. Startup also improves: experts load directly into their final host layout before memory pinning.

Now the evaluation, which is more careful than most systems papers. Six machines from an 8-gigabyte RTX 4060 laptop to a single RTX PRO 6000 workstation with 96 gigabytes and 512 gibibytes of host memory. Two primary models: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B in BF16 for exact precision parity across engines, plus its official NVFP4 release on the 8-gigabyte laptop, and MXFP4 DeepSeek-V4-Flash from the official checkpoint; GLM-5.2 with NVFP4 experts as the 433-gigabyte-checkpoint frontier tier. Four real agentic workloads: AIME math reasoning with long chain-of-thought decoding, SWE-bench repository issues through the OpenCode harness over three scripted user turns, the same issues through Claude Code via Anthropic-compatible endpoints that spawn concurrently requesting subagents and grow sessions, and email-plus-calendar automation through OpenClaw. Baselines: llama.cpp, Ollama, KTransformers, MoE-Infinity—with Ollama and MoE-Infinity lacking DeepSeek-V4 support entirely. One methodological detail worth respecting: rented server CPUs were capped at six threads pinned to the GPU's NUMA node so host bandwidth matched genuine edge machines, measured at 56.7 to 77.3 gigabytes per second against 53.8 on the real desktop and 47.5 on the laptop, and every bandwidth figure came from deployed tensor shapes rather than spec sheets. The spread across those machines is the design constraint in one glance: measured host-to-device transfer runs 52.7 gigabytes per second on the 5090 server, about 25 on the 4090 and 3090, 49.0 on the consumer desktop, 11.8 on the laptop's x8 link, and 51.5 into the workstation, while CPU-side expert-kernel bandwidth lands between 47 and 178 depending on memory channels—a five-to-one hardware ratio no static policy can span. Not every frontier model fits this treatment: Kimi-K3 ships open weights but exceeds what consumer host memory can hold, which is why the evaluation set stops at GLM-5.2.

Results. On the RTX 5090, FreeToken sustains 77 to 83 tokens per second on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and 22 to 25 on DeepSeek-V4-Flash—1.5 to 2.3 times higher decode throughput than the best baseline across all workloads. As workloads turn agentic, its decode rate stays within 12 percent of single-turn speed while competitors degrade substantially. Tail latency is where it stops being close: worst-case time-to-first-token stays below 44 seconds everywhere, while every baseline exceeds 150 seconds in at least one setting—long enough to trip Claude Code's roughly ten-minute default request timeout across multi-call sessions. Across five consumer systems the advantage ranges from 1.3 times on the 3090 and 4090 to 2.1 times on a 5090 desktop. On the 8-gigabyte 4060 laptop over PCIe x8 at 11.8 gigabytes per second measured bandwidth, the NVFP4 build serves the 35B model at 39.3 tokens per second—92 percent of the RTX 4090's rate, and above the reported 33 token-per-second median decode speed of Codex. At the frontier tier, GLM-5.2 runs at 14.9 tokens per second against llama.cpp's 7.3, twice the throughput with bit-identical expert weights and comparable mean time-to-first-token at 7.5 versus 7.8 seconds, while KTransformers has no servable path because its GLM-5.2 methods need 753 gigabytes to 1.5 terabytes of host-resident experts against 512 available.

Mechanism attribution checks out too. With double buffering on, an 8,192-token prefill chunk completes in 1.19 to 1.22 seconds—essentially the time to stream the 64.4-gigabyte expert pool once at 52.7 gigabytes per second, the practical PCIe 5.0 ceiling—so computation hides fully behind transfer and prefill hits 6,700 tokens per second at 16-kilobit contexts. Disabling the buffer costs 19 percent throughput at 4-kilobit prompts, 25 percent at 8, and 26 percent at 16, growing with prompt length as more computation becomes hideable. The LRU cache beats static placement decisively: replaying identical routing traces from all four workloads at equal cache capacity, FreeToken misses 16 and 39 percent of decode-time expert reads on Qwen3.6 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash respectively, versus 41 and 59 for KTransformers' prefill-updated placement and 62 and 89 for llama.cpp's routing-blind split—and the ordering holds across workloads at every capacity short of the full pool. Caching cannot eliminate misses entirely: cold starts, abrupt working-set shifts, and limited capacity guarantee residual traffic, which is exactly what the partition policy is sized against. A clean natural experiment seals it: identical 5090 silicon on a server versus a dual-channel consumer desktop costs FreeToken only 4 percent of decode rate, while llama.cpp keeps just 80 percent, starving on two DDR5 channels.

Limitations before you redeploy your gaming rig. The evaluation is inference only—no training or fine-tuning story. Quantization is mostly inherited from official checkpoints rather than explored. The miss-partition policy adds tunable machinery whose behavior under adversarial routing patterns is not characterized. Four workloads do not cover batched multi-user serving, which is a different regime. And several baseline cells are simply unfillable because competitors cannot serve the models at all.

For implementation: code is released at flashml.ai supporting more than twenty MoE models. Trust the measured-bandwidth methodology; re-verify tail latency on your own agent traces since timeout behavior depends on your harness's retry policy. Gotchas: budget host DRAM carefully on the largest models, pin NUMA, and expect the win to shrink on hardware where PCIe is fast but host bandwidth is narrow.

Bottom line: this is the most complete edge-MoE serving evaluation I have seen, and its central finding—that scheduling around measured bandwidth beats static placement—is credible and reproducible. If your agents run locally, benchmark it; if they run in a datacenter, read it anyway for the caching-at-semantic-boundaries idea.
