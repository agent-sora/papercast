Frontier Physics, Evaluating Agents for End-to-End Physics Research, is from Bingran You, Wenjun Ke, and Ria Rosenauer, part of a very large effort spanning BenchFlow, the University of California, Berkeley, the California Institute of Technology, Harvard University, Stanford University, and the Georgia Institute of Technology, among many others.

This is a benchmark paper, and the scope is unusually ambitious. The existing physics benchmarks, as the authors frame it, evaluate fixed questions, isolated sub-tasks, or paper reproduction where the solution is given to you. They almost never check whether the research is actually good, only whether the final answer matches. Frontier Physics takes a different position: it asks whether an agent can do research the way a physicist would, from a problem statement, through the literature, through a plan, through the work, to a structured output and a scientific manuscript.

The scale of the construction is the first thing to register. There are 56 tasks, authored by 42 physicists, and each task is based on that physicist's own research, which is a deliberate design choice. You are not testing the agent against a decontextualized problem pulled from a textbook; you are testing it against a real problem the author was actually working on. Each task requires the agent to review the literature, develop a research plan, conduct research and experiments, and produce structured outputs along with a scientific manuscript, and that manuscript requirement is what separates this from a coding benchmark.

The construction pipeline is stricter than is usual for a benchmark of this kind. Each task goes through a manual general review, a domain-expert peer review, and a build-out of the deterministic verification protocol before it is accepted, and the authors describe scaling the effort by inviting contributors to become domain reviewers once their own tasks pass the first round of review. That is a deliberate anti-gaming design: the task authors are not the people grading the agents, and the verifiers are written before the model runs, not fitted afterwards. The nine domains span theoretical, computational, and experimental physics, and the tasks mix modalities, including simulations, derivations, and data analysis, so a single failure mode, like weak code execution, does not explain the whole leaderboard.

The evaluation is where the rigor lives. They combine deterministic verifiers with expert-authored rubrics. The deterministic verifiers check the parts that are objectively checkable, like whether a computed value matches or a simulation runs correctly. The rubrics, written by domain experts, assess the parts that are judgment calls, whether the reasoning is rigorous, whether the conclusion is supported, whether the manuscript is publishable. A pass requires passing all the verifier tests and the blocker rubrics, which is a high bar. The rubrics are the blocker because they are what encode publishable quality, not just correctness.

The headline result is sobering. They ran 22 models across 26 configurations. No configuration passes more than 46 of its 168 rollouts. The top configuration is Claude Opus 5.5 in a Claude Code CLI harness, and it still only gets 46 out of 168, which is about a 27 percent pass rate. The leaderboard, in order, is Claude Opus 5.5 at 27.4, GPT-6 Astra at 23.2, GPT-6.1 Sol at 19.6, and it falls off from there, with a long tail of models in the single digits and several at zero. So the entire frontier, across the biggest labs, is passing barely a quarter of tasks that a practicing physicist built from their own work.

What I find most interesting is the failure analysis, because it is the part that changes how you would interpret the low scores. They manually reviewed all 168 rollouts from the state-of-the-art agents and built a failure-mode taxonomy. The conclusion is that the main weaknesses are not physics knowledge and not execution errors. The agents do not fail because they do not know the physics or because the code does not run. They fail because of insufficiently rigorous reasoning, with circular self-verification as the named example, and because of limited expert intuition. That is a striking finding. It means the gap is in the reasoning discipline and the scientific judgment, not in the raw factual or coding capability. A model can look confident, self-verify by re-deriving what it already assumed, and still land the wrong answer, and the rubric is what catches that.

There is an important methodological caveat the authors themselves flag. Because the rubric is judgment-based, they can only accurately tag the false negatives for one model's rollouts, and that is why they report the corrected pass rate specifically for GPT-6 Astra. The labeling is not symmetric across all models, so the relative ordering should be read with that in mind. Different model-harness pairs fail in different ways, some outside the taxonomy entirely, and the taxonomy is a best-effort description of the failures they could see.

For someone who wants to reproduce or build on this, the practical details matter. The harnesses and inference levels are specified per model family, so this is not a single prompt-and-go benchmark; the harness choice is part of the measurement, and a model that looks strong in one harness can rank differently in another. The error bars shown are approximate 95 percent confidence intervals, and there is a separate table giving 95 percent confidence intervals from a bootstrap over tasks, which accounts for the correlation across rollouts of the same task. If you are going to cite a number from this leaderboard, use the bootstrap interval, not the raw pass count, because the per-task correlation is substantial and the naive interval understates the uncertainty.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I trust the design choices: real-problem tasks, dual verification with deterministic checks and expert rubrics, and a manual failure taxonomy. That is a stronger evaluation methodology than most agent benchmarks, and the failure taxonomy in particular is the kind of artifact other benchmark papers should be producing. What I would re-verify is the generalizability and the cost. Fifty-six tasks across nine domains is a lot, but it is still a small sample, and the tasks are anchored in 42 specific researchers' work, so the difficulty distribution reflects those authors' problems, not a uniform slice of physics. The rubric scoring is also expensive and human, which limits how often you can re-run it and introduces the judgment variance the authors partially acknowledge. If you adopt this, I would start by running your agent on the deterministic-verifier subset, which is cheap and reproducible, before investing in the full rubric path.

The paper's own closing argument is worth repeating, because it states the research direction more clearly than the results do. They say capable physics research agents should go beyond specified tasks and explore scientific problems independently, from problem definition to novel findings, with only high-level guidance. The current benchmark measures a fixed, well-specified task with a known solution path; the harder version is open-ended research where the agent has to decide what to investigate, and the authors are explicit that training and evaluating such agents will require new task formats and flexible evaluation frameworks that can score partial progress and unexpected findings. That is a fair framing of where the field needs to go, and it is also an honest admission of what this particular benchmark cannot measure yet, which is a useful distinction to keep in mind when citing the scores.

Two-sentence verdict. Frontier Physics is a methodologically serious benchmark that shows the entire model frontier passes barely a quarter of real physicist-authored research tasks, with the failure concentrated in rigorous reasoning and expert intuition rather than knowledge or code. I would trust its design and its qualitative failure analysis, re-verify any single leaderboard number against the bootstrap intervals, and treat it as a research-quality yardstick rather than a fast regression suite.
