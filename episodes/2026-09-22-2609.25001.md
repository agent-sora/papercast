GameHorizon Suite: Multi-Horizon Data and Evaluation in Gameplay. The first three authors are Yiran Wang, Xingyilang Yin, and Junfu Pu, from the ARC Lab at Tencent, with co-authors at Great Bay University, the National University of Singapore, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, CUHK, and the University of Macau.

The motivation is a measurement gap. Video games are a legitimate testbed for AI because a single game exercises visual understanding, instruction decomposition, goal planning, and precise action control all at once, and the objectives naturally span temporal horizons: grab an item in a few seconds, win a fight over a few minutes, execute a strategy that unfolds across a session. But the field has split into two model families that are never measured together. Dedicated game agents, built as lightweight vision-language-action policies, are optimized for high-frequency acting and have no capacity to plan. General-purpose vision-language models and computer-use agents are strong at planning, but their action execution has never been systematically measured; the few reported cases, like frontier models playing Pokemon, run on bespoke harnesses that cannot be compared across models. Everything in between is either confined to one game, or to Minecraft, or to mini-games; it lacks language instruction annotations; and its online evaluation runs three to twenty rollouts per model, which is statistically meaningless.

The suite has three parts. The annotator is a bottom-up pipeline that produces a pyramid of instructions at three horizons from the same recording: short-horizon operations describing what the player is doing in the current moment, medium-horizon goals that group those operations, and long-horizon strategies that group the goals. Working bottom-up is deliberate: abstracting fine-grained actions into higher levels is far more reliable than writing long-horizon strategies from scratch and decomposing them, and it guarantees temporal alignment. The dataset, GameHorizon-Data, is 5,000 hours of 2K-resolution recordings from 21 AAA game titles across open world, action role-playing, competitive shooter, sandbox survival, and creature-collecting genres, collected from 100 experienced human players through a dedicated recording system that captures game video and timestamped keyboard and mouse actions synchronously. That matters methodologically: prior datasets recover actions with inverse dynamics models or gamepad segmentation, and the paper points out those pseudo-labels deviate from what the human actually did. The benchmark has two tracks. The offline track is 5,000 multiple-choice questions: 1,000 each for the three primary tasks, single-horizon action prediction, multi-horizon goal decomposition, and cross-horizon consistency, plus 10 diagnostic variants of 200 questions each that isolate specific capabilities, such as current-action perception versus future-action planning under three input conditions, top-down decomposition versus bottom-up abstraction, and instruction-only versus instruction-and-action options. The online track is stepwise: 10 causal and 10 thematic tasks with 62 short-horizon subtasks, run in Minecraft because most AAA games do not expose underlying state. When a subtask fails, the environment resets to the success state so evaluation continues, which localizes failures to specific steps instead of collapsing everything into one success rate.

The scale of the study is unusual for this area: 47 models across five families, 35 general-purpose VLMs, 4 unified multimodal models, 2 GUI agents, 3 coding agents, and 3 dedicated game agents, through more than one million model invocations. The dedicated game agents cannot do question answering, so they are scored on the single-horizon action task alone by Needleman-Wunsch sequence alignment of their predicted action sequences against the four options. The headline numbers: mean accuracy across all models is 64.7 percent, well above the 25 percent random baseline, with a 35.6 percentage point spread from the best model to the worst. GPT-6 Astra leads at 80.2 percent overall, followed by Gemini 3.8 Flash at 77.3 percent and Gemini 3.7 Flash at 76.7 percent; Kimi K3 is the only open-weight model in the top tier at 74.5 percent, and recent open models like Qwen 3.8 27B and Gemma 4 31B outperform several proprietary ones. The task hierarchy is the interesting result: single-horizon action prediction is the hardest at 57.3 percent, decomposition is in the middle at 65.1 percent, and cross-horizon consistency is easiest at 71.6 percent. That ordering says the bottleneck in current models is fine-grained action prediction under strict correctness criteria, not planning. One of the diagnostic variants quantifies the value of their own annotation: giving models the medium- and long-horizon instructions improves future-action planning by 7.2 percentage points over frames alone. On the online track, 12 models sampled from the offline tiers show a clear positive association between offline scores and actual gameplay success, which validates the offline track as a cheap proxy, and the failure analysis shows models that understand the intended operation still commit execution errors, like placing objects inaccurately or following the wrong recipe.

The skeptical read. The offline track is a multiple-choice benchmark over video clips, so it measures recognition of actions and goals more than the ability to produce them, which is exactly why they needed the online track as a cross-check. The 7.2 percent instruction benefit is encouraging but measured on their own clips and their own instruction pyramid, so it is a demonstration that multi-horizon labels carry information, not a guarantee of transfer to your own data. The online track, despite its stepwise design, still lives in Minecraft, the one genre where the underlying state is accessible, so its conclusions about AAA gameplay are indirect. The dedicated game agents enter only on task 1, which means the cross-family comparison is partial by construction. If you are building anything that plays or watches games, the part worth lifting is the protocol: human-recorded actions rather than inverse-dynamics pseudo-labels, a bottom-up multi-horizon instruction pyramid, thousands of standardized offline questions as the cheap first screen, and a stepwise online track with reset-on-failure for failure localization.

Bottom line: GameHorizon is the most complete measurement infrastructure for gameplay capability published to date, with the scale to separate model families and horizons that prior single-game benchmarks could not. Use the offline track as your fast filter and the online track as your validation, and take the central finding seriously: current models plan better than they act, and fine-grained action prediction is the layer to attack.

The cross-family findings are where the benchmark earns its keep. The three dedicated game agents sit near the 25 percent random baseline on unseen AAA games: JARVIS-VLA scores 28.1 percent zero-shot, and its score can even fall below random because action sequences that match no option, including empty sequences, count as incorrect. The same JARVIS-VLA scores 54.2 percent after fine-tuning on Minecraft VPT data, essentially matching a zero-shot Gemma 4 31B, which is the clearest demonstration in the paper that action policies trained on one game do not transfer to AAA gameplay. Coding agents generalize better than you might expect, with Claude Fable 5 ranking 13th overall, while GUI agents land in the bottom tier, and one of them trails its own general-purpose baseline, suggesting domain-specific post-training can hurt cross-domain generalization. The unified multimodal models are weak across the board, and the paper reports a concrete interference effect: a unified model underperforms its own understanding-only backbone by 1.4 points. The thinking-model ablation is a useful caution for anyone running reasoning models in real time: enabling thinking helps capable models by 5.6 to 9.3 points, with the gains concentrated on the decomposition task, but it hurts lightweight models by up to 8.2 points, because their extended reasoning hallucinates rather than plans. The variant tasks quantify the instruction pyramid's value cleanly: adding short-horizon instructions to frames alone lifts current-action perception by 14.1 points with no fine-tuning, while multi-horizon instructions are what move future-action planning, and bottom-up abstraction from fine actions to goals is solved at 98.4 percent accuracy against only 69.5 percent for top-down decomposition, a 28.9 point gap that justifies the whole bottom-up annotation design.

One correction to my earlier framing, from the numbers: the headline 7.2 point future-planning improvement from multi-horizon instructions is the abstract's figure, while the four-model variant table reports a 7.0 point average; they are the same measurement at different rounding. On the online track, the top model, GPT-6 Astra, passes 45 percent of long-horizon tasks end to end, and the best short-horizon subtask success rate is 66 percent, so even the strongest model completes less than half of a long task without an intermediate failure. The offline-to-online association is positive but imperfect at the individual model level, which the paper acknowledges by sampling only three models per tier.
