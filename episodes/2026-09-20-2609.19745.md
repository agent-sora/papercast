Region-Level Policy Optimization for Fine-grained MLLM Perception. The first three authors are Yuheng Shi, Xiaohuan Pei, and Minjing Dong, from the University of Sydney and the City University of Hong Kong, with the final author Chang Xu at the University of Sydney.

The paper addresses the cost of fine-grained visual perception in multimodal large language models, the systems that answer questions about specific details in an image. The standard fix for fine-grained perception is to raise the input resolution, but higher resolution means more visual tokens, which inflates both the vision-encoding cost and the language-model prefill cost, and answer-relevant evidence often occupies only a small fraction of the image. The paper's starting observation is that fine-grained perception involves two distinct operations, localizing the region of interest and then recognizing its content, and that these two operations have different resolution requirements. The method that follows from that observation is called Vision-RL2, a region-level reinforcement learning approach that optimizes a region proposal network with a signal that standard training cannot provide.

The key piece of evidence is a controlled diagnostic built on ZoomBench with Qwen3.5-4B. The model predicts a region of interest box from the scene and then answers from the resulting crop, with the crop capped at at most 128 visual tokens. On a filtered set of cases that the pipeline answers correctly at full resolution and that are not solvable from the question alone, the authors run two sweeps. In the localization sweep, they compress only the scene from which the box is re-predicted, leaving the crop untouched. In the recognition sweep, they freeze the box and compress its crop. Survival is the fraction of cases still answered correctly. The result is that localization tolerates roughly three to four times stronger token compression than recognition. A concrete example from the paper: the box is still re-predicted correctly at 25 times token reduction, while reading the crop fails at 9 times. That measured gap is the quantitative basis for the whole method: localize from a coarse, heavily compressed view, and reserve the resolution budget for the selected evidence.

The engineering problem the method then solves is that the region proposal network, which predicts where to look, is hard to train with the usual tools. The baseline system, SD-RPN, is a proposal network attached to intermediate layers of the multimodal model that predicts an answer-free region of interest map in a single pass, trained on token-wise pseudo-labels distilled from the model's attention over the image. That attention-based supervision is efficient, but it has a structural flaw: the attention targets may include spurious activations, and the pseudo-labels are never checked against the answer. The problem is that the region of interest map reaches the final answer only through a discrete region choice. The map is binarized, regions are extracted, and the selected crop is re-encoded, so no gradient connects the final answer back to the map. Standard supervised learning cannot close that loop, and direct geometric supervision is also insufficient, because in fine-grained visual question answering the region of interest is not uniquely annotated; it is an arbitrary question-relevant region, not a well-defined object.

Vision-RL2's answer is to supply the missing signal with region-level reinforcement learning. Built on the trained SD-RPN predictor to keep the single-pass efficiency, the method treats coherent regions as the action space. A reader model scores every proposed region's contribution to the answer, and that per-region score becomes the reward that updates the proposal network. The design delivers exactly what the predictor was missing: an answer-level signal with region-level credit, a per-region measure of whether keeping that region helped or harmed the answer. The reward structure distinguishes helpful regions from harmful ones, so the network learns not just where to look but where not to look, which is the part attention distillation cannot teach it because attention does not know which regions were distracting.

The result on Qwen3.5-4B is that the method matches the accuracy of SD-RPN using 4.2 times fewer visual tokens. That is the headline number: same accuracy, a quarter of the visual-token budget. The mechanism is the resolution decoupling from the diagnostic, the coarse localization view and the high-resolution crop, implemented on top of a proposal network that has been corrected by answer-level reinforcement rather than left to attention distillation.

What would I trust? The diagnostic, which is the intellectual core of the paper. The controlled compression sweeps, localizing the scene versus freezing the box and compressing the crop, with survival as the metric, are a clean way to measure the asymmetry between the two perceptual operations, and the three-to-four-times gap is exactly the kind of measured, falsifiable result that motivates an architecture. The identification of the training problem, that the region map is disconnected from the answer by a discrete region choice so no gradient flows back, is a correct and important observation, and it explains why attention distillation, the natural default, leaves the predictor with uncorrected noise. Region-level reinforcement as the fix is the right shape of solution for that specific gap, because it is the standard way to optimize through a discrete decision.

What would I re-verify before building on it? First, the reader model that scores each region's contribution is itself a model, and the reward it produces inherits its errors. If the reader systematically misjudges which region mattered, the proposal network learns to propose for the reader's bias rather than for the answer. I would want to see the reader's agreement with the ground-truth evidence region on the diagnostic set, because that is the ceiling on how well the reinforcement can correct the predictor. Second, the 4.2-times token reduction is measured against SD-RPN on Qwen3.5-4B, a single model at a single scale, and the cap of at most 128 visual tokens on the crop is part of the protocol. I would want to know whether the ratio holds as the crop budget changes, because if the crop is too small, the recognition side, which the diagnostic shows is the fragile operation, starts to fail, and the savings buy nothing. Third, the evaluation is on ZoomBench-style fine-grained questions with a verifiable answer, which is what makes the region-level reward computable. In open-ended visual reasoning, where the answer is not a single checkable value, defining the per-region contribution to the answer is harder, and I would want to see whether the reward construction generalizes beyond the verifiable-answer setting.

One more point on the practical value. The method is designed to sit on top of an existing efficient predictor rather than replace it, which is the right integration choice: it keeps the single-pass cost of SD-RPN and only adds the reader's scoring during training, not at inference. That means the deployment cost is the same single pass, and the 4.2-times token reduction is a real inference-time saving, not a training-time artifact.

It is also worth noting what the method deliberately does not do. It does not change the multimodal model's reader, the part that answers from the crop. The reader is assumed to be capable; the paper's target is the routing that decides what the reader sees. That scoping is a strength and a limitation at the same time. It is a strength because it keeps the method modular, the same reader can be paired with a better predictor, and the training of the predictor does not have to relearn any of the reader's capabilities. It is a limitation because the 4.2-times token reduction is only as good as the reader's ability to work from a 128-token crop, and if the reader itself degrades on small crops, the routing savings are partly illusory, the model is looking at the right place but reading it badly. The diagnostic that motivates the method, the three-to-four-times asymmetry between localization and recognition compression, is the paper's answer to that concern, but it was measured on one model at one crop size, and the interaction between predictor quality and reader quality is something the method's design leaves to the deployment to manage.

Bottom line: Vision-RL2 is a well-motivated method for fine-grained multimodal perception, grounded in a clean diagnostic showing that localization tolerates three to four times more compression than recognition, and its headline result, matching SD-RPN accuracy with 4.2 times fewer visual tokens, comes from optimizing the region proposal network with answer-level reinforcement through a discrete region choice that standard gradients cannot reach. The main open question is how reliable the reader model's per-region reward is, because that reward is the entire training signal for the predictor.
