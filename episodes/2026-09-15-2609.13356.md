Paper three is ZGCM-1: A Fully Open and Extremely Efficient Foundation Model for Math and Agentic Search, from Jiyan He, Guang Liang, and Hao Liu, out of the Zhongguancun Academy and the Zhongguancun Institute of Artificial Intelligence. This is a fully open foundation-model report in the strongest sense of the word: a 7.4-billion-parameter dense model trained from scratch, with weights released from every stage of training, the training code, the per-stage data mixes, and the W and B logs. The thesis of the paper is that a compact model does not need to memorize the open web to be a strong reasoner, because deliberate internal thinking plus active external tool use can substitute for the parametric capacity that a hundred-billion-parameter model would use for storage.

The architecture is a decoder-only transformer with grouped-query attention, root-mean-square normalization, SwiGLU activations, and rotary position embeddings, 32 layers, a hidden dimension of 4,096, 32 query heads and 8 key-value heads, head dimension 128, and a maximum context of 256,000 tokens. The interesting part is the hybrid attention schedule. 27 of the 32 layers use a gated sliding-window attention with a 128-token window, and 5 layers use full causal attention over the whole sequence, in a 5-to-1 local-to-global ratio, the same ratio that Gemma 3 deployed at scale. The gating is a learned sigmoid applied element-wise to the local attention output before the output projection, so each layer can decide how much of its local attention to pass through. They benchmarked this against full attention, FlashAttention-2 with grouped query attention, and a multi-head latent attention baseline at a matched budget of 10 billion tokens on 8 H100s. The 5-to-1 sliding window won on throughput at 9,566 tokens per second per GPU with a tail loss of 1.93, only a hundredth of a point behind the best loss, and at 256,000 context it runs at 3.94 times the throughput of full attention. The practical consequence for serving is the KV cache: at 256,000 tokens a full-attention 7B model needs 32 gigabytes of KV cache, this hybrid needs 5, a 6.4 times reduction, because 27 layers only ever store 128 tokens. They also run an iso-parameter comparison against a linear-attention baseline, 7 global layers plus 21 Gated Delta Net layers, which lands at 7 gigabytes at the same context, so the sliding-window hybrid is about 1.4 times smaller in cache than the linear variant while staying in the familiar causal-transformer family. That matters for long-reasoning and agentic workloads specifically, because autoregressive decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound, and a smaller KV footprint directly buys decode throughput when the context is long.

The systems side is where the "extremely efficient" claim comes from. Matrix parameters are optimized with Muon, momentum 0.9, five Newton-Schulz steps, a constant learning rate of 2 times 10 to the minus 4, weight decay 0.1; scalar parameters go through a plain Adam. The matrix multiplies run in Transformer Engine hybrid FP8, E4M3 forward, E5M2 backward, with delayed scaling over a history of 1,024 steps, and everything else stays in BFloat16 or float32. They add an activation regularizer, called TWEO, that suppresses intermediate activation outliers, and they report this as the thing that makes FP8 stable at this scale. Sustained throughput was about 585 model TFLOP per second per GPU, which is roughly 60 percent of the BFloat16 dense peak of the H100. They decompose the total efficiency gain over a BFloat16 AdamW baseline into four factors: 1.4 times from the sliding-window attention, 1.5 times from the FP8 systems stack, 1.8 times from Muon, and 1.1 times from pre-layer normalization, which multiply out to the 4.2 times pre-training time-to-loss speedup they claim at 16,000 context.

Training went in two phases. General pre-training on 4.19 trillion tokens total, 0.99 trillion in stage one and 3.2 trillion in stage two, with the stage-one data ordered from low to high lexical complexity for the general-language portion only, because they found complexity ordering is a cheap signal for web text but a bad proxy for the difficulty of code and math. The stage-two mix is 54 percent web, 23.6 percent code, 10.8 percent academic OCR, 9 percent math, 2 percent LaTeX papers, and a controlled 0.6 percent of specialized reasoning data taken from Nemotron's pretraining release. The mixture itself was tuned with a 0.3-billion proxy model trained on 30 billion tokens, the standard proxy-model search practice, and the tokenizer is the GLM-5.1 one, which means the vocabulary is not a custom asset. Then mid-training, 600 billion tokens from a 2.86 trillion pool, with context expanded progressively from 16,000 to 64,000 to 256,000, and interaction traces reformulated as Markov decision process state-action transitions, which is the paper's way of saying the agent data is trained with the same next-token objective as everything else. Code and math stay near 20 percent of the mixture throughout; the agentic slice grows from 1.5 to 3.3 percent as context grows. Post-training is a mixed think-and-no-think supervised fine-tuning on execution-verified trajectories, which is how they get a model that can both do long deliberation and answer directly when the task is short. One detail in the context schedule worth keeping: the later stages stay cumulative, so the 256,000 stage still replays 16,000 and 64,000 length data, with 127.8 billion of its tokens at 16,000 or under, 21.7 billion between 16,000 and 64,000, and only 31 billion above 64,000. That is the same multi-stage expansion philosophy as Qwen2.5's million-token context work, rather than the single-final-stage extension that most other open reports use, and the paper credits the schedule with the long-context generalization result.

Results: 75.0 percent on AIME 2026, 97.1 on MATH-500, 70.4 on HMMT 2025, and across 14 reasoning benchmarks they report the best average rank among 7 to 8 billion parameter models, with the comparison set including Qwen3-235B-A22B and GLM-5.1 in the figure, though those are not 7B-scale. On the agentic side, 63.1 on WebWalkerQA and 62.0 on a binary function search benchmark, where the task is to recover a function from a stripped binary, and 19.4 on BrowseComp, a number they compare against frontier models an order of magnitude larger, and 19.4 is the honest gap: deep browsing remains well out of reach at this scale. The paper distills eight findings, and the two I would act on are the SFT quality-pruning result, that a smaller curated fine-tuning set beat a larger low-quality one, which is consistent with every other lab's experience, and the long-context result, that the model generalizes to 256,000 tokens with the multi-stage expansion schedule rather than a single final extension. They also report that agent swarms running the research-and-development workflow, cluster ops, data curation, diagnostics, got higher autonomy ratings from the nine core contributors on experimentation and monitoring than on architecture and learning-algorithm design.

My skeptical read: the 4.2 times efficiency number is a product of four individually measured factors, not one controlled end-to-end run, so the multiplication is an assumption; the agentic benchmark set is small and self-assembled in places; and "fully open" is a strong claim that rests on the data mixes actually being released, which the Hugging Face data card promises but you should verify before you build on it. The genuinely reusable pieces are the 5-to-1 sliding-window-plus-gate schedule, the FP8-plus-TWEO stability recipe, and the MDP reformulation of interaction traces for mid-training. Bottom line: ZGCM-1 is the most complete open small-model recipe published this year, the efficiency claims are plausible but partially extrapolated, and the math results at 7 billion parameters are the real headline of the day.
