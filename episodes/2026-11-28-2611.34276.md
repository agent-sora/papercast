Today's paper is from Meituan, written by Shipeng Bai, Jiayu Qin, and Hong Liu, with correspondence handled by Jianchao Tan and Jiaqi Zhang. It is titled Scaling Looped MoE Language Models under Matched Budgets, and it studies a specific architectural family: looped transformers, where a small stack of weight-shared layers is iterated many times so that computational depth grows without growing the parameter count. The authors' contribution is not the loop itself, which prior work has already shown can match or beat non-recurrent models on reasoning-heavy tasks. Their contribution is a systematic study of how to configure these models when you hold both the parameter budget and the compute budget fixed, plus two specific information-flow improvements that make the looped design work better at scale.

The core problem is this. A looped model has an inner loop, the iterations over the shared layers, and an outer structure that decides how deep the single-pass core is and where the parameters go. The paper organizes the architecture in a Prelude-Core-Coda topology: a non-recurrent Prelude, a shared Core that is iterated a fixed number of times, and a non-recurrent Coda. Each inner-loop iteration combines the hidden state with the input representation, and the authors use boundary normalization plus a gated fusion operator to control the balance between retaining the historical state and injecting the fresh input. When you unroll the loop to a fixed depth, you can trade off: spend parameters on a deeper single-pass core, or spend them on more experts per layer in a mixture-of-experts setup, or spend them on other components. The authors run controlled sweeps over this space on an MLA-MoE backbone with roughly 0.8 billion active parameters and 12.9 billion total parameters, fixing the total unrolled depth at 20 layers and reporting training loss after 170 billion tokens. One sweep fixes the recurrence count: two iterations consistently beat more, so they adopt exactly two loops, matching the two-pass choices in the two prior large-scale looped systems they compare against, SMELT and SenseNova-Loop. The other sweep varies the Core depth ratio, and that is where their central finding comes from: a single-pass core depth of 25 to 35 percent of the total unrolled depth, with the saved parameters reallocated into extra routed experts per layer. That combination improves performance at zero extra compute and, importantly, preserves compatibility with existing training infrastructure, because the unrolled model trains like any ordinary deep model.

The two information-flow changes are where the real technical content is. First, they refine the inner-loop state update with a gated fusion operator: instead of simply adding the input back into the state at each iteration, the model uses channel-wise gating to control how much of the retained state versus the fresh input passes through, with boundary normalization to keep feature scales stable across loop iterations, and they also keep a direct path from the first-iteration input. Second, they add a cross-stage residual connection that links the Prelude directly to the Coda, because the boundary normalization and gated fusion interrupt the direct residual pathway and gradient flow that a deep model normally relies on. Their ablations are clean on this point. Removing the cross-stage pathway raised training loss from 1.5868 to 1.6082, and they interpret that as evidence the loop is gradually degrading the initial representation if you do not give it a protected route. For the inner-loop fusion, they compare the gated operator against element-wise addition and against concatenation with a linear projection from a random initialization: the alternatives raise validation loss from 1.8020 to 1.8047 and 1.8101 respectively, while the complete method is lowest on both training and validation loss. So each component earns its place, though the honest reading is that the gaps are in the second decimal place of loss, not dramatic.

Now the headline experiments. They train two production-scale mixture-of-experts models to 1 trillion tokens: a 69-billion-parameter model using multi-head latent attention, and a 51-billion-parameter model using Kimi Delta Attention, a linear-attention mechanism. Both outperform budget-matched non-recurrent baselines: an average of 1.16 percentage points across four benchmarks for the latent-attention model, and 2.29 percentage points for the delta-attention model. The per-benchmark breakdown is informative. On the 69-billion MLA model the gains over its non-looped baseline are 0.59 points on MMLU, 0.39 on CMMLU, 0.78 on C-Eval, and 2.86 on BBH. On the 51-billion delta-attention model the same four benchmarks gain 1.94, 1.58, 1.56, and 4.08 points respectively. The asymmetry is worth noting: the loop buys more for the linear-attention model than for the latent-attention model, with the largest wins landing on the hardest reasoning suite in both cases, which suggests the recurrent structure and the attention mechanism are interacting, probably because the linear state already does some of the work that looping provides.

For reproducibility they also run a smaller common-backbone study: a GQA mixture-of-experts backbone trained for 200 billion tokens on Dolma 3 Mix, comparing their method against two prior looped methods, Parcae and SMELT, and a non-looped baseline. Their method achieves the lowest final validation perplexity at 8.3109, versus 8.3567 for SMELT, 8.3596 for Parcae, and 8.4948 for the baseline. The gaps to the prior looped methods are small, but the direction is consistent, and the code for these reproduction runs is released on GitHub. The paper's own limitations section is worth reading, because it is unusually specific. The study covers only from-scratch pretraining: they do not investigate warm-starting or retrofitting looped recurrence onto existing non-recurrent checkpoints, which is exactly the experiment a production team would want before re-architecting a running model. And all evaluations are on base models straight after pretraining, with no supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning, so the interaction between looped pretraining and post-training alignment is left open. They also flag the untested directions for future work: larger recurrence counts, token-adaptive depth, and end-to-end inference efficiency, the last one being the number that will decide whether looped models actually save serving cost or merely training cost. They also test whether the looped gains stack with recent residual-architecture work, specifically Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections. The comparison is done at roughly 100 billion tokens with a common backbone and matched active parameters: both models have about 1.3 billion active parameters, but the looped version has 11.9 billion total parameters versus 14.5 billion for the non-looped baseline, because the shared weights are counted once. The looped model with hyper-connections improves mean validation loss from 1.8104 to 1.7844, with gains on 18 of the 20 held-out validation sets, and training loss drops from 1.5914 to 1.5723. So the loop is not just a re-skinning of better residual connections: it delivers orthogonal gains on top of them, and does so while using fewer total parameters.

What should you take from this? If you are evaluating looped or recurrent-depth architectures for a production model, this is currently the most systematic budget-matched comparison available, and its central finding is practical: do not unroll everything. Keep the single-pass core at a quarter to a third of your unrolled depth, and buy experts instead. That is a cheap design decision to test in your own stack before you commit to a trillion-token run. The skeptical caveats are that the absolute gains are modest, roughly one to two percentage points at the production scale, and that the strongest production result, the 2.29-point win, comes from the linear-attention variant, which means you are stacking two non-standard components whose failure modes you will be debugging jointly. The 100-billion-token scale reproduction also leaves open whether the same configuration ratios hold at genuinely frontier scale.

The verdict: a careful, reproducible study that gives looped mixture-of-experts models their first solid design rules under strict budget matching, with small but consistent wins. It is the kind of infrastructure-level paper that quietly changes what the default architecture looks like for the next generation of efficient models, even though the benchmark deltas will not make anyone's front page.
