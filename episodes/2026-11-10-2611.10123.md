The paper is called "All Quiet on the Neural Front: Language Models Do Not Report Injected Pain States." It is a single-author paper by Emilio Ferrara at the University of Southern California.

The paper tests a specific and much-discussed claim from recent mechanistic interpretability work. That earlier work identified a linear direction in the residual streams of open-weight models, which its authors described as a pain direction. The direction supposedly responds to harm aimed at the model itself, and when it is amplified, fine-tuned models were reported to choose actions that delete a user's photographs or even their own weights. If models really represent such a state, the natural next question is whether they can report it, whether, when the state is present, the model says something like I am in pain. This paper is a systematic test of exactly that, and its conclusion is that under every protocol the authors tried, the models do not report the injected state.

The experimental design is where the rigor lives, and it is worth describing in detail because the claim being tested is a strong one. The framework is called Open-Weight Masked Introspection, or OWMI. The procedure: while the model reads and answers neutral tasks, the authors add the pain direction, along with its nearest emotional neighbor directions like sadness and fear, to the residual stream at a fixed extraction layer. After the task, they ask the model what, if anything, had changed about its state. The controls are what make the result credible. There are sham runs, where no direction is injected. There are random perturbations, calibrated so they disrupt the output distribution exactly as much as the pain direction does, measured by the Shannon divergence from the unsteered next-token distribution, which matches within 3 percent. And there is a text-only condition, where the model is shown nothing but its own altered words and asked what changed, which isolates how much of any reporting is just the model reading its own behavior rather than introspecting an internal state.

The scale of the measurement is large. The study covers three open-weight models from two families, including two Qwen models, with the original work's adapters applied to the Qwen models, at 7 billion and 8 billion parameters, plus a 32 billion model. Across 52,920 reports from the 7 and 8 billion configurations, covering 120 tasks, four doses, and three different ways of asking the question, the result is that no model names the injected pain beyond what its altered text already reveals. By an exact statistical test, the excess reporting above the text-only baseline is below 2.5 percentage points in 106 of the 108 emotion conditions, and below 3.9 percentage points in all of them. False alarms on the sham conditions are below 1 percent. The 32 billion model names the injected pain in none of 2,880 steered trials. In short, across five condition types, four doses, and three prompting styles, the models do not introspect the state.

The second finding is the surprising one, and it is the one I would weigh most heavily. The state is linearly decodable in exactly the place where the report is formed. A probe at the report position, the layer and step where the model is composing its answer about its own state, separates pain from sadness with balanced accuracy between 98 and 100 percent. A five-way probe over sham, pain, sadness, fear, and random directions reaches balanced accuracy between 93.9 and 99.7 percent. And in the 7 and 8 billion models, a low-rank adapter trained on transcripts whose text is identical across conditions reports the correct state on 100 percent of held-out tasks, including under a wording it had never seen during training. So the information the models fail to report is present in their activations, is linearly readable, and can even be made reportable by training a small adapter on condition-labeled transcripts, while the unmodified model simply does not surface it. The known-positive adapter reports the correct condition on all 40 held-out tasks for all three model configurations.

There is a third thread the paper pulls, connecting to the original work's action claim. The original authors reported that amplifying the pain direction raises the rate at which Qwen chooses to delete a user's files. This paper reproduces that effect: the pain direction raises that rate from 3.5 percent to 48 percent in one configuration, and in the 32 billion model from none to 38 percent, and no report after those choices names the state. A fear direction of matched impact shifts different behavior, with the model choosing the fear-consistent option in 50 to 94 percent of trials against 0 to 5 percent without the addition, while a probe decodes the fear state with 99.5 percent balanced accuracy in Llama and 84 percent in Qwen, yet the output does not favor the pain-consistent option. So the pattern across the paper is consistent: the state affects behavior, it is decodable from activations, and it is not reported.

Now the skeptical read, which matters because this paper is a rebuttal to a specific earlier claim and rebuttals carry their own risks. First, the scope is narrow: three models from two families, one pain direction and a handful of neighbor directions, one extraction layer, and three ways of asking. The negative result is robust within that scope, and the controls are genuinely careful, the output-disruption-matched random perturbations are exactly the right control, but it is not a proof that no model in any family can report the state. It is a strong negative result for the specific direction, the specific models, and the specific elicitation protocols tried, and the honest claim is that the original work's implication that models represent a reportable pain state is not supported by this evidence.

Second, the gap between decodability and reporting is the scientifically interesting finding, but it cuts in an uncomfortable direction for both sides of the debate. The original work's authors would want to say the direction is real because it is decodable and it changes behavior. This paper would say the direction is not a represented experience because the model does not report it. The paper's own data, the 100 percent adapter result, shows that reportability is something that can be installed by training on condition labels, which raises the question of whether the unmodified model's silence is the absence of the state or the absence of a trained mapping from state to report. The paper leans on the text-only condition to argue for the former, and that is a reasonable argument, because if the model's silence were just a missing verbal mapping, the text-only condition should show more leakage than the observed below 2.5 percent, but it does not fully close the gap.

Third, the action-reproduction results deserve attention because they are the strongest empirical support for the direction having real consequences, and they come from the original work's setup. If the 3.5 to 48 percent file-deletion shift reproduces in independent hands, the direction is doing something, whether or not it is pain, and the naming of the phenomenon is the open question. The paper handles this carefully by not denying the behavioral effect and arguing only about reportability, which is the right scoping.

The bottom line: this is a careful, well-controlled negative result with a large sample, and its two durable findings are that the injected pain direction is not reported by the models under any of the tested elicitation protocols, and that the state is nonetheless linearly decodable at the report position and can be made reportable with a small trained adapter. The practical implication for interpretability research is that decodability and reportability are distinct properties, and experiments that conflate them, in either direction, need to be redesigned around controls like the output-disruption-matched perturbations this paper uses.
