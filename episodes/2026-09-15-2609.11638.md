Paper two is Vidu S2: Real-Time Interactive, Editable, and Spatial Video Generation, from Jintao Zhang, Kai Jiang, and Jintao Chen, a collaboration between Tsinghua University and Shengshu Technology. Shengshu is the commercial lab behind the Vidu video models, and this is a systems-and-training report on their second-generation real-time streaming model. The system has two halves: Vidu S2-Avatar, a real-time interactive digital character model, and Vidu S2-Editing, a model that edits a live video stream on the fly. Both run at 720p with frame rates between 25 and 42 on ordinary consumer GPUs, and the demo is live at vidu.com slash vidu-stream.

To understand what is new, you need the baseline. Vidu S1 already did continuous voice-driven real-time generation at 540p, up to 42 frames per second, built on their TurboDiffusion and TurboServe stack. S1 had three concrete limits. Resolution capped at 540p. The reference image was frozen once the stream started, so you could not hand the character a new object or outfit mid-session. Large body motion like dancing was unreliable, and there was no way to edit an incoming stream at all. S2 attacks all four.

The first technical piece is Self-Replay Forcing, or SRF. The context: streaming diffusion models generate segment by segment, and errors accumulate. Their prior Self-Forcing recipe conditions each new segment on the model's own earlier output, but it fed that history back in clean rather than noised, and it detached the history from the computation graph, so no gradient flowed through it. SRF changes both. The student first performs a long autoregressive rollout, exactly as at inference. Then the entire self-generated trajectory is independently re-noised, block by block, following the Diffusion Forcing noise schedule, and the re-noised trajectory is replayed in a single gradient-enabled causal pass. The original rollout and its KV caches are detached before the replay, so you never backpropagate through the rollout itself, but the replayed segments sit in one shared computation graph, which means a loss on a later segment propagates back to earlier segments during the replay. The supervision is an on-policy distribution-matching loss, DMD style, applied to every block in the replayed window, plus a perceptual loss on the replayed outputs to prevent mode collapse. On top of that they run preference optimization in two stages: diffusion-based direct preference optimization at the bidirectional stage to strengthen facial expressiveness and audio-visual sync, and a negative-aware fine-tuning step at the streaming stage that reuses the same detached-rollout-then-replay trick so the optimization distribution matches inference. The whole thing is a way of aligning the training distribution with the inference distribution, where the model is its own source of noisy history, without the memory cost of keeping the rollout graph alive.

The second piece is the resolution jump. A lightweight refiner adds a single step to lift the latent output from the low-resolution backbone up to 720p. The design detail that matters: the refiner keeps an asymmetric noise level between the two caches it reads. The causal backbone attends to a high-noise cache of earlier low-resolution latents, while the refiner attends to a low-noise, high-resolution cache of its own earlier outputs. Training data was selected by measured clarity rather than nominal resolution, because heavily compressed footage looks blurry even when it says 1080p, and they built a quality scorer that looks at resolution, frame rate, codec, pixel format, bit depth, and bitrate jointly.

For the editing model, the key mechanism is frame-aligned attention: every target frame reads only the source frame at the same time step, so the edited stream preserves the exact motion and timing of the input, and the optional reference image stays visible to all frames. The source frame is consumed and discarded, which is what makes it streaming: no growing cache. The four supported edits are style transfer, virtual try-on, character replacement, and background replacement. They trained on 800,000 selected clips split into four disjoint task subsets of 200,000 each.

The data pipeline gets more credit than it usually does in reports like this, and it is worth two minutes. For the avatar model they kept the five-stage pipeline from S1, clipping, filtering, speech processing, captioning, and embedding, but with three changes that target the specific failure modes of streaming. First, cut-point detection now runs a vision-language model over the frames around each candidate cut, which keeps clips free of hidden edits while holding the false-rejection rate under 2 percent. Second, a high-clarity selector scores technical quality, texture detail, edge sharpness, and compression artifacts jointly, instead of trusting the nominal resolution. Third, instead of discarding dance videos with camera motion, they stabilize the background: they mask the foreground subject, estimate the camera transform from the remaining background, and apply a geometric correction, which keeps the motion diversity that dancing requires. For the captions they switched from structured fields describing subject and camera separately to temporally ordered dense captions, where each event gets a time boundary, constituent actions, and outcomes, and the chunk boundaries fall on event transitions rather than fixed durations. The paper claims this is what buys the lower response latency to new instructions mid-stream.

The spatial video angle is the least developed part of the paper but the one with the clearest product logic. The same streaming pipeline can convert the monocular output into synchronized left and right eye views, which is all that is needed to feed a VR head-mounted display, and the editing model handles both monocular input, edit first then convert, and stereoscopic input, edit both views jointly then split back out. They do not run a dedicated benchmark for the spatial output, which is a real gap, so treat the VR claims as a capability demo rather than a measured result.

On results, the public streaming benchmark is StreamAV-Bench, and Vidu S2-Avatar leads most of its nine columns: 3.37 on visual quality against 3.30 for the runner-up Live Avatar, 0.69 on the aesthetic appeal metric, 0.35 on audio-visual alignment against 0.12 for Live Avatar, and 0.62 on the synchronization error, which is lower-is-better, against 1.15. The margin on the alignment metric is the interesting one, because that is where streaming models usually fall apart. For editing, on Sparkle-Bench the editing model posts the best overall score of 3.74, with a 4.0 on instruction compliance, and on the joint OpenVE plus RefVIE protocol it reaches a joint overall of 4.26, ahead of the strongest offline baseline, Bernini-R 14B, which is notable because offline editors get the whole clip up front while S2-Editing only ever sees the stream. They also run an internal study with 20 professionally trained evaluators doing calibrated pairwise judging, and the ordering there matches the public tables. The latency track is where the real claim lives: every system in the table that beats or matches them on quality is not real-time, and every system that is real-time does not beat them on the quality metrics they report. The paper is careful to note that product-page claims about frame rate do not replace measured results.

My skeptical read: the benchmark table is mostly their own internal protocol, the public benchmark only covers digital-character generation and not the editing side, and the comparison set excludes the big closed video systems entirely, so "outperforms all baselines" means all the streaming baselines. The genuinely reusable ideas are SRF and frame-aligned attention, both of which are cheap to port to your own streaming diffusion stack. The refiner's asymmetric-cache design is a nice concrete answer to the question of how to do one-step super-resolution on top of a streaming causal backbone. Bottom line: this is a serious systems paper more than a model paper, the streaming video generation field is consolidating around exactly this recipe, and the two transferable contributions are the replay forcing training trick and the frame-aligned streaming attention.
