WebHarbor, Docking Real Websites for Evolving GUI Agent Environments, from UNC Chapel Hill and Microsoft, a large collaboration with fifty-seven listed authors, the first three being Zhaoyang Wang, Qianhui Wu, and Boyu Gou. This is an environments paper, and the problem it solves is the one every web-agent researcher hits eventually: you cannot reliably evaluate or train on live websites. Live sites change their content, they run bot detection, they impose regional restrictions, and they rarely expose the authenticated workflows that most real user requests live behind. The paper quantifies the pain from its own experiments: about thirty-eight percent of Online-Mind2Web tasks were blocked by bot detection, and twenty-two percent of WebVoyager tasks were outdated because the underlying websites had changed. That is a lot of uncontrolled variation leaking into your evaluation, and it prevents agents from ever learning the workflows hidden behind logins.

The answer is to dock the websites. WebHarbor is a suite of one hundred fifty-three executable, dockerized website environments that reconstruct the visual interfaces, content, and functional workflows of real web services under deterministic control. They are not screenshots and not mock DOMs, they are running environments with state, with functional workflows you can actually drive, and they can be reset deterministically, which is what makes them usable for reinforcement learning. The construction method is the interesting part, and it is a human-agent collaborative pipeline. Task-driven docking works like this: a new task proposes the functional scope a website environment needs, coding agents implement the changes to extend the environment, and human contributors audit the result for visual fidelity, functional correctness, and task validity. The environments evolve as the task suite expands. That is a community-driven construction loop, and it is the mechanism that lets the suite keep growing without a fixed team of environment engineers.

The resulting suite has three thousand five hundred eighteen tasks spanning multi-step and post-authentication workflows, which is exactly the coverage that live-web benchmarks lack. The task mix is worth noting: four hundred forty-eight transaction and booking tasks, thirty-one point eight percent of the suite requiring authentication, thirty-five point six percent modifying persistent state, and fifty-three point seven percent involving cross-page reasoning. The diversity is measured, not just claimed: the nearest-neighbor cosine similarity of task semantics is zero point five eight within WebHarbor, lower than the zero point six six to zero point seven six range of the existing suites, meaning the tasks are semantically less redundant, and the least-similar half of the suite covers about ninety-one percent of the one hundred fifty-three sites. The environments themselves cover the everyday workflow classes the paper lists, information retrieval, account management, bookings, purchases, and saved items, each reconstructed with enough functional depth that a wrong click has a real downstream effect on state rather than just a rendered page.

Now the evaluation claim. On consistency, four agents score between four point four and eighteen point eight points higher on WebHarbor than on live WebVoyager, reflecting reduced interference from content drift and access restrictions, and all six pairwise agent rankings are preserved between the two benchmarks, with a Spearman rank correlation of one point zero and a Pearson score correlation of zero point eight. That is a strong reproducibility result: the offline suite reproduces the live benchmark's ordering of agents almost exactly while being stable.

The training result is the second half, and it is a controlled comparison by construction. They take OpenWebRL-4B as the baseline, the current state-of-the-art open-source agent trained through online RL on two thousand one hundred two live-web tasks across seven hundred four live websites. Their mirror policy, WebHarbor-4B, starts from the same SFT checkpoint, uses the same multimodal multi-turn GRPO objective and the same ReAct-style GUI harness, and trains only in the offline WebHarbor environments. The single variable is the environment. On the live benchmarks, WebHarbor-4B scores eighty-one point two on WebVoyager and seventy point four on Online-Mind2Web, an average of seventy-five point eight, against seventy point six for the live-trained OpenWebRL-4B. Both differences are significant under paired tests at p less than zero point zero one, and WebHarbor-4B is the strongest open-source agent on both live benchmarks. The gains concentrate exactly where the training data lived: plus thirteen point three on authentication, plus twelve point six on multi-page navigation, plus ten point eight on stateful operations in WebHarbor, and plus twenty-one point four on transactions on WebArena. An agent trained purely on synthetic environments outperforms one trained on live websites, on live-website evaluation.

The generalization protocol is worth a sentence because it preempts the obvious objection that the offline agent is just memorizing its own training sites. They evaluate on four external benchmarks: Online-Mind2Web with three hundred tasks across one hundred and more live websites, WebVoyager with six hundred forty-three tasks, a two hundred twenty-six task subset of WebArena with its programmatic state validators, and WebArena-NL with automatic login disabled so the agent must authenticate from credentials in the task description. The protocol follows OpenWebRL: a one thousand two hundred eighty by one thousand viewport, temperature zero point six decoding, a fresh browser context per task, and a thirty-step budget except fifty for WebArena. The result is complementary generalization: the offline-trained agent does not merely reproduce the training distribution, it holds its own against leading open-source GUI agents on benchmarks it has never seen.

The skeptical read. The comparison is tightly controlled, which is the paper's strength and also its boundary: same checkpoint, same objective, same harness, one model family at four billion parameters. The generalization claim is that RL in high-fidelity synthetic environments can match or beat RL on live websites, but it is demonstrated for one policy class at one scale, and the external benchmarks still include some tasks that are structurally close to the training distribution. The construction loop depends on human auditing at every step, which bounds how fast the suite can grow and means the fidelity is only as good as the auditors, and a large contributor base helps throughput but introduces consistency questions across environments. And the deterministic sandboxes, while ideal for evaluation, can encode the auditors' assumptions about how a real site behaves, so an agent that learns to exploit a sandbox quirk is not learning a portable skill. The latencies are good, though: mean initialization seven point four three seconds, reset six point six eight, verifier four point zero four, which is fast enough for large-scale rollout.

If you want to use this. The code and data are public. The immediate win is evaluation: swap your flaky live-web benchmark for the docked suite and your scores become reproducible run to run, and the ranking consistency result says you are not distorting the relative comparison. The second win is training: if you are doing RL for a GUI web agent and live rollouts are your bottleneck, this is evidence that a high-fidelity offline environment trained with the same objective can beat live training. The thing to verify in your own domain is fidelity transfer: the construction pipeline is domain-agnostic in design, so the question is whether you can get the human-audited fidelity high enough in your target application space for the same match-or-beat result to hold.

The verdict. WebHarbor is a well-executed answer to the reproducibility and training-environment problems of web-agent research, with one hundred fifty-three dockerized sites, three thousand five hundred eighteen tasks, and a Spearman-one ranking consistency against the live benchmark. The mirror-trained agent beating the live-trained baseline on live evaluation is the standout result, though it is demonstrated at one model scale, and the human-audited construction loop is the part that will limit how far the approach scales. The practical takeaway for anyone building a web agent is that the environment is now a first-class artifact worth engineering deliberately, not an afterthought bolted on at evaluation time.
