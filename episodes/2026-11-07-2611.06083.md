SleuthVLM, Do We Really Need Audio-in-Video for Omni Video Understanding, from the University of Bristol, Ant Group, and Tsinghua University. The first three authors are Peiran Wu, Fangfei Gou, and Zhaoxi Mu. The question the paper asks is a direct one, and it is aimed at a lot of current omni-model effort. The mainstream way to build a model that answers questions about video is to extend a vision-language model with an audio encoder and train it on large amounts of paired audio-visual data, so that it processes the sampled frames and the audio track together in a single pass. The paper asks whether that single pass, and specifically the direct audio input, is actually necessary. The answer it gives is no, and it demonstrates the claim with an agent harness built on a vision-language model that never hears the audio at all.

The idea. Instead of feeding the raw audio track to the model, the audio is transcribed once, with word-level timestamps, into text. The vision-language model is then given that timestamped transcript as an index it can search, along with a small set of tools to search the video and the transcript. The harness specifies only the accessible resources, a brief agentic guide, and a budget. The model decides where to look, how closely to inspect a moment, and when to stop, using the transcript to locate the relevant moments and then pulling the corresponding frames to look at them closely. There is no predefined workflow. The model is not told to transcribe, then summarize, then answer, in that order. It is an open-ended agent that plans its own inspection of the video. The transcript is doing the work that the audio encoder would do, except as searchable text that the model can reason over, re-query, and cross-reference with the visual frames, rather than as a fixed signal it can only attend to once.

The results are the part that makes the claim hard to dismiss. Across four omni benchmarks, with videos up to ninety minutes, SleuthVLM achieves the best average accuracy at seventy-five point zero percent, two point six points above the strongest closed-source omni model, which is Gemini 3.8 Flash. It also leads the Video-MME-v2 leaderboard at seventy-seven point eight percent, six point eight points above Gemini 3.8 Flash. With an open-source twenty-seven billion vision-language model running on a single GPU, the harness outperforms every open-source omni model by thirteen point eight points. So a model that does not hear the audio, driven by an agentic loop over a transcript and the frames, beats models that do hear the audio directly, including closed-source omni models that are substantially larger.

The paper draws three findings from the experiments. First, searching outperforms passive observation. Moving from a single pass over fixed frame samples to agentic inference, where the model decides where to look, improves accuracy by up to ten point three points, and the simple harness outperforms agents that are equipped with specialized tools. That last point is worth pausing on, because it suggests the win is not from a clever toolset, it is from the agentic loop itself, the ability to go back, re-inspect, and follow a thread, which a fixed-input model cannot do. Second, agentic ability matters more than direct audio access. Even when the omni models are given access to the audio inside the same harness, they perform below SleuthVLM with a strong vision-language model that relies on transcripts. So the bottleneck is not whether the model can hear, it is whether the model can act on what it has access to. Third, small agentic models are highly competitive. The open twenty-seven billion model on a single GPU matches the performance of the closed-source omni models, which is the practical takeaway, that you do not need a frontier omni model to do this, you need a strong vision-language model and an agentic harness.

The comparison set is also worth stating plainly, because it frames the claim. The closed-source omni models it is up against, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash, and Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus, all process sampled frames and the audio track jointly in a single turn, with progress driven largely by scaling model size and omni training data. The open-source omni models it beats, the Qwen-Omni family, MiniCPM-o, and the others in that class, follow the same recipe at smaller scale. The band of closed-source omni models spans roughly sixty-four to seventy-two point four average accuracy on the four benchmarks, and SleuthVLM sits above the whole band. So the comparison is not against a weak baseline, it is against the current generation of omni models on their home turf, the benchmarks they were trained and tuned for, which is exactly why a transcript-plus-agentic-loop system sitting above them is the interesting result rather than a benchmarking artifact.

The mechanism behind why this works is the revisit property. A single pass over a fixed set of sampled frames is the structural limitation of the omni-model approach. Once the frames are sampled and the audio is attended to in that one pass, the model cannot go back and look more closely at a moment that turns out to be relevant to the question, because it does not know the question is coming until it is asked, and by then the frames are already downsampled and gone. The agentic harness removes that constraint. The model sees the question first, uses the transcript to locate candidate moments, and then inspects exactly those frames at whatever resolution and duration it needs. For a question that hinges on a two-second detail in a ninety-minute video, that difference between fixed sampling and on-demand inspection is the entire game, and it is a difference that no amount of audio-encoder scaling addresses.

A skeptical read. The result is striking and the revisit argument is sound, but I would push on a few things before treating this as a replacement for omni models. The first is cost and latency. An agentic loop that searches a transcript, pulls frames, re-inspects, and iterates is going to use a lot more model calls and tokens than a single pass, and the paper reports accuracy but the wall-clock and compute cost per question is the number that determines whether this is practical for anything other than an offline benchmark. A model that takes ten times longer and ten times the tokens to beat Gemini by two and a half points on average is a different product than the single-pass model, and the paper should be read with that in mind. The second is the benchmark scope. Four omni benchmarks is a reasonable set, but it is the set where agentic inspection of timestamped content has an obvious advantage, and the gain over fixed sampling is likely to shrink on tasks where the relevant information is distributed and temporal, like understanding the overall tone of a scene or the rhythm of a performance, where a single dense pass is arguably the right tool. The third is the dependence on the transcript. The whole approach leans on the quality of the one-time transcription, and a transcription that drops a name, mishears a number, or gets the word-level timestamps slightly wrong becomes a hard ceiling that the agent cannot see past, because it is searching text that is already wrong. The omni model at least has the raw signal. For a deployment where the audio is clean and the questions are localizable, this harness is a strong and cheap starting point, and the finding that agentic ability beats direct audio access is the durable one, because it reframes omni understanding as a tool-use and inspection problem rather than a modality-fusion problem.

The bottom line. SleuthVLM shows that a vision-language model that never hears the audio, but is given a timestamped transcript and an agentic loop for on-demand frame inspection, matches or beats omni models that process audio directly, including closed-source ones. The takeaway is that for omni video understanding, the binding constraint is often the model's ability to act and revisit, not its access to the audio signal, and a simple agentic harness over a transcript is a competitive and cheap way to get there.
