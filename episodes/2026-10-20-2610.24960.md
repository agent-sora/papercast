The paper is CoworkEnv, a framework for synthesizing training environments for long-horizon cowork agents, from Zhongwei Xie, Yiming Du, and Jiaxin Bai, at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, and Hong Kong Baptist University. The target is a specific kind of agent: the AI coworker that resolves a customer-support request, which requires consulting account history, locating the relevant procedure, updating the right ticket, and respecting access restrictions, all across many turns of communication. The paper's claim is that the bottleneck in training compact models for this is not the architecture, it is the training experience, and that experience is hard to synthesize because a single plausible final answer does not prove the agent did the right intermediate actions. CoworkEnv is a capability-guided pipeline that generates executable environments where the request, the available evidence, the tool interfaces, and the grading checks all fit together, so that a rollout is only rewarded when the intended state changes actually happened and the prohibited ones did not.

The core difficulty is consistency. For a synthetic coworking task to be useful, the request, the service records, the tool behavior, and the grading must all agree. Records referring to the same customer have to match across different services. The tools have to expose exactly the information the task needs and implement the expected state changes. And the grader has to check the relevant actions and outcomes, not just the final text. A model can produce a perfectly reasonable-sounding reply and still have updated the wrong record or skipped a required step, and a grader that only reads the final message will reward that. So the paper builds environment construction and verification as first-class parts of the synthesis, not as an afterthought.

The pipeline is capability-guided. Workflow requirements drive task construction: the framework starts from the capabilities a coworker needs, multi-turn communication, information gathering, tool coordination, and decisions about when to act, and generates tasks that exercise them. Each generated task is then checked by programmatic validation with feedback repair. The specification, the generated service records, the tool implementations, and the grading behavior are all validated, and when a check fails, the validation loop feeds the error back and regenerates the offending piece. This is the step that keeps the request, the evidence, and the grader mutually consistent, and it is where most of the engineering lives.

From the validated environments, two kinds of training data fall out, and they answer different questions. Teacher executions, runs of a strong model, yield curated demonstrations for supervised fine-tuning, which teach the shape of a correct coworker trajectory. Student interactions, rollouts of the model being trained, provide the trajectories and rewards for reinforcement learning, which teach the model to optimize for the graded outcome under the actual constraints, something a single demonstration cannot express. The paper then trains compact models it calls PocketWorker, and the base for the main results is Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, an 8-billion-parameter vision-language model.

The supervised fine-tuning results are the cleaner of the two. On the Claw-Eval benchmark, which has 300 tasks scored from 0 to 100, SFT lifts the overall mean score from 46.6 to 64.1, a gain of 17.5 points. The gain is concentrated where the tasks are hardest. On multi-turn tasks, the SFT model scores 77.8 against 45.9 for the base, a 31.9 point improvement, and on general tasks it scores 60.9 against 46.7, a 14.2 point gain. The paper reports both pass at 3 and pass-3, the rate of reaching the threshold at least once versus consistently across all three trials, and on multi-turn the SFT model gets 78.9 and 50.0, versus 28.6 and 21.6 for the base. That gap between reaching it once and doing it consistently is itself informative: multi-turn tasks remain inconsistent even after fine-tuning.

The reinforcement learning comparison is the more interesting part, because it is a controlled study of training dynamics rather than a single number. Starting from a shared supervised warm-up checkpoint, the authors train four different RL methods for about three epochs: GRPO, Dr.GRPO, GDPO, and GiGPO, which differ in their reward processing, advantage estimation, and credit assignment. All four beat the base on the overall score, GRPO at 56.6 and GDPO at 57.2, but the spread on multi-turn is large. GDPO reaches 74.9 on multi-turn, a 29.0 point gain, the highest, while Dr.GRPO reaches only 67.1. GRPO, by contrast, does better than GDPO on the multimodal task category. So the ranking of RL methods depends on which capability the task demands, and no single configuration wins everywhere. That is a useful negative result for anyone choosing an RL recipe for long-horizon agents.

The paper also extends an SFT-only model to a second benchmark, ClawMark, to show the approach is not overfit to a single eval, and it includes ablations on the fraction of SFT data used, which show the gains are not an artifact of data volume alone.

The ClawMark results are worth a paragraph because they put the compact model against published frontier scores. ClawMark is a second coworker benchmark with 100 tasks, and on it the base Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct scores 11.8, while the SFT PocketWorker reaches 19.4, a gain of 7.6 points. For context, the frontier models on the same 100 tasks score GPT-5.4 at 56.7, Claude Sonnet 4.6 at 53.1, Qwen3.6 Plus at 48.7, and Gemini 3.1 Pro at 38.9. So the 8B model is still far below the frontier on this second benchmark, and the paper is honest about that. The more interesting comparison is on Claw-Eval, where the 8B multi-turn score of 77.8 sits within the range of the published frontier-model scores, including Nemotron 3 Super at 120 billion total parameters, Kimi K2.5 at a trillion, and Qwen3.5-397B at 397 billion, though the paper is careful to note that a claim of outperforming a specific reference requires aligned evaluation settings. There is also a 4-billion-parameter variant, where SFT raises the general score from 28.7 to 36.6 and multi-turn from 56.1 to 66.0, gains of 7.9 and 9.9 points, which shows the approach is not dependent on the 8B scale, but the gains are smaller than at 8B.

What I would trust here is the validation-with-repair step and the fact that the grader checks state changes rather than final text. That is the correct design for this class of task, and it is the part most synthetic-agent benchmarks get wrong. The multi-turn result is also real and large, and the RL comparison across four methods is the kind of thing that is useful to practitioners. What I would re-verify before adopting: the trained model is only 8 billion parameters, and the benchmarks, Claw-Eval and ClawMark, are the authors' own or closely affiliated, so the absolute scores are not independently reproducible in the way a public benchmark would be, and the SFT demonstrations come from a teacher model whose identity and strength the paper does not foreground. The RL results are preliminary by the authors' own framing, they say the improved training rewards provide preliminary evidence that the environments support policy optimization, which is an honest but weak claim, and the three-epoch budget is short. A skeptical read: this is a solid, well-motivated data-synthesis contribution with a correct grader design, and the message that multi-turn consistency is the real bottleneck is well supported. But it is a compact-model, in-house-benchmark result, and the transfer to a deployment with different tools and records is exactly the consistency problem the pipeline solves at synthesis time, which means you would need to re-run the whole validation loop for a new domain.
