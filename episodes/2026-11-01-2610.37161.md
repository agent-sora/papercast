WorkFactory: Turning Professional Intents into Executable Work for Scalable Agent Training, by Ziyang Zeng, Tingyang Chen, and Jia Deng, with the project led from IQuest Research and co-authors at Zhejiang University, Renmin University of China, the Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and Peking University, is a data-construction paper for training working agents, the kind that do professional digital work rather than answer questions. The claim is that supervised scaling for these agents is bottlenecked by task quality, not task quantity, and specifically by whether a task environment gives a teacher model enough information to do the work while withholding the work itself. The system builds 112,686 executable professional tasks spanning 612 occupations, collects teacher trajectories, and fine-tunes Qwen models at 4B, 27B, and 35B active-parameter scales, with the flagship model jumping from 879 to 1565 Elo on GDPval-AA version 2.1.

The problem, stated in the paper's own example, is the support boundary. A supplier-selection task might provide demand forecasts in one file and vendor quotations in another, but omit whether one vendor's price includes freight. Without that term, lifecycle costs cannot be computed consistently, and the teacher will either make up an unsupported assumption or leave the work incomplete. But handing the teacher the computed costs directly removes the substantive work the trajectory is supposed to demonstrate. So a good task environment must make all the premises available and leave all the derivations to the solver, and constructing that boundary across distributed, heterogeneous artifacts is what breaks most synthetic task pipelines. That is the gap WorkFactory targets: not generating more tasks, but generating tasks whose environments are coherent enough that a teacher's execution is actually a reliable demonstration.

The pipeline has four stages. First, intent synthesis: professional intents are derived from three sources, occupational hierarchies, documented skills, and real professional deliverables, with an LLM review for coherence, feasibility, and plausibility as real workplace activity. Second, graph-guided construction: for each intent, a planner works backward from the intended output and builds an information support graph. The graph's nodes are information units, each recording its content, its entity and scope, and whether it is supplied, externally acquired, solver-derived, or internal to construction. Edges record support relations, association, transformation, evidence, constraint, including cases where several sources jointly support one result. Location bindings then assign each unit to a carrier: the user request, a reference artifact, an external resource, a future deliverable, or internal construction requirements. Internal requirements stay hidden; anything the solver needs must be in or reachable from solver-visible inputs. The graph is hidden from the solver throughout. The planner translates the graph into a construction plan, and an environment worker reuses, adapts, or synthesizes artifacts, with programmatic and LLM checks against the plan and bounded retries before a task is discarded.

Third is the part that distinguishes this paper from the related work, execution-based repair. A probe solver attempts the realized task using only solver-visible inputs, with no access to the graph or the plan, and diagnoses environmental issues it grounds in inspected sources and tool observations. It distinguishes environment defects from solver mistakes and legitimate open choices, and only issues judged to block completion trigger repair. A refiner then edits the request and reference files, preserving file count, types, and dependency structure, and each revision must pass both the construction checks and a work-preservation review against the original intent. The paper's example repair adds a missing freight term of 15 cents per carton for one supplier, which restores the support needed for a net-present-value comparison without performing the comparison for the solver. Revised tasks that fail either check are discarded; those that pass get a fresh probe. The loop ends when no blocker remains or the budget is exhausted, and unresolved tasks are dropped.

Fourth, trajectory collection: an independent teacher, GLM-5.3, attempts each retained task in a clean sandbox with the same information boundary the probe had. The teacher is deliberately separate from the pipeline models, so the demonstrations do not inherit the construction stage's biases. Rollouts that pass lightweight filters for repeated reasoning, repeated tool calls, and missing deliverables are kept, with selective rejection sampling applied to the 5 percent of tasks with the longest rollouts, where the failure rate of long trajectories is highest. The final corpus averages 4.15 reference files per task, 57 assistant turns per trajectory, 63 tool calls, and 136,000 tokens per trajectory, with deliverables dominated by spreadsheets at 30.7 percent of tasks, PDFs at 28 percent, and documents at 22.9 percent. The corpus is screened against the evaluation tasks with request-overlap checks and file hashes, and the paper reports no evaluation-task reuse found, which matters because professional-work benchmarks and synthetic professional data share a lot of surface vocabulary.

The results. All three WorkPilot models beat their matched base models on four professional-work benchmarks: GDPval-AA, JobBench, Workspace-Bench, and OmegaUse-OfficeVal. WorkPilot-35B-A3B improves from 879 to 1565 Elo on GDPval-AA, and WorkPilot-27B reaches a locally estimated 1612 Elo, which the paper notes places it in the range of the frontier models it compares against. For context, the frontier models in the figure sit at roughly 1189 to 1588 on the same scale, so the 27B result is the more interesting number, with the explicit caveat that it is a local Bradley-Terry estimate calibrated to the official scale, not an official rating. The local Elo estimator is itself audited in the paper, with a mean absolute error of about 24 Elo against official ratings on the calibration set, which is a reasonable but not negligible uncertainty band on a 1612 claim. The scaling study shows model-dependent diminishing returns in performance as task scale grows, and execution length that first increases and then decreases with more data, which is the pattern you would expect if the extra data teaches the model to be more direct rather than more verbose.

Now the skeptical read. The primary comparisons are against matched base models, which is the right control for the data's effect, but it measures the value of this particular supervision, not the value of the construction method in isolation, and the paper's component analysis shows planning and repair each reduce probe-diagnosed defects and improve downstream scores, but those are internal ablations on the same pipeline. The 1612 Elo figure is a local estimate, and the 1565 for the flagship is likewise, with the paper reporting task-bootstrap confidence intervals rather than official ratings, so treat the frontier-comparison as directional. The teacher is a single frontier model, which means the student models inherit the teacher's style and failure modes, and the rejection-sampling screen only reaches the longest 5 percent of rollouts. The benchmarks are professional-work suites with LLM judges in places, and judge reliability is a real variable in these numbers. The genuinely useful artifacts are the released task subset and the model weights, and the information support graph plus probe-repair loop is a reusable recipe for anyone building task environments where the support boundary matters. The support boundary framing is the part that generalizes beyond this dataset: any time you construct a task whose solver must derive something rather than read it, you need a principled way to say which facts are premises and which are conclusions, and the graph, with its explicit supplied, acquired, derived, and internal unit types plus the probe that checks the boundary from the solver's own vantage point, is a clean way to do it. That is a more durable contribution than any single Elo number in the table.

My verdict is that this is a serious, well-controlled data-scaling paper for working agents, with the probe-repair loop as the methodological contribution and the 27B model approaching frontier GDPval scores as the empirical highlight, albeit via local Elo estimates. If you are training professional-work agents, the takeaway is that task environment coherence, enforced by an isolated probe and work-preserving repair, is a first-order lever on the quality of your teacher demonstrations, and the released data and weights make it testable.
