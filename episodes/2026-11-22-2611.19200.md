Title: LogT: Logically Think with Images for Visual Search
Authors: Yanjun Fu, Quanzeng You, Jiadong Guo
Labs: Meta Superintelligence Labs; University of Maryland
Arxiv: 2611.19200
Day: 2026-11-22
Upvotes: 14
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19200
---

LogT, short for logically think with images, by Yanjun Fu, Quanzeng You, and Jiadong Guo, at Meta Superintelligence Labs with the University of Maryland, is a fully automated data engine that improves how vision-language models use the zoom-in tool for fine-grained visual search. The paper's thesis is that the field has been chasing the wrong lever: instead of designing more complex reinforcement learning for tool use, the binding bottleneck is the quality of the supervised fine-tuning data. LogT generates an SFT dataset of challenging, visually grounded questions paired with structured tool-use traces that follow human-like logic. Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-VL-7B on this data produces a model that, per the paper, outperforms the state-of-the-art trained with both SFT and RL while using 3.1 times less data and more than 120 times fewer GPU hours. Adding a simple, off-the-shelf RL recipe with no tool-specific design on top of LogT-SFT then beats prior methods with complex RL setups by a 9.59 point margin.

The setup is worth stating, because "thinking with images" is a specific thing. Vision-language models lose fine visual detail when the image is compressed into tokens, so a model that is asked to read small text or identify a distant object cannot recover what the encoder discarded. Text-based chain-of-thought reasoning cannot fix that, because reasoning cannot recover missing pixels. The standard response is to give the model an external tool, most commonly the zoom-in tool, which crops a region of the image at higher resolution and feeds it back. The model learns to call the tool, interleave the zoomed views with its textual reasoning, and eventually answer. Most existing methods train this with a two-stage pipeline: supervised fine-tuning to teach the tool-calling format, then reinforcement learning with outcome-based and tool-specific rewards. The paper's key empirical claim is that the improvements from this literature largely come from familiarity with the tool format acquired during post-training, not from genuine logical reasoning about when and how to call the tool. The model learns the shape of a tool call; it does not learn a strategy for finding what it is looking for.

LogT's answer is a data engine with two quality targets. The first is question quality. The VLM's input is enriched with detailed visual context in addition to the full image, which lets the synthesis step generate questions that actually target fine-grained details, followed by rigorous filtering to keep only high-quality question-answer pairs. The second is reasoning-trace quality, and this is where the logic chain lives. For each task, the engine first constructs a logic chain, a skeleton that specifies the key tool-use steps a human would take, such as multi-hop reasoning that focuses on different regions in sequence, or exhaustive search when the target is hard to localize. The logic chain then guides the VLM in generating a full, coherent reasoning trace with the appropriate tool calls interleaved. The engine also mimics a human efficiency behavior: for easy questions where the base model already answers correctly across samples, it synthesizes direct-answering traces with no tool call at all, so the model learns not to zoom when zooming is unnecessary. The whole pipeline is automated and uses only open-source models. The resulting dataset has 7.1 thousand high-quality entries.

The reported results, from the parts of the paper I could verify, are the efficiency story. With only 5.4 to 7.1 thousand synthesized examples, LogT-SFT on Qwen2.5-VL-7B surpasses the best open-source model trained with both SFT and RL, which the paper identifies as Mini-o3-RL, on average across seven benchmark test sets, while using 3.1 times less data and more than 120 times fewer GPU hours. The paper's own framing of the compute comparison is that the prior SOTA RL setup used roughly 3.1 thousand GPU hours against about 25.6 for LogT-SFT. On top of that, applying a plain off-the-shelf RL recipe with straightforward outcome-based rewards, and no tool-specific reward design, yields LogT-RL, which beats the prior methods by at least 9.59 points. Both the SFT and RL checkpoints also report better tool-call accuracy and tool-call efficiency, and a human evaluation shows the traces are more logically coherent. The authors also validate on a second base model, InternVL3.5-8B, fine-tuned on the same 7.1 thousand entries.

The skeptical read has a few points. First, the 120 times fewer GPU hours comparison is only as strong as the baseline's compute is representative. If the prior RL systems spent that much compute partly because the field was exploring the method, not because the method requires it, then the efficiency margin is somewhat an artifact of comparing a mature pipeline against the field's learning curve. The paper does not, in the parts I could verify, show a prior method re-run with the same budget to isolate the data-quality effect from the compute effect. Second, the "beats SOTA" claim is against a specific set of open-source SFT-plus-RL methods, and the seven test sets are the authors' own selection; the margin is reported as an average, so I would want to see the per-benchmark table before treating 9.59 as a typical gap rather than an average dominated by one or two sets. Third, the logic chain is generated by the same class of open-source VLMs that are being improved, which means the ceiling on trace quality is the teacher's own strategic competence; the filtering helps, but the engine can only teach the strategies its teachers can express. That is a standard limitation of synthetic data work, but it bounds the claim that the traces are "human-like."

That said, the paper's central empirical point is well-motivated and, if it holds, practically useful. Shifting compute from RL complexity to SFT data quality is a lever most labs can actually pull, and a 7.1 thousand entry dataset that beats a 3.1 thousand GPU hour pipeline is a strong efficiency result by any reading. The logic chain as an explicit intermediate representation, a skeleton of tool steps that conditions the full trace, is a clean design choice that decouples "what steps should I take" from "how do I phrase the reasoning," and it is the kind of structure that should transfer to other tool-use domains beyond zoom-in.

The engine's treatment of easy versus hard questions is a detail that a lot of synthetic-data pipelines get wrong, and it is worth spelling out. For questions where the base model already answers correctly across repeated samples, the average at 8 is 1, and the engine synthesizes direct-answering traces with no tool call at all. This teaches the model the negative behavior, which is to recognize when zooming is unnecessary and answer directly, rather than defaulting to a tool call on every question. For the harder questions, the logic chain is constructed first as a skeleton of the key tool-use steps, and the trace generation is conditioned on it, which is what produces the structured, multi-hop, or exhaustive-search patterns the paper shows. The data scale is deliberately small: 7.1 thousand entries in the full dataset, with the SFT model using as few as 5.4 thousand of them. Against that, the paper's compute comparison is that the prior SOTA RL setup used roughly 3.1 thousand GPU hours while LogT-SFT used about 25.6, which is the more than 120 times figure in the abstract. A human evaluation on top of the automatic benchmarks shows the traces are more coherent and higher quality, which matters because the whole method's premise is that trace quality, not trace quantity, is what transfers to the model.

My verdict: LogT is a clean, efficient, and well-argued data-engine paper whose claim that SFT data quality is the real bottleneck for visual tool use is credible and actionable. The caveats are about baseline compute accounting and average-reported margins, not about the core result. If you are training vision-language models with tools, this is a paper worth implementing.
