SpeakerMem-R1: Speaker-Centered Dual-Track Memory for Multi-Party Dialogue. The first three authors are Haobo Zheng, Tan Tang, and Yan Chen, from the State Key Laboratory of CAD and CG at Zhejiang University, with the remaining authors Weijie Wang and Yingcai Wu.

Most long-term memory work for language models is built around one user and one assistant: split the conversation, compress it, index it by relevance, retrieve the relevant chunk at query time. That works until the conversation has more than two people, and it fails in a specific, describable way. A group chat is not a longer version of a two-person chat. It contains speaker relations, who replied to whom, cross-topic branches, and states that get revised over time, and flattening that into a message stream you then compress in order throws away exactly the structure that makes group memory hard. The two bottlenecks the authors isolate are message attribution, distinguishing who said what, whom it is about, and whether a piece of information is private to one person or shared by the group, and state reconstruction, recovering the current or a past state from clues that are distributed across different members, the group as a whole, and different times. A relevance retriever can find the sentence, but it cannot tell you that it was said by the quiet member about a different member, and that it was later walked back.

SpeakerMem-R1's design is a dual-track memory. One track stores the raw, speaker-labeled messages verbatim, so the original evidence is always recoverable. The other track stores derived states, facts and relations that have been pulled out of the messages, organized into two views: a person-level view, what is true about each individual, and a group-level view, what is true about the group or shared by it. At query time a four-step procedure, Anchor, Separate, Resolve, Compose, retrieves from both tracks and organizes the evidence by participant, by event, and by time, then composes an answer. The reason for keeping both tracks is that each one fails where the other succeeds: the verbatim track has the full evidence but no structure, and the structured track has structure but can drop or garble the original. The ablations are the honest part of the paper, and they confirm the two tracks are genuinely complementary rather than redundant, and that the person-level and group-level views each contribute.

The training is where the R1 in the name comes from. The writer, the model that builds the structured track from the raw messages, is trained with a custom reward called SpeakerLevenshtein, an edit-distance measure that penalizes mis-attributing a fact to the wrong speaker, combined with speaker-conditioned GRPO, a group-relative policy gradient where the reward is conditioned on who said what. The point of the RL stage is to reduce attribution and update errors while keeping the query and answer modules frozen, so the ablation cleanly isolates the effect of training the writer. On a controlled set of three hundred five questions, the RL writer lifts the supervised writer's mean accuracy from fifty-seven point three eight percent to sixty-eight point twenty percent, an average gain of ten point eight two percentage points, or thirty-three more correct answers. For reference, a much larger frontier writer, a DeepSeek model, scores seventy-one point four eight percent on the same set, so the locally trained three-billion model is within three points of a far bigger one.

The headline numbers, on three multi-party and long-horizon benchmarks: GroupMemBench, SocialMemBench, and EverMemBench, with binary accuracies of forty-seven point nine percent, sixty-nine point two percent, and sixty-one point nine percent respectively, beating the best mainstream framework on each by three point three, twelve point four, and nine point four percentage points. On the public EverMemBench leaderboard it reports sixty-two point three three percent, the best among the latest state-of-the-art frameworks, and on LoCoMo, used here as a two-person boundary test, it reaches seventy point eight five percent across all nineteen hundred eighty-six questions.

The skeptical read. The dual-track, person-plus-group decomposition is a sound answer to a real failure mode, and the fact that the ablations show the tracks and the two views are each load-bearing, rather than decorative, is what separates this from a lot of memory-system papers that bolt a graph onto a retriever and claim the graph did the work. The RL writer result is the most interesting claim, that a small, locally deployable model trained with a speaker-aware reward closes most of the gap to a much larger writer, which is a genuinely useful result for anyone who cannot ship a frontier model. What I would want before trusting the leaderboard claims is a look at the baselines and the metric. Binary accuracy on multi-party memory benchmarks is a coarse signal, and the gains over the best mainstream framework, three to twelve points, sit in a range where benchmark choice and question phrasing can move things. The paper is also self-reported on the EverMemBench leaderboard, so the best-reported-result claim is the authors' own number, not a neutral one. If you are building memory for group chat or multi-agent conversation, the person-level versus group-level split and the SpeakerLevenshtein reward are worth stealing directly. Verdict: a well-motivated, well-ablated dual-track memory that treats attribution and relational structure as first-class, with a small-RL-writer result that is the most practically interesting claim, though the leaderboard edges over mainstream baselines are in the range where benchmark details matter.

The query-time procedure is where the two tracks actually earn their keep, and the four steps have a logic worth following. Anchor takes the question and locates the relevant participants, events, and time ranges, so the retrieval is conditioned on who and when before it looks at what. Separate pulls the candidate evidence from each track independently, the verbatim messages and the derived states, because the two tracks index differently and a single retrieval path would miss one of them. Resolve is the attribution step, matching evidence to the right person or the right group view, which is where the person-level and group-level decomposition pays off, because a fact that is true of one member and a fact that is true of the group resolve to different places and a flattened store cannot tell them apart. Compose integrates the surviving evidence by participant, event, and time and builds the answer, which is where state reconstruction happens, assembling a current or past state from clues that no single message contains. The design implication is that the hard part of multi-party memory is not retrieval, it is the bookkeeping of who-said-about-whom-and-when, and the system is built around that bookkeeping rather than around a better retriever.

The SpeakerLevenshtein reward deserves a moment, because it is a small idea with a clean target. The dominant error mode in building the structured track is mis-attribution, assigning a fact to the wrong speaker, or to the group when it was personal, or recording a state that was later revised without marking the revision. A plain fact-level reward cannot see that error, because the fact itself is right, only its owner is wrong. An edit distance that is sensitive to the speaker label treats a wrong-owner fact as a real edit, so the writer is directly penalized for the error that matters most in multi-party settings. Paired with speaker-conditioned GRPO, where the group-relative advantage is computed at the writing position with the speaker as part of the condition, the training signal is aimed precisely at attribution and update fidelity. The controlled result, fifty-seven point three eight to sixty-eight point twenty on three hundred five questions with the query and answer modules frozen, is a clean causal claim about the writer, because nothing else in the pipeline moved.

The cost table in the appendix is the unglamorous part that a deployer would actually read. The dual-track system makes far more calls and moves far more tokens per question than a plain BM25 or dense retrieval baseline, by an order of magnitude or more, because it is maintaining and querying a structured store rather than searching a vector index. That is the price of getting attribution and relational structure right, and it is a real consideration for anything that has to run at low latency or at scale, which is also why the locally deployable small writer is a meaningful selling point, you can run the writer on your own hardware and pay the token cost on infrastructure you control. The paper reports both binary accuracy and token F1, and the ablations show the two tracks and the two views are each complementary, which is the internal consistency check that the architecture is not just adding parts. The remaining open question is scale, these benchmarks are hundreds to a few thousand questions, and whether the person-group decomposition holds when the group has dozens of members and the history spans months is exactly the regime where the cost table starts to bite and the relational bookkeeping becomes hardest.
