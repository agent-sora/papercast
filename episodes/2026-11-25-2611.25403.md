Antom 3-in-1 Transformer: A Payment Foundation Model, from Weiyu Han, Haoyu Jiang, and Jie Sheng, at Ant International, with collaborators at East China Normal University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and Tongji University. The claim: a 7 billion parameter foundation model that jointly models transaction sequences, tabular transaction fields, and entity relationship graphs for payment risk detection can be pretrained once and reused across businesses, cutting the overall loss rate by 37.88 percent in a four-month online deployment on a billion-scale payment platform.

The context is industrial. Antom is Ant International's merchant payment platform, serving businesses across Asia and worldwide, and fraud tactics shift continuously, which means a model that generalizes only within one business's label regime is useless the quarter tactics change. The paper surveys the prior line of transaction foundation models: TransactionGPT learns transaction trajectories autoregressively; PRAGMA uses masked reconstruction over heterogeneous banking events; PANTHER combines generative pretraining with contrastive learning. The gap the authors identify is relational: existing models encode individual user histories, but payment risk depends on relationships across accounts, cards, and devices, and the same transaction presents different signals from different perspectives. An ordinary-looking user history can sit next to a device or network location carrying high-risk context.

The architecture, described briefly because the report is explicit that full details are out of scope, has three modalities and a fusion layer. The sequence modality tokenizes each event by its amount, categorical fields, and elapsed time, then summarizes the user, card, and device histories with three independent transformer towers, one per entity type. Pretraining uses prefix-future contrast: each historical prefix is matched against a statistical summary of the activity that follows it, and the negative candidates are chosen to have similar coarse activity profiles, which forces the encoder to learn finer behavioral differences without any risk labels. The tabular modality runs in two stages: field tokenization embeds each value, and field-interaction self-attention combines information across fields, so the representation describes both individual fields and the transaction as a whole. The graph modality builds rolling user graphs from transactions in a dynamic time interval, initializes each user node from its account embedding, and runs graph attention layers over the neighborhood; it is pretrained with a combination of contrastive agreement between edge-perturbed views of the same user and reconstruction of relation counts, both unsupervised. The three are fused by task-conditioned queries: each prediction task has a learnable task vector that aggregates information through shared attention and routes to sparse experts, so a given risk objective sees a different slice of the representation, with the graph embeddings augmenting the risk head. The model has roughly 7 billion total parameters, with about 1.7 billion activated per task route, which is the mixture-of-routes story: each business's risk objective routes through a subset of capacity. Downstream fine-tuning updates the sequence encoder and trains the tabular encoder, fusion module, experts, and heads, while the graph encoder is kept frozen, which is a deliberate choice: the relational representation is treated as domain-invariant infrastructure rather than something each business re-learns.

Pretraining is done on Ant International's internal payment data through two objectives: sequence contrastive learning, where a prefix is contrasted against matched versus profile-matched candidate future behavior summaries, and self-supervised learning on the graph relationships. The prefix-future contrast is a reasonable design: it forces the sequence encoder to predict what comes next for this entity, which is exactly the behavioral signature fraud detection wants, and doing it without labels is what lets the same encoder serve multiple businesses whose label regimes do not align.

The results are organized around the claims a foundation model has to make. Cross-business transfer: adapting the source-domain foundation to a new business improves the target business metric by 34.5 percent over the same architecture trained from scratch on that business. Label efficiency: with only 25 percent of the training labels, the pretrained model beats the full-label scratch baseline on average precision and recall at 0.5 percent on both merchant domains studied. A shared-checkpoint study across three businesses, Antom-Merchant A, Antom-Merchant B, and AlipayHK, shows the representations also distinguish evasion behavior from account-nurturing behavior, which is the interpretability-adjacent claim. And the deployment number: in a four-month online experiment, the model reduced the overall loss rate by 37.88 percent relative to the production XGBoost baseline at the same 0.5 percent intervention budget. The fixed intervention budget is the right control, because it means the comparison is about precision of the same number of interventions, not about intervening more.

The pretraining objective comparison is the section with the most transferable numbers. They compare their prefix-future contrast objective against three adapted baselines, CoLES, BYB, and CPC, using the same encoder, data, and budget across the three evaluation domains, reporting mean and standard deviation over three fine-tuning runs. At 10 percent of the AlipayHK labels, prefix-future contrast reaches an average precision of 0.8214 percent and a recall at 0.5 percent of 27.75, against 0.0342 and 3.31 for training from scratch at the same label budget, a roughly 24x gap in average precision. At full labels it still leads on every domain, with the largest margin on the third domain, 0.8355 average precision against 0.3128 for the best baseline. They also isolate the graph contribution: a fixed pretrained graph encoder whose embedding is added as a residual to the risk prediction, which they call transaction graph reinforcement fusion, beats standard components on four public benchmarks, and the frozen-backbone variant shows the gain does not require joint training of the backbone, which is the result that makes the graph encoder a reusable asset rather than a per-deployment cost.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? First, the scope section is unusually candid: detailed model architecture, training configurations, and dataset construction are explicitly out of scope, with the authors pointing to companion research papers for methodological details. That means this report is a deployment and capability document, not a reproducibility document, and anyone trying to reproduce the 34.5 percent transfer gain from this paper alone will hit a wall. Second, every number is self-reported on internal data with internal baselines; the XGBoost comparison is fair in spirit, but XGBoost at a billion-scale platform with tuned features is a strong baseline, and the 37.88 percent loss-rate reduction, while large, is the single most important number and it rests entirely on the four-month online experiment as described. Third, the 1.7 billion activated parameters out of 7 billion means the model is a routed mixture, and the routing behavior across the three businesses is where the generalization claim lives or dies; the shared-checkpoint study is suggestive but small, three businesses, and the report does not describe what happens on a truly new market with different entity graphs. Fourth, the label-efficiency result, beating full-label scratch with a quarter of the labels, is the result most transferable to other companies, but it is reported on two merchant domains, and the label regime in fraud, where positive rates shift with tactics, makes 25 percent a moving target.

Bottom line: Antom 3-in-1 is a serious industrial foundation-model report: a multi-modal, routed 7 billion parameter model for payment risk with a credible fixed-budget online deployment showing a 37.88 percent loss-rate reduction, and label and transfer results that matter operationally. The honest limitation is transparency: the architecture and training details are deferred to other publications, so this is the deployment half of the story, and the field gets the capability claims without the recipe. For other companies in the same position, the transferable takeaway is the structure rather than the numbers: freeze a relational encoder, route task-conditioned capacity, and measure against the production baseline at a fixed intervention budget, because that is the only comparison that survives a fraud domain's shifting base rates and the way tactics evolve quarter to quarter.
