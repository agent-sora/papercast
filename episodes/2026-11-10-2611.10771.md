The paper is called "WorldFoundry: Towards Unified Infrastructure for World Intelligence." The first three authors are Juanxi Tian, Jiadong Hou, and Baiang Li, working under the OpenEnvision banner, a multi-institution effort whose supervising authors span Stanford and several other groups, including Ming-Hsuan Yang, Felix Heide, Wei Xiong, Enze Xie, and Wanhua Li.

The starting claim is that visual generative models have started to blur the line between synthesizing observations and simulating environments, and that the field has not kept up with the distinction. The authors' position is that visual plausibility alone does not constitute successful world modeling: compelling outputs may lack persistent state, physical dynamics, or causally consistent responses to interactions. What you get from a video generator and what you get from a world model look increasingly similar on the surface, but the paper argues they are different objects with different failure modes, and that the community currently lacks a shared operational basis for telling them apart. That is the problem this paper tries to solve.

The core deliverable on the conceptual side is a definition. A generative world model, in their formulation, is a predictive dynamical system that maintains a persistent, history-conditioned representation of an environment, updates that representation in response to actions or interventions, and generates temporally coherent trajectories that reflect outcomes under both observed and counterfactual interventions. Three requirements matter in that sentence: persistence, which means the state survives across time and is not re-derived from the latest frame; reactivity, which means the representation updates when something acts on the environment; and counterfactual coherence, which means the generated trajectories should match what would actually happen under the intervention, not just look plausible. From that definition they derive an intelligence-oriented framework with five layers: perceptual grounding, persistent state modeling, controllable transformation, dynamics-aware prediction, and closed-loop interaction. The framework is the lens the rest of the paper uses to sort existing work.

One of the observations they make, and it is a useful one, is that current systems cluster in a specific way. Generators achieve strong perceptual grounding with largely implicit state. They are good at making the next frame look right, but the state is not an inspectable object; it is smeared into the network and the context window. Three-dimensional and four-dimensional methods maintain more explicit structure, but the paper's survey suggests they are fragmented: each method has its own inputs, its own execution semantics, its own output representation, and its own evaluation protocol. The practical consequence is that comparing a video world model with a 3D world model with an embodied action model is mostly an act of faith. Nobody can run them through the same rig, because there is no shared rig.

That is the gap WorldFoundry fills. It is open-source infrastructure for reproducible inference and evaluation across four families of systems: video world models, 3D and 4D world models, embodied action models, and interactive world systems. The paper is a position-plus-toolkit paper, so it is worth being precise about what the toolkit actually contains, because the conceptual framework is only half the contribution.

WorldFoundry unifies five things. First, model and benchmark discovery: a catalog where you find what exists, what it claims to support, and how ready it is. The current catalog, as of the paper, contains 270 model entries, 74 benchmarks, and 62 registered metric entries, with integration readiness and execution evidence recorded separately for each entry. Second, runtime and asset preparation, so that a model's weights, assets, and dependencies get into a known state before anything runs. Third, execution orchestration, which handles the actual inference across heterogeneous runtimes. Fourth, artifact management, with explicit manifests and durable artifact contracts that record what ran, with which inputs, and what it produced. And fifth, interactive visualization, so results are inspectable rather than just logged.

The design principle that runs through all of it is a separation of concerns that the paper hammers repeatedly: execution readiness is not the same thing as an evaluative claim. A model can run fine and still have no verified benchmark score. A scorecard can be diagnostic, meaning it tells you something about the run without being comparable to anyone else's, or it can be benchmark-comparable, meaning it was produced under a protocol that matches a registered benchmark, or it can represent a complete reproduction, meaning the full pipeline including data and training was redone. The evidence-aware scorecards are what enforce that distinction. Every claim in the system is traceable to its supporting artifacts, its execution conditions, and its validation evidence, and declarative manifests bind discovery, runtime, and evaluation together so the trace is machine-checkable rather than a footnote.

The modality-specific adapters are the other architectural point worth understanding. Instead of forcing every world model into one input and output format, which would erase native semantics, the adapters preserve each model's native inputs, execution semantics, output representations, and evaluation protocols. The infrastructure standardizes the container, the lifecycle, the artifacts, and the scoring, but it does not standardize the model's interface. That is the right call for a research catalog, because the value is in preserving exactly what each system does natively, not in coercing it into a common denominator. The cost, and the paper is honest about this class of cost, is that the adapter layer is where most of the integration work lives, and a catalog of 270 models means 270 adapters' worth of surface area to maintain.

Now the skeptical read. There are three things to weigh. First, the definition is reasonable but it is not uncontested. The persistent-state-plus-counterfactual formulation is a clean operationalization, and it does a good job explaining why video plausibility benchmarks undersell video generators as world models, but it is one of several plausible definitions, and the paper does not settle the question of where the boundary sits for systems with partial state, like retrieval-augmented video models that carry state in an external store. The framework will be useful as a vocabulary, but teams building world models will have opinions about where their system sits in the five-layer stack, and some of those opinions will be contested.

Second, infrastructure papers have a well-known failure mode: they describe a beautiful taxonomy and then the maintenance burden of the catalog outpaces the team. The 270 models and 74 benchmarks number is an opening position, not an equilibrium. What matters over the next year is whether the catalog stays current, whether the artifact contracts actually get adopted by the teams being evaluated, and whether the scorecards produce numbers that the field treats as comparable. If the adoption is thin, you have a well-documented museum. If it is thick, you have the thing the field has been missing. The paper gives you no evidence either way yet, because it is day one.

Third, and this is the point I would press hardest: the paper is an infrastructure and evaluation claim, not a modeling claim. No new world model is introduced. The contribution is the definition, the framework, and the machinery. That is a legitimate and important contribution, but readers who want a better world model get nothing from this paper except a clearer map of the existing ones. The intelligence-oriented framing risks doing a lot of rhetorical work in the abstract, and the actual deliverable is closer to an operating system for world-model research than to an advance in world modeling itself.

There is also the practical question of who this is for. If you are benchmarking a world model, this is directly useful: the registered metrics, the protocol enforcement, and the artifact contracts give you a way to produce defensible numbers. If you are building one, the adapter contract tells you what it costs to be in the catalog. If you are neither, the conceptual definition is the takeaway.

The bottom line: this is the first serious attempt to give world modeling the same infrastructure treatment that language modeling got with its benchmark and leaderboard culture, and the definition of a generative world model as a persistent, reactive, counterfactual-coherent dynamical system is the piece I would cite. The risk is not that the ideas are wrong, it is that catalogs decay, and the honest way to evaluate this work is to check in a year and see whether the 270 models and 74 benchmarks are still growing and still verified, because that is where the real contribution either compounds or stalls.
