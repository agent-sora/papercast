Incentivizing Self-Learning in Large Language Models via Reinforcement Learning, by Lingkai Kong, Yuzhe Gu, and Haiteng Zhao, from Shanghai AI Laboratory, with co-authors at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, MMLab at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, the University of Science and Technology of China, and Fudan University, is an RL paper that trains a model to teach itself from a textbook in the middle of solving a problem, rather than from a pre-assembled context.

The capability they target is what they call self-learning: given a problem and a large reference book, the model has to decide what to study, read the relevant sections, and fold that knowledge into a developing proof, with the solution withheld. This is different from retrieval-augmented generation, where the relevant context is found and handed over before reasoning starts, and it is different from meta-RL or test-time adaptation, where the model learns from the outcomes of its own attempts. Here there is no repeated-attempt feedback and no preselected context; the model must interleave reasoning with deliberate reading of a static corpus. The concrete task is textbook-based mathematics: each problem is paired with a structured textbook whose exercise solutions have been stripped out of the model-visible text.

The data comes from 158 undergraduate and graduate mathematics textbooks, parsed into chapter-indexed sections with exercises and solutions extracted and removed, yielding 5,183 proof problems. The average book runs 56 sections and about 208 thousand tokens, so this is a genuinely long-form study environment. Training uses 4,557 problems from 132 books, and evaluation is split three ways to separate the effects: in-domain book, 205 held-out problems from 4 training books; out-of-domain book, 155 problems from 7 books covering trained subjects; and out-of-domain subject, 266 problems from 19 books on probability and statistics that were never in task-specific training.

The interface is called BookNavigator, and it is deliberately minimal. There are three actions. get-toc returns the full table of contents for orientation. search-index runs keyword retrieval across all sections and returns ranked section identifiers with matching snippets. read-section returns the full text of a named section. The model can interleave reasoning with any of these as many times as it wants, up to eight interaction turns and a 64 thousand-token trajectory cap. The final proof is judged by an LLM judge against the withheld reference solution, and that judge is what provides the RL signal.

The reward design is where the paper does its actual work, because a final-proof-only reward is known to be a weak signal for retrieval decisions: a correct trajectory can be full of redundant reads, and the model can quietly stop consulting the book once it gets good enough at solving things unaided. They add two things on top of standard GRPO. First, an annealed exploration bonus: for any correct trajectory that made at least one successful tool call, a bonus is added, scaled by a coefficient that follows a cosine schedule down from 1 to 0.25 over the 50 rollout steps. This keeps an incentive to open the book throughout training rather than letting it collapse. Second, round-level tool credit: a frozen copy of the initial policy acts as a tool judge that labels each non-terminal round as useful, neutral, or harmful, based on whether the retrieved material shows up in the next round of reasoning. Harmful rounds get a score of 0.5, useful and neutral get 1.0. The trajectory-level advantage from the reward is then redistributed across rounds proportional to how far each round's tool score deviates from its token-weighted mean, which preserves the overall advantage while letting the gradient punish redundant retrieval and reward productive study. The reference solution and the final correctness verdict are withheld from this tool judge, and the redistribution strength is set to 1.

The main result is a reversal. Before RL, the self-learning protocol is the worst of the five they compare: overall proof accuracy of 51.5 percent, below no-book at 55.7 percent and below every fixed-retrieval baseline. After 50 rollout steps of GRPO it reaches 79.5 percent overall, the best of all protocols, and the largest improvement, 28 percentage points. It beats the strongest fixed-retrieval baseline, top-k sections, by 6.4 points, and it wins on both out-of-domain splits, 85.2 percent on out-of-domain book and 86.7 percent on out-of-domain subject.

The attribution analysis is the part worth reading, because it separates the story from the easy one. If the gain were just general reasoning improvement, removing the book should not hurt much. In fact, RL also lifts the no-book condition from 55.7 to 71.7 percent, a real 16-point gain in unaided reasoning, but the trained policy is still 7.8 points better with its paired book than without it, so textbook access continues to contribute independently. If the gain were memorization of the specific book, swapping in a different book on the same subject should collapse it; it does not, staying at 75.2 percent, well above the no-book 71.7. If it were dependence on the exact source section, deleting that section and its neighbors should matter more than deleting a random set of the same size; and it does, a 5.2-point gap between source removal at 74.6 and non-source removal at 79.8, but neither removal brings it back down to no-book level. So the evidence points to a transferable study strategy rather than memorization.

They also test cross-task transfer without the BookNavigator at all: on BrowseComp-Plus the trained policy gains 1.7 points over base, and on CL-bench it gains 2.3, the best of all RL protocols on both. But the transfer is selective. On CL-bench's procedural task execution category, every RL-trained policy actually falls below base, which the authors attribute to a mismatch between proof-oriented training and faithful workflow execution. That is an honest negative result and worth keeping in mind.

The ablation is the cleanest evidence for the reward design. Correctness-only GRPO raises overall accuracy to 74.9 percent but collapses tool use: the fraction of trajectories with any tool call drops from 36.9 percent at base to 6.7 percent, and calls per trajectory fall from 1.5 to 0.3. Tool credit alone does not reverse that, only 7.0 percent tool use. Adding the exploration bonus alone pushes tool-using trajectories to 79.9 percent and improves the out-of-domain splits, and the full method combining both gets the strongest result on every split while holding tool calls per trajectory at 3.0, which is below the exploration-bonus-only 5.9. So the method gets better generalization without maximizing tool-call frequency, which is exactly the failure mode you would not want to ship.

Skeptically: the whole setup leans on two LLM judges, the correctness judge running an eight-shot pessimistic protocol, meaning a proof only passes if all eight independent reviews call it correct, and the tool judge which is a frozen copy of the very policy being trained. That frozen self-judging is a neat trick and it is what makes the tool credit cheap, but it also means the fine-grained signal is calibrated to the initial model's sense of what good study looks like, and it cannot see the reference solution, so it is fundamentally a heuristic about process, not a check of outcome. The 50-step rollout budget is short, and the gains on the external benchmarks, 1.7 and 2.3 points, are small enough that I would want them on more seeds before treating cross-task transfer as established. The 86.7 percent on the out-of-domain subject split, however, with the book-swapping and source-removal controls, is the strongest argument in the paper and it is the result I would actually believe.

Bottom line: this is a credible demonstration that RL can turn a weak, book-consulting protocol into a strong one, and that the gains are a mix of real unaided reasoning improvement and a transferable reading strategy rather than memorization, thanks to controls that would have exposed the easy story. The reward design, annealed exploration plus round-level tool credit from a frozen self-judge, is a reusable ingredient for any agent that has to learn to use reference material, but the cross-task transfer evidence is thin enough to warrant more replication before you bet on it.
