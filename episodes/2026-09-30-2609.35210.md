Understanding On-Policy Distillation: A Mechanistic Interpretability Perspective via Sparse Crosscoders. The first three authors are Zichao Yu, Qianshuo Ye, and Xu Wang, from the University of Hong Kong, the University of Cambridge, and the Shenzhen Loop Area Institute, with corresponding author Difan Zou.

The question this paper asks is deceptively simple, and it matters because on-policy distillation has become the standard post-training technique for reasoning models, adopted by Qwen3, MiMo-V2-Flash, GLM-5, and Kimi K3. The common belief is that on-policy distillation transfers knowledge from a stronger teacher to the student. But the evidence is mixed: on-policy distillation can fail to improve on, or even degrade, the student it starts from, and prior work suggests it mainly improves sampling efficiency rather than expanding the student's capability boundary. What on-policy distillation actually does to the student's internal representations has not been measured. This is a mechanistic interpretability paper, and its tool is the sparse crosscoder, a dictionary of features learned sparsely from a model's residual stream, which has become the workhorse of modern interpretability analysis.

The technical obstacle is specific to comparing models. A standard crosscoder learns one shared feature dictionary that can encode the teacher, the student before distillation, and the student after distillation into one set of feature activations. But that shared dictionary identifies model-specific features; it cannot tell you how a model's use of its features changes, because every model is projected into the same activation space. The paper's methodological contribution is the swap readout. The crosscoder has slots for a teacher activation and a student activation, and the swap readout places the same student checkpoint in both student slots, with the teacher's activation fixed, and reads the features that checkpoint uses. This lets the authors measure, per feature, how training changes the student's firing rate, even for checkpoints the crosscoder has never seen. The readout is the right instrument for this question, and it is reusable beyond this paper.

The setup is worth being precise about. The sparse crosscoder is trained to reconstruct activations of all three models, the pre-distillation student, the post-distillation student, and the teacher, into one shared feature space. Because the crosscoder is trained jointly on all three, it learns a dictionary that is simultaneously useful for all of them, and the features it extracts are shared by construction. The swap readout exploits this: for any given student checkpoint, you feed its activations into both the student-before and student-after slots of the crosscoder, holding the teacher slot fixed at the teacher's activations. The resulting feature firing rates are those of that specific checkpoint, measured in a space that is comparable across checkpoints and across the teacher. This is what makes it possible to track feature usage across a training run, checkpoint by checkpoint, without retraining the crosscoder for each one.

One detail from the training-signal analysis is worth repeating because it explains where the reweighting happens. In an on-policy distillation run, the student writes a rollout and the teacher scores every token, and the paper shows that the distillation signal peaks at decision tokens, the positions where the model commits to a branching reasoning step. The swap readout places the same student checkpoint in both student slots of the crosscoder, with the teacher's activation held fixed, and reads the features the checkpoint uses. Because the readout is checkpoint-specific, it measures per-checkpoint feature usage even for checkpoints the crosscoder encoder has never seen, which is what makes it applicable to intermediate checkpoints during training rather than only the final model.

The findings are stated plainly and are the heart of the paper. Across three on-policy distillation settings, on-policy distillation neither creates new features in the student nor passes on the teacher's own features. It leaves the firing rates of over 98 percent of the student's frequently used features within 20 percent of their pre-training values. The student's feature repertoire is essentially unchanged; what changes is how the student uses the features it already shares with the teacher. The signal concentrates at decision tokens, the positions in a rollout where the model commits to a reasoning step, which matches where the distillation's training signal peaks.

The second half of the paper examines the supervised fine-tuning warm-up on the teacher's rollouts that commonly precedes on-policy distillation and is known to make it more effective. The expected story, that the warm-up adds new features, is not what happens. The warm-up reweights the shared features in two ways. First, it already raises and lowers many of the features that on-policy distillation later raises and lowers, doing part of on-policy distillation's work in advance. Second, it changes features that on-policy distillation alone would not touch, most notably features for conversation format, style of reasoning, and mathematical notation, and those changes persist through on-policy distillation. The paper's causal test is the clean part: imposing this reweighting on the features of a directly distilled student, without changing the student's weights at all, brings its accuracy close to that of the warmed-up student, whereas the same reweighting applied to shuffled features does not. So the warm-up's benefit is carried by the feature reweighting itself, not by the weight updates that happen to produce it.

The overall conclusion is that on-policy distillation behaves more like a reweighting of existing features than an acquisition of new ones. The student learns from the teacher how to use the features they already share. This reframes what you should expect from on-policy distillation: it is not a capability-transmission mechanism in the strong sense, and if the student and teacher do not already share the relevant features, reweighting will not create them.

Here is the skeptical read. The 98 percent figure, that nearly all frequently used features stay within 20 percent of their firing rates, is a strong negative result, but the interesting content lives in the 2 percent that do move, and the paper's granularity on exactly which features change and why they are the right ones is where you would want more. The swap readout is a good instrument, but crosscoder features are themselves an approximation of the model's computation, and a reweighting measured in feature-activation space may understate structural changes that the dictionary does not capture. The three on-policy distillation settings are a reasonable spread, but the models involved are the standard research-scale pairs, and whether the conclusion holds when the teacher is substantially larger than the student, the regime where on-policy distillation is most often deployed, is not directly tested. The causal reweighting experiment is compelling within the crosscoder's frame, but it shows that the reweighting is sufficient to recover the accuracy, not that it is the only mechanism at work in the real training run.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the methodological contribution: the swap readout is a genuinely useful tool for measuring how training changes a model's feature usage, and I would expect it to get reused in follow-up work. I would trust the headline negative result, that on-policy distillation is a reweighting rather than an acquisition, because it is consistent with the mixed empirical record of on-policy distillation failing to expand capability boundaries. Before building decisions on it, I would re-verify the 98 percent stability figure on a teacher-student pair with a larger capability gap, check whether the decision-token concentration holds for models without explicit reasoning traces, and look at the warm-up result carefully, because the finding that the warm-up changes conversation format, reasoning style, and mathematical notation features in ways on-policy distillation alone does not is a practical warning: if you care about those surface properties, the warm-up, not the distillation, is where you set them. The engineering gotcha for practitioners is the warm-up's persistence: those format and notation changes survive the subsequent distillation, so a warm-up chosen for one product behavior can lock in that behavior for the final model.

This is a careful interpretability paper that answers a question the on-policy distillation literature had been assuming an answer to: it reweights what you already have, and the warm-up before it does real, persistent work. If you are running on-policy distillation in production, the actionable takeaway is to treat the SFT warm-up as a first-class behavioral knob, not just a training convenience.
