Paper four is PhysBrain 1.5: From Vision-Language Models to Physical Foundation Models, from the DeepCybo team, with Yu Bin and Haipeng Cao among the listed authors. DeepCybo is a robotics and physical-AI lab, and this is a foundation-model release in the embodied sense: an 8-billion-parameter model that unifies three jobs in one set of weights, understanding a physical scene, generating an end-effector action trajectory, and predicting what the scene will look like next. The framing is the physical loop: you observe, you act, the environment changes, you observe again, and the paper's argument is that those three stages should share a model and a training objective rather than being three separate systems bolted together.

The architecture is the interesting part. They start from a general vision-language model in the Qwen3-VL family and extend its vocabulary. The unified vocabulary is the union of the language tokens, a set of action tokens, and a set of visual tokens, and all three kinds of target are generated by the same backbone with the same autoregressive next-token objective, selected per training example by a loss mask. For action, the representation is a 10-dimensional vector per timestep: 3 for relative translation, 6 for relative rotation in a continuous six-dimensional representation of the rotation matrix, and 1 for absolute gripper closure. The model predicts chunks of 16 timesteps, always relative to the current wrist pose, so the targets are not accumulated step by step and do not drift with chunk length. Those continuous action vectors are discretized with the ActionPiece tokenizer, which they trained on 28.7 million 16-step end-effector segments, about 459 million action timesteps, and which gives a 512-token action vocabulary; a full 16-step wrist trajectory becomes 32 discrete tokens. For the visual side, future frames are represented as three token streams: RGB, depth, and a robot mask, all spatially aligned. Human and robot data share the same action representation, with human motion converted to robot end-effector form using a Human-as-Humanoid retargeting that keeps both wrists and drops the fingers.

Training has two stages. Pre-training is entirely from human interaction video, organized into task-centered episodes from their egocentric and panoramic captures, where each episode pairs the semantic and spatial context of a task with the recovered human motion and the subsequent visual state. The data taxonomy has three pre-training families: physical perception data, which is the scene understanding side; human action data, where the recovered motion becomes the action supervision; and human-interaction future-state data, where the frames that follow the action become the prediction supervision. All three flow through the same masked next-token objective, so a single episode can supervise language, motion, and future frames at once. Then supervised fine-tuning on a mixture of human demonstrations, real robot trajectories, and simulated experience, so the action side sees actual embodiments rather than only retargeted human motion, with separate fine-tuning splits for understanding, action, and future-state data, all trained on the same backbone so the three capabilities keep reinforcing each other.

The headline result is across 28 embodied understanding benchmarks: the 8-billion model averages 72.5, which they report as the new open-source state of the art, first on 14 of the benchmarks and second on 10. The leaderboard they put next to it: GPT-6 Astra at 73.3, Gemini 3.6 Flash at 73.0, then PhysBrain at 72.5, then Claude Opus 5 at 67.9, and the next open-source models, Hy-Embodied-VLM at 66.0, Embodied-R1.5 at 64.9, RynnBrain at 63.1, Cosmos 3 Nano at 62.3, and Qwen3-VL 8B at 59.5. The gap to the top proprietary pair is under a point, and the gap to the rest of the open field is 6 to 13 points. The action and future-state sides are shown qualitatively: generated end-effector trajectories on manipulation tasks, and predicted future scenes with aligned RGB, depth, and mask outputs. They do not run a large-scale closed-loop manipulation benchmark, which is the obvious thing to ask for, and the qualitative samples do not substitute for one.

Walking the per-benchmark table is where the story gets more honest. On foundational visual-spatial perception, PhysBrain 1.5 scores 87.9 on BLINK and 90.0 on CV-Bench, the best open-source numbers in those columns, and it beats its own base model, Qwen3-VL instruct, on every single one of the 28 rows, with gains of 2 to 9 points on most of them. The biggest relative jumps are in the spatial and multi-view category: 63.0 on 3DSRBench against 53.6 for the base, 81.8 on EmbSpatial against 79.9, and 86.2 on MindCube against 39.2, which is the single largest improvement in the table and the one I would want to reproduce before believing it, because MindCube is a cube-rotation style task where a model can overfit to the visual format. On the embodied cognition and planning side the margins are thinner: 72.8 on COSMOS against 70.4 for the base and 72.8 for RynnBrain, essentially tied, and 69.3 on EgoPlan-Bench2, where Hy-Embodied-VLM sits at 70.4. So the understanding claim is real and broad, but it is concentrated in the perception half of the 28, and on the reasoning half it is trading places with the 30-billion open model.

Why this design, as opposed to a separate vision-language model plus a separate policy network? Because the shared token space means the same weight update improves all three capabilities at once, and the loss-mask formulation means you can train on pure understanding data, pure action data, or prediction data without a separate head for each. This is the second release in the line: Phys-Brain 1.0 took egocentric human video and converted it into structured physical-commonsense supervision that was then used to pretrain a separate policy, and 1.5 collapses the two stages into one model that does the perception and the action in the same forward pass. The choice to make action targets relative to the current pose, rather than absolute, is also deliberate: absolute targets make the task harder for no reason, because the robot knows where its wrist is, and relative 16-step chunks keep the prediction bounded regardless of where in the workspace the task is happening. The cost is that the action tokenizer has to be good, because a bad discretization of continuous motion is a ceiling no amount of downstream data fixes, and their training the tokenizer on nearly half a billion timesteps before the main training is a deliberate answer to that. The other cost is that the depth and mask streams are a commitment: the model has to be evaluated on whether its predicted futures are physically plausible, and the paper shows samples but does not quantify prediction error.

My skeptical read: the 28-benchmark average is self-organized, the proprietary comparisons are copied from those labs' published numbers rather than re-run, and the action-generation and future-prediction claims rest on qualitative figures with no closed-loop success rate, so the embodied half of the foundation-model claim is less supported than the understanding half. The MindCube jump from 39 to 86 also deserves a second look, and the Human-as-Humanoid retargeting that drops the fingers is a real limitation if you care about dexterous manipulation, which is exactly where the future-state prediction is supposed to shine. The transferable contributions are the unified vocabulary with per-token loss masks, the 10-dimensional relative action chunk, and the practice of training the action tokenizer on a corpus an order of magnitude larger than the trajectories you will later fine-tune on. Bottom line: PhysBrain 1.5 is a credible step toward a single model that perceives, acts, and predicts, the understanding numbers are real and strong for 8 billion parameters, but the action and prediction sides need a closed-loop benchmark before the "physical foundation model" label earns its keep.
