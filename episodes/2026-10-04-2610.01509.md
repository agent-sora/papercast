Sharpening Tax in Post-Training is from Changdae Oh, Qi Zeng, and Qi Qi, with the work done at Meta Superintelligence Labs, in collaboration with the University of Wisconsin–Madison, New York University, and Stanford University. It is a study of what reinforcement-learning post-training actually does to a model's ability to solve tasks across many sampled attempts.

The premise is a hypothesis that has been circulating about reinforcement-learning post-training: that it does not teach the model new capabilities, but merely sharpens behaviors the base model already had, improving single-shot accuracy at the cost of solution coverage. That trade-off had been observed on math and coding tasks, and the natural question is whether it extends to agentic work, where multi-turn tool use might require capabilities genuinely acquired during post-training. The paper's answer is that the trade-off extends further than expected. Pre-trained models, given only a light inference harness, serve as capable agents: despite far lower pass@1 accuracy, they often beat their post-trained counterparts on pass@K, solution coverage, given a sufficient test-time budget. The mechanism behind this is that post-training pushes tasks toward two extremes, always solved or never solved. That makes sampling more efficient and more consistent, which is why pass@1 goes up, but it narrows the set of solution paths the model can produce, which is why coverage goes down as K grows.

To measure this cost, the authors define the Sharpening Tax. For an evaluation budget K, they first compute a raw area quantity: the cumulative performance recovered by additional parallel rollouts up to K, which is the area under the pass@K curve between pass@1 and the ceiling. A larger area means extra test-time compute buys more. The tax itself is a calibrated version of the loss in that scalability after post-training: it compares the base model's area against the post-trained model's area at matched budgets, so a tax near zero means post-training preserved test-time scalability, and a high tax means the post-trained model, for all its higher single-shot accuracy, can no longer benefit from sampling many rollouts. The metric has a practical property the paper emphasizes: it can be estimated from a handful of rollouts and predicts the full-budget tax well, which matters because computing pass@128 curves is expensive. The evaluation covers 14 base and post-trained model pairs from four model families, Gemma, Ministral, Qwen2.5, and Qwen3, on three agentic benchmarks that require multi-turn tool calling: BFCL v4 multi-turn, WebShop, and ACEBench, for 42 cases in total. The benchmarks were chosen so that both base and post-trained models reach non-trivial success within budget, because pass@K curves that stay near zero for both policies reveal little about sharpening.

The results split by model scale, and that split is the finding worth internalizing. The tax is prevalent in most of the 42 settings, but its sign depends on where the model sits. For smaller models, post-training raises the performance floor, pass@1, with little coverage to lose, because the base model was already sampling from a low-coverage regime where sharpening mostly helps. For larger models, post-training quickly lowers the performance ceiling, pass@K, because the base model had a broad set of working solution paths and RL collapses them. The paper draws a practical conclusion from this: a user running a large model with an abundant test-time budget who needs one good solution among many rollouts is better served by the base model than by its post-trained counterpart. That is a counterintuitive deployment recommendation, and it is the strongest claim in the paper. There is a related result on harnesses: the light inference harness that makes base models into capable agents dramatically improves both pass@1 and pass@32 on the benchmarks where base models struggle, while leaving already-manageable tasks unchanged. But the same harness hurts the post-trained models, which is consistent with the broader evidence that prompt engineering is not uniformly beneficial once post-training has narrowed the model's behavior.

The second contribution is the fix. The authors present posterior-tempered group sampling, or PTGS, a plug-and-play Bayesian sampler that adapts the sampling temperature per prompt based on its estimated difficulty. The logic is direct: the sharpening problem is that a fixed temperature either wastes samples on prompts the model always solves, or fails to explore on prompts it never solves, so the sampler estimates each prompt's difficulty and sets the temperature accordingly, keeping exploration where it is productive. The paper shows the tax is not an artifact of how the open checkpoints happened to be post-trained, because it also appears under task-specific RL from scratch. On Sokoban with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, the base model scores a pass@1 of 20.7 and a pass@128 of 76.6. Plain PPO raises pass@1 to 46.5 but cuts pass@128 to 55.0, with a tax of 0.094. PPO with PTGS reaches 61.1 on pass@1, 69.7 on pass@128, and a tax of 0.081. GRPO with PTGS gets 39.1 on pass@1 but 72.5 on pass@128 with a tax of 0.025. FrozenLake shows the same pattern: the base model is 26.3 on pass@1 and 89.1 on pass@128, PPO drops coverage to 74.1 with a tax of 0.039, and PTGS variants hold coverage above 80 while raising or matching pass@1. So PTGS pays a smaller tax than the fixed-temperature baseline, solving more tasks under repeated sampling while also improving single-shot accuracy, in both agentic environments tested.

The honest caveats are several. First, the "post-trained" models are open checkpoints whose recipes are not fully disclosed, and the authors are explicit about this: the Instruct variants may mix supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization, and RL with verifiable rewards in unknown proportions. The sharpening tax is therefore a property of the checkpoint pairs they chose, and while the from-scratch Sokoban and FrozenLake results show the phenomenon reproduces under controlled RL, the 42-case study's specific magnitudes are tied to those particular recipes. Second, the claim that base models are better agents with a sufficient budget is a claim about test-time compute budgets that many deployments simply do not have: if you can afford 128 rollouts per task, the base model can win on coverage, but most production systems run one or a handful of attempts, where pass@1 is what matters and the post-trained model is ahead. The paper's own scale analysis supports reading the result as budget-conditional rather than as a blanket indictment of post-training. Third, the harness results cut both ways: the same harness that unlocks base-model agency hurts the post-trained models, which raises the question of how much of the base model's apparent competence is the harness and how much is the model, since the two are entangled in the reported numbers.

Verdict: this is the clearest treatment yet of the sharpening hypothesis on agentic tasks, with a diagnostic metric that is cheap to estimate, a scale-dependent breakdown that avoids overclaiming, and a sampler that measurably reduces the tax under controlled RL. The deployment recommendation to sometimes prefer the base model with a big budget is counterintuitive and worth testing in your own stack, but it is budget-conditional, and the open-checkpoint caveat means the effect sizes should be treated as indicative rather than as properties of a specific post-training recipe.

The practical takeaway is about how to evaluate your own post-trained checkpoints. If you deploy a model behind a best-of-K or rejection-sampling harness, the pass@1 number on a leaderboard is not the number that matters, and this paper gives you a concrete protocol for measuring the one that does. Compute the pass@K area up to a fixed K, normalize against the best achievable area, and do it for both the base and the post-trained checkpoint on the same agentic tasks. If the post-trained model loses area, you are paying the sharpening tax, and the fix is not to train harder but to keep the posterior broad enough that repeated sampling still pays off.
