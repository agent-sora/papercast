DiffusionART: Efficient, Stable and Scalable Reinforcement of Diffusion Models, from Zhenglin Wan, Jiayu Chen, and Zhengfeiyang Zhang at the HPC-AI Lab of the National University of Singapore. The paper is about online reinforcement learning for visual diffusion and flow-matching models. These models are trained to fit the data distribution by regressing noise or velocity targets, not to optimize anything downstream like visual quality or human preference, so reward-guided fine-tuning is central to getting them to produce good images and video. Online diffusion reinforcement learning is the harder variant, because unlike differentiable reward-model optimization or offline preference learning, it evaluates clean samples from the evolving generator and can use a black-box reward. The catch is credit assignment. The reward only arrives after a complete output is generated, so a single costly, output-level signal has to be translated into effective updates across many denoising states. That is the problem the two existing families of methods both fail to solve well, and the paper starts from a diagnosis of why.
The diagnosis is the paper's real contribution. The authors show that NFT-style forward-process training, the method behind DiffusionNFT, is unstable because its objective can introduce an implicit forward-reverse self-consistency force that is not reliably canceled across states. The empirical signature is a puzzle: training on only the positive reward branch, or only the negative branch, each collapse, but joint positive-and-negative training learns fine. That is surprising because the ideal positive and negative guidance directions are collinear, so naively either branch alone should be enough. The resolution is that the self-consistency force is only partially canceled in the joint case, and the EMA averaging people add to stabilize NFT actually works through a restoration effect, not through off-policy rollouts as usually assumed. EMA also needs per-task tuning, which is exactly the fragility they want to remove.
Their fix, DiffusionART, stands for Diffusion Reinforcement with Aggregation-First Reward Tilting. Instead of letting each transition carry its own noise, it aggregates each prompt's rewards into a single shared reward-improving direction for the reverse-process states, which removes the consistency force by construction. Then it adds a trust-region-masked second update per rollout: the first update starts from the current policy where restoration is zero, and after the policy moves, the second update combines the reward guidance with a restoration pull back toward the starting point, but only where a trust-region mask permits it. This gives drift-adaptive restoration without any EMA tuning, and it needs no differentiable reward model. There is also a pairwise variant, DiffusionART-Pairwise, for reward models that only compare outputs.
The method name, Diffusion Reinforcement with Aggregation-First Reward Tilting, encodes the central design choice, and it is the aggregation that does most of the work. In standard policy-gradient RL for diffusion, each denoising transition carries its own reward signal, and the variance across those signals is what makes the forward-reverse consistency problem show up. DiffusionART instead aggregates all of a prompt's rewards into a single shared direction before applying the update to the reverse-process states, so every state in that prompt's trajectory is pushed along the same reward-improving vector rather than along a noisy per-transition one. That removes the consistency force by construction rather than by averaging it out afterward, which is why the method does not need the per-task EMA lag that DiffusionNFT requires. The trust-region-masked second update is the stability mechanism, and it is deliberately conservative. The first update starts from the current policy where the restoration term is exactly zero, so the model moves in the reward direction. After that move, the second update re-evaluates and pulls the policy partway back toward the starting point, but only in the regions of state-space where a trust-region mask says the move is trustworthy. The mask is what adapts the restoration to the local drift, so regions where the policy moved too far get a stronger pull-back and regions where it moved safely get less. There is also a pairwise variant, DiffusionART-Pairwise, for the common case where the reward model only ranks outputs against each other rather than assigning an absolute score, which covers a large fraction of real preference models.
The results are the strongest part of the paper. On image generation with SD3.5-M, DiffusionART matches or beats the baselines without per-task EMA tuning, and where DiffusionNFT with its on-policy setting suffers a severe collapse under multi-reward optimization, DiffusionART stays stable. On text-to-video with HunyuanVideo-13B, using equally weighted visual and motion quality rewards, the efficiency claim is large: DiffusionART reaches the late-stage mean reward levels of DiffusionNFT and Flow-GRPO-Fast with twelve point zero two times and eleven point seven two times fewer GPU hours respectively, while actually achieving twenty eight point eight percent and fifty three percent higher mean training rewards. On the motion quality target, DiffusionART needs nineteen steps and forty two point nine GPU hours where DiffusionNFT needs thirty three steps and one hundred sixty five point six GPU hours, a roughly one point seven times saving just on that component. The ablations isolate the two design choices. The single-update variant learns quickly but gradually deteriorates, while the two-update variant, which adds the restoration step, provides long-term stability, and DiffusionNFT with a carefully tuned restoration weight is still needed to match it. Removing only the trust-region mask causes a drop around step two hundred that partially recovers, and the mask measurably tames the gradient norm: over the first three hundred training steps the maximum first-update gradient norm is one point nine six with masking versus three point three three without it.
The reward setup is worth being precise about, because it bounds what the efficiency numbers mean. For image generation on SD3.5-M the rewards are PickScore, CLIPScore, and HPS, the standard model-based image quality and preference proxies, and the evaluation is on GenEval, which scores compositional correctness of the generated images against the prompt. For the text-to-video result on HunyuanVideo-13B, the reward is VideoReward, split into a visual-quality component and a motion-quality component, and the paper runs both separately and then jointly with equally weighted visual-to-motion ratios. The joint case is where the baselines get fragile. DiffusionNFT, which needs its EMA lag tuned per task, degrades visibly under the multi-reward setting, and the paper's point is that the forward-reverse consistency force it diagnosed is what makes that degradation happen when several rewards are optimized at once. The GPU-hour comparison is done at matched late-stage reward levels, not at matched step counts, which is the fair way to compare two methods that converge at different rates, and it is the reason the eleven and twelve times multipliers are the numbers to hold the authors to rather than the raw step counts. The fact that DiffusionART also reports higher mean training reward at those matched levels, twenty eight point eight and fifty three percent higher, is the second axis: it is not just reaching the same reward faster, it is reaching a higher reward for the same budget.
The skeptical read. First, the headline multipliers are relative to specific baseline configurations, and the baselines include a flow-matching method that requires an ODE-to-SDE conversion for tractable likelihoods, so part of the efficiency gap is the overhead of that conversion rather than a fundamental limit. Second, the two-update design doubles the per-rollout optimizer work, and the paper is honest that it trades some reward-improvement speed for stability, so the GPU-hour comparison is only clean at matched reward levels. Third, the rewards are model-based proxies, PickScore and CLIPScore and HPS for images and VideoReward for video, and the same reward hacking concerns that plague any reward-guided post-training apply here; a method that optimizes a proxy faster is also a method that can overfit it faster. Bottom line: the diagnosis of the forward-reverse self-consistency force is the durable insight, and it directly explains a real failure mode in NFT-style diffusion post-training. The trust-region-masked second update is a clean, tuning-free way to get stability, and the efficiency numbers on a thirteen-billion video model are credible and useful. I would want the gains checked on a second video model and against a better-tuned baseline before calling the efficiency gap robust.
