Title: Intern-Decision: Efficient Multimodal Decision Making with Autoregressive Masked Language Modeling
Authors: Tong Zhu, Zhe Xu, Zefeng He
Labs: Shanghai AI Laboratory; Shanghai Jiao Tong University
Arxiv: 2610.22131
Day: 2026-10-18

The paper is Intern-Decision, from Tong Zhu, Zhe Xu, and Zefeng He, from Shanghai AI Laboratory and Shanghai Jiao Tong University. The system is a family of multimodal decision models at point eight billion, two billion, and four billion parameters, and the headline result is that the four billion model reaches a ninety percent unweighted average accuracy across seven evaluation suites, beating a much larger general-purpose model, Jev at version one thirteen zero, which sits at eighty-eight and three quarter percent, while running at forty-four milliseconds mean latency for a three field decision on a single RTX four thousand ninety GPU. The whole premise is that interactive agents spend a huge fraction of their time on small, structured choices, picking an action from a fixed set, classifying a label, choosing an option in a menu, and that generating a free-form sentence for each of those choices is both slow and a mess to parse, so you want a model whose job is only to return a structured choice with a confidence.

The core technical contribution is the objective. Instead of ordinary causal language modeling, Intern-Decision uses an autoregressive masked language modeling objective. The model is given a complete assistant JSON skeleton, the structure of the answer with a field for each thing that needs deciding, and inside that skeleton there is one decision placeholder per field. At training time the ground-truth symbols for those fields are shown only in supervision, never as input, and at inference time all the fields are evaluated in a single causal forward pass using the backbone's native language model head. That is the clever part, and it is why the latency is what it is. A normal small language model answering a multi-field question would have to decode token by token, field by field, and each field is a separate autoregressive pass. Intern-Decision instead scores the candidate answers at the placeholder positions in one shot, like a masked model filling in the blanks, but it does it with a causal decoder and its existing head, so it gets the parallelism of masked evaluation without needing a separate encoder. The model takes text and optionally an image, which is the multimodal part. A screenshot, a game frame, a browser page, and it has to make a decision about it, and the decision comes back as structured symbols with probabilities.

The calibration story is worth calling out because it is usually the weakest part of these compact models and here it is actually handled. On a separate pilot of ninety-six cases from a known distribution, temperature scaling reduces the expected multiclass Brier score from point six two eight to point five five zero and the expected calibration error from point two one three to point zero eight nine. That is a big calibration fix for a small adjustment, and it matters for the use case the paper is really aiming at, which is confidence-based handoff. The demonstration of that handoff is the most practically interesting result in the paper. On one hundred eleven public items from Jevbench Hard, the model alone gets eighty-two right, seventy-three and a half percent, but when you route the cases where the model's confidence is low up to a larger frontier model, the combined system gets ninety-seven right, eighty-seven and three quarter percent, by sending forty-two of the items upward. That is a clean, quantified version of the small model does the easy work and escalates the hard work pattern, and it is the argument that these decision models are a component of a larger agent system rather than a replacement for the big model.

The latency numbers are the reason the architecture matters, and they are concrete. A fixed two eighty nine token request with three fields on one consumer grade GPU gives forty-four and a half milliseconds mean latency for the four billion model and about thirty-three to thirty-four milliseconds for the smaller ones. That is fast enough to sit inside a real-time loop, a game, an interactive interface, a browser agent, and the paper backs it up with qualitative demonstrations in exactly those settings, a game, a visual interface, and a browser. The design intent is that a decision like this, which is the bottleneck in an interactive agent not because it is hard but because the round trip to a large model is slow, gets answered locally in a few dozen milliseconds with a confidence attached.

The ablations and the training details reinforce the design. The masked objective is applied to a causal backbone, not a retrained encoder, which means the decision head is the model's existing language model head, so there is no separate classification layer to train and no extra output space to align. The placeholders are placed in a complete JSON skeleton rather than a free-form prompt, which gives the model a fixed contract for what to fill in, and that is what makes the single-pass scoring well defined. The image input path matters for the demonstrations, because a game frame or a browser screenshot is the whole state the decision depends on, and a point eight billion model having to look at pixels and emit a structured action in tens of milliseconds is the actual capability being demonstrated, not just the text side.

What I would trust is the masked-decision-in-one-forward-pass idea, because it is a genuinely clean solution to a real latency problem, and the confidence-based handoff result is the kind of evidence that shows the model is useful as a system component, not just a benchmark entry. The calibration fix being so effective, expected calibration error dropping from point two one three to point zero eight nine with temperature scaling, suggests the probabilities are well behaved enough to actually route on, which is the property that makes the handoff result believable rather than cherry-picked. The seven suite evaluation and the comparison to a named, real baseline, Jev, is a more honest setup than many papers in this space manage, because you can see what it is beating and how close the gap is, two and a half points, which is small.

What I would re-verify is the scope of the win and the generality of the decision space. The unweighted average across seven suites is a useful number, but unweighted averages hide which suites are being won and which are being lost, and I would want the per-suite table to see whether the two and a half point lead over Jev is uniform or concentrated in a couple of easier suites where a small model has a structural advantage. The calibration pilot is on a known distribution, which is the honest setup, but that is also the setting where calibration is easiest, and I would want to see the handoff numbers on a distribution the model has not seen, because routing on confidence is only as good as the confidence, and out of distribution is where small model confidences go wrong. The demonstrations are qualitative, which is fine for a first look, but the interactive agent claim, that this is a practical component, really rests on the handoff result and the latency, and both of those are on the paper's own chosen benchmarks and its own chosen routing threshold, so I would want an independent replication of the routing tradeoff before wiring one of these into a real system.

Two-sentence verdict: Intern-Decision is a tidy, well-motivated answer to the latency problem of small structured decisions in interactive agents, using autoregressive masked language modeling to score multi-field choices in a single forward pass with a native head, and the confidence-based handoff result, jumping from seventy-four to eighty-seven percent by escalating the low-confidence items, is the evidence that the model works as a system component. The two and a half point accuracy lead over a real baseline at consumer GPU latency is a fair and honest claim, but the unweighted average and the in-distribution calibration pilot are the soft spots, and I would want the per-suite breakdown and an out-of-distribution routing test before trusting the handoff numbers.
