Title: Learning What Actions Change: Action Effect Matching for Dynamics-Faithful World Models
Authors: John Won, Yonghoon Dong, Jinwoo Shin
Labs: KAIST; RLWRLD
Arxiv: 2611.25980
Day: 2026-11-20
Upvotes: 9

Learning What Actions Change, by John Won, Yonghoon Dong, and Jinwoo Shin, at KAIST and RLWRLD, introduces AFFECT, an auxiliary training objective for robotics world models that makes them sensitive to how small changes in an action change the future observation. The problem is that current world models, which are built on video diffusion foundation models and generate photorealistic, temporally coherent rollouts, are bad at a specific and important thing: they produce visually plausible videos that ignore small differences in the action, so two actions with genuinely different real-world consequences get similar predicted futures. That makes the world model unreliable as a policy evaluator, because it cannot distinguish a grasping action from a missing one, and it misleads a planner into picking the wrong action. The paper's argument is that action sensitivity is a distributional property, and the model should be trained to capture how the distribution of future observations shifts as the action varies, not just to reproduce a single recorded future with high visual fidelity.

The prior-art framing is worth being precise about. A robotics world model takes an observation and a sequence of actions and generates the future video. The standard training objective is to match the generated video to the recorded video from the real execution, and that objective rewards visual realism, which is why the models have gotten so good at producing convincing footage. But the objective has a blind spot: if the training data mostly contains one action per state, the model never sees what would have happened with a slightly different action, so it learns to produce the one future it was shown, and it generalizes that same future to nearby actions. The consequence is that the model is a good interpolator of the recorded data and a bad counterfactual reasoner, and counterfactual reasoning is exactly what policy evaluation and model-based planning need, because a planner is asking what would happen if I did this instead.

The method is the core contribution, and it is an elegant data-level fix. The key move is to inject temporally correlated noise into the actions of a policy trained on task-agnostic human teleoperation, which the paper calls play data, where an operator interacts with objects freely without a predefined task. By perturbing the play policy's actions with structured noise, the authors create pairs of action sequences that are nearly identical but differ in a controlled way, and the corresponding recorded futures diverge in a way that reflects real dynamics. The world model is then trained with the AFFECT objective so that its generated videos respond to this injected action noise the same way the recorded videos do, without requiring repeated executions from the same initial state, which would be expensive and physically hard to reproduce exactly. The temporally correlated noise matters because random per-step noise would not capture how a small deviation early in a trajectory compounds over time, which is the regime that matters for manipulation, where an early reach error determines whether the gripper grasps, misses, or pushes the object away. There is also an implementation detail that makes AFFECT cheap to add to an existing world model: it operates on features from frozen encoders, so it is an auxiliary loss on top of the representation the world model already computes, not a change to the encoder or the video decoder. That means you can bolt AFFECT onto a pre-trained video-diffusion world model without retraining the heavy components, which is what makes it a drop-in objective rather than a new model.

The results are on three setups: an OGBench simulation, and two real-robot configurations, DROID and ALOHA 2. The headline metric on DROID is the Pearson correlation between the policy success rates predicted by the world model and the success rates measured on the real robot, and AFFECT raises that correlation from 0.47 to 0.79, which is a large improvement in faithfulness, because a world model that ranks policies with a 0.47 correlation is barely better than a coin flip at telling you which policy will succeed, while 0.79 is a usable evaluator. Alongside that, the Fréchet video distance, which measures visual quality, drops by up to 23 percent, so the faithfulness gain does not come at the cost of visual realism, which is a nice result because the usual concern with an auxiliary objective is that it degrades the primary visual quality. The gains also carry over to planning: selecting actions using an AFFECT-trained world model improves task success by up to 8.3 percent, which is the downstream payoff, the reason to care about action sensitivity in the first place.

The fact that the result appears across three different setups is part of the evidence that this is a property of the objective rather than of one dataset. OGBench is the simulation, where you have clean access to the ground-truth dynamics and can measure the faithfulness directly, and it is where the cleanest before-after comparison lives. DROID is the large-scale real-robot manipulation dataset, and it is where the headline 0.47 to 0.79 correlation is measured, because it has enough real rollouts to make the correlation between predicted and measured success rates statistically meaningful. ALOHA 2 is the bimanual setup, which is the harder configuration because two arms acting in the same workspace means the action perturbations have more interacting effects to get right, and it is the setup that most directly tests whether the method generalizes beyond a single-arm gripper. Seeing the faithfulness gain hold across a simulation, a single-arm real dataset, and a bimanual setup is what pushes this from a one-dataset trick toward a general property of the training objective.

The skeptical reading. First, the 0.47 to 0.79 correlation gain is the headline, but it is measured on DROID, which is a specific dataset with a specific distribution of tasks and objects, and the correlation is between the world model's ranking of a set of policies and the real robot's ranking, so it is a ranking faithfulness metric, and a high correlation can hold even if the absolute success rates are off, which matters if you are using the world model to estimate how good a policy is rather than just which of two is better. Second, the method depends on play data from a teleoperated policy, and the injected noise is the source of the counterfactual signal, so the quality of the learned action sensitivity is bounded by how well the noise distribution covers the action perturbations that actually matter at evaluation time, and if the evaluation actions explore a region the noise never touched, the faithfulness will degrade. Third, the 8.3 percent planning gain is an upper bound reported as up to, and the per-task breakdown matters, because a planning method that helps on some tasks and hurts on others is not a drop-in improvement, and you would want the per-task numbers before wiring an AFFECT-trained world model into a planner.

For implementation, the reusable idea is the action-perturbation data augmentation, injecting temporally correlated noise into a play policy's actions to create counterfactual action-future pairs, and the AFFECT objective that trains the world model to match the response to that noise. If you are building a robotics world model and finding that it is visually great but a poor policy evaluator, this is the specific fix to try, and the thing to check is the Pearson correlation between your world model's predicted success rates and your real-robot success rates on a held-out set of policies, because that is the metric that tells you whether the world model is actually faithful to dynamics rather than just to the video. The OGBench and DROID setups give you the reference configurations to reproduce against.

To summarize the bottom line. AFFECT is a clean and well-motivated auxiliary objective that makes robotics world models sensitive to action perturbations, and it delivers a large faithfulness gain, lifting the DROID success-rate correlation from 0.47 to 0.79, with a downstream planning improvement of up to 8.3 percent and no loss in visual quality. Its limits are the dependence on play-data noise coverage, the ranking-nature of the headline correlation, and the upper-bound framing of the planning gain, so the right validation before adoption is a per-task faithfulness check on your own policy set. If your world model looks great but evaluates policies poorly, this is the fix to reach for.
