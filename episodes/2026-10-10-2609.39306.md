"ReSAIL," by Shengjie Jin, Hengbo Xu, and Zelong Sun, from the Gaoling School of Artificial Intelligence at Renmin University of China. This paper tackles a specific and underappreciated failure mode in the recursive self-improvement literature: when you iterate self-distillation across deployment cycles, the model can get worse. Not plateau, worse. The authors demonstrate this with two existing methods, SDPO and OEL, and the fix they propose is a plug-in augmentation that addresses two distinct questions about what the distillation supervision actually contains.

The setup is worth stating precisely, because the failure mode is subtle. An LLM agent deploys in an environment, generating trajectories of actions, observations, and outcomes. Those trajectories are fed back into training, and the model is updated. The updated model deploys again, generates new trajectories, and the cycle repeats. This is the recursive self-improvement loop, and the premise is that each cycle should produce a better agent. The specific variant the paper addresses is self-distillation with privileged information. During training, the model has access to information it will not have at deployment time, things like the true state of the environment, the task goal spelled out, or intermediate annotations. The student is trained to reproduce the teacher's behavior conditioned on that privileged information, but to do so without actually seeing the privileged information at inference. The hope is that the student internalizes the behavior that privileged information would have produced, and then generalizes it to deployment where the information is absent.

The collapse. The authors run SDPO and OEL, two established iterative self-distillation methods, on ALFWorld, a text-based interactive environment, with Qwen3-4B. They track two quantities across three deployment cycles. The first is deployment success rate, how well the model does when it acts without privileged information, which is the quantity that matters for the actual use case. The second is privileged-conditioned success rate, how well the model does when it is given the privileged information and the tasks are held fixed across cycles. This second quantity is the one that reveals the pathology. Both methods show declining privileged-conditioned success over successive cycles, with the same tasks and the same privileged information held constant. The model is losing the very behavior it is supposed to be distilling. Each cycle, the updated student becomes the teacher for the next cycle, and if the student has drifted away from the privileged-conditioned behavior, the next teacher is weaker, and the next student distills from a weaker source. The collapse is a compounding error, and it shows up in the deployment numbers too, eventually.

The two questions the paper asks are the right ones. The first, which interaction steps should be prioritized for distillation, is a within-cycle question. Not all steps in a trajectory carry the same amount of useful supervision. The authors define a quantity called privileged-information sensitivity, the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the teacher's output distribution with privileged information and without it, computed at the same interaction step. A high-sensitivity step is one where the privileged information genuinely changes what the teacher would do. That is the step where the student has the most to learn, because the privileged-conditioned behavior is meaningfully different from the unprivileged behavior, and the distillation objective is trying to close that gap. A low-sensitivity step is one where the privileged information does not change the teacher's action much, and distilling from it is close to distilling from the model's own behavior, which is low-value supervision. The method, called sensitivity-guided selection, keeps the high-sensitivity steps and drops or down-weights the low-sensitivity ones, and then balances the resulting losses across trajectories so that one long trajectory with many high-sensitivity steps does not dominate a shorter one.

The second question, what happens when the student becomes the next teacher, is the across-cycles question, and it is the one that addresses the collapse directly. The base self-distillation objective trains the student only in its unprivileged mode. It does not explicitly constrain the student's privileged-conditioned output distribution to stay close to the teacher's. So when the student is promoted to teacher status for the next cycle, its privileged-conditioned behavior has drifted, and the next cycle's supervision is weaker. The authors' fix is a retention objective, which regularizes the student's privileged-view output distribution toward the frozen teacher's privileged-view distribution, at both the selected and the unselected steps. The frozen teacher is the original model, not the previous cycle's student, so the retention target does not compound. The student is being told, in addition to learning the unprivileged behavior, to keep its privileged behavior close to a fixed reference. That is the load-bearing design choice, and it is what distinguishes this from simply adding a KL term between consecutive students.

The results. On ALFWorld and TextCraft, across multiple model scales, ReSAIL sustains gains over three deployment cycles when added to the self-distillation baselines. The average absolute gain in final-cycle success rate is twenty two and a half percent over the self-distillation baseline, which is a substantial number for a method that is described as a plug-in rather than a new training paradigm. The result that ties the two modules together is the one in Figure 1 of the paper: without ReSAIL, both SDPO and OEL show their privileged-conditioned success declining over the three cycles, and that decline is what the retention module is built to stop. With ReSAIL added, the privileged-conditioned trajectory flattens or rises, and the deployment success that follows from it is higher in every cycle. The two modules are doing different jobs and the paper is careful to show both: the selection module improves the first cycle by spending the student's compute on the informative steps, and the retention module is what keeps the later cycles from degrading, which is the part that addresses the collapse the paper sets out to fix. The efficiency analysis on Qwen3-4B on ALFWorld is also worth noting. The sensitivity-guided selection component reduces the student optimization cost by approximately seventy four percent, because the student is trained on a subset of the interaction steps rather than all of them, and improves first-cycle task success in the process. The full ReSAIL configuration, which retains privileged-view predictions at all steps, costs roughly one point two three times the baseline compute, which is a reasonable price for the stability it buys.

There is also a transfer result that I find interesting. The authors apply the sensitivity-guided selection as a static offline data filter for a multimodal GUI agent on AITZ. They compute the PI sensitivity with the initial model, retain the global top eighty percent of interaction steps, fix that subset, and train. The filtered training improves final action accuracy by one point zero six percentage points over unfiltered training, with the same update budget. That is a small number, but the point is that the sensitivity score is a usable criterion for filtering offline agent trajectories without the full iterative machinery, which is a practical use case for teams that have a fixed corpus of trajectories and want to spend their training budget on the informative steps.

The honest limits. The evaluation is on two text-based agent environments and one multimodal GUI benchmark, all with relatively small models. The privileged information in these settings is well-defined and structured, which makes the sensitivity computation clean. The transfer to settings where the privileged information is harder to specify, like a real deployment where the ground-truth state is not available in a form the model can consume during training, is not addressed. And the method is validated over three cycles, which is enough to demonstrate that the collapse is mitigated but not enough to establish that the loop can be sustained indefinitely, which is the stronger claim the recursive self-improvement literature would like to make. There is also a practical dependency: the method assumes the privileged information is available at training time even when it is not at deployment, which is the same assumption the whole self-distillation line makes, and the value of the fix is bounded by how well that assumption holds in the setting you are applying it to.

My verdict is that this is a careful, well-motivated fix for a real pathology, and the privileged retention objective targeting a frozen reference rather than a moving student is the design choice that actually prevents the compounding. If you are running iterative self-distillation loops and seeing performance drift, this is the first paper I would reach for.
