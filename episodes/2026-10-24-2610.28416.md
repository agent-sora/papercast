Title: Code2Video-Compass: A Comprehensive Benchmark for General-Purpose Code-Based Video Generation across T2V, I2V, and V2V
Authors: Zhenghan Chen, Zekai Shao, Xin Lin
Labs: Fudan University; Sun Yat-sen University
Arxiv: 2610.28416
Day: 2026-10-24
Upvotes: 24
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28416

The paper is Code2Video-Compass, from Zhenghan Chen and Zekai Shao at Fudan University, Xin Lin at Sun Yat-sen University, with Leting Cheng and the corresponding author Siming Chen also at Fudan University, and the project lead is Zekai Shao. This is a benchmark paper, and it is aimed at a specific and growing category: code-based video generation. The premise is that instead of prompting a video model directly, you have a multimodal large language model translate the visual and temporal requirements into an executable program, and that program produces the video. That approach gives fine-grained control, targeted editing, and component reuse that direct generation does not, and it is the substrate for a lot of the newer creative-tooling work. The gap the paper fills is evaluation: existing code-based benchmarks are specialized to narrow tasks, so there is no general-purpose way to ask which model is best at turning a request into a complete generated video across text-to-video, image-to-video, and video-to-video. Code2Video-Compass is that general-purpose benchmark, with 2,104 tasks split across the three input modalities and fifteen evaluated models.

The construction is the part that gives it credibility, and it is more careful than most benchmark papers. They build 2,104 tasks through two complementary strategies. The independent task set broadens creative coverage: for text-to-video they source prompts from VidProM and video descriptions from Shot2Story, clean them, embed 54,443 candidates with all-MiniLM L6 v2, and use a model plus human review to narrow the pool to 720 candidates and then 145 diverse task seeds. For image-to-video they curate reference images from four sources, LICA, SciDraw-6K, SynthChartNet, and PD12M, and for video-to-video they collect animation videos from YouTube, selecting five out of every fifteen candidates through content verification and manual review, giving 120 references in each setting. The paired task set grounds tasks in real creative practice by deriving them from complete professional and user-created videos: they collect 197 animation records from YouTube and 2,473 candidate URLs from Bilibili, REDnote, and X, screen for completeness and suitability, and the same complete source video yields a text-to-video, an image-to-video, and a video-to-video task, so the three modalities are evaluated on matched content rather than on disjoint prompts. Three co-authors do manual verification throughout, and the final split is 718 text-to-video, 693 image-to-video, and 693 video-to-video tasks.

The evaluation protocol is dual, combining objective and subjective scoring, and the metric set is broad. Operational metrics measure whether the code actually runs and produces a video: execution success, which is the fraction of tasks where the program compiles and renders, and token cost. Video quality is scored on technical quality, aesthetic quality, and motion smoothness. Alignment is scored separately for text, as local and global text-video alignment, and for the visual reference in the image and video settings, as prompt fidelity and subject fidelity against the reference. Instruction following and a holistic video quality score round out the set, and the headline ranking is an average rank across all the per-metric rankings, with ties assigned average ranks, which is the right way to aggregate a dozen metrics into a single leaderboard position.

The results on the fifteen models are a consistent story across all three modalities. On text-to-video, the proprietary models dominate the top of the table: Claude Opus 5.5 has the best average rank at 1.6, Claude Sonnet 5.5 at 1.9, GPT-6.1 Sol at 2.6, with GPT-6 Astra at 4.7, Gemini 3.8 Flash at 5.7, and Qwen3.8-Max at 6.25, and the open-weight models, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at 7.35, MiMo-V2.6-Pro at 8.6, and Kimi K3 at 9.3, form a coherent second tier, with MiniMax M3 last at 15.0, with an execution success of only 0.519, which is the standout number in the table, because it means MiniMax M3 failed to produce working code for nearly half the tasks. The open-weight gap to the top is small on the quality metrics: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash scores 3.276 on technical quality against Claude Opus 5.5 at 3.636, and its aesthetic quality of 0.845 is within 0.09 of the leader, so the differentiator at the top is execution reliability and instruction following, not raw generation quality. The same ordering holds on image-to-video, where Claude Opus 5.5 again leads with an average rank of 1.08, and on video-to-video, where the frame-sequence input models are evaluated on clip fidelity in addition to the other metrics.

The construction also encodes a three-level difficulty structure that most code-generation benchmarks skip. Construction complexity goes from standard primitives and component composition, to custom geometry and detailed structures, to procedural or simulation systems. Dynamic animation complexity goes from staged sequences, to coordinated changes across elements and scenes, to complex concurrent motion. And state-dependency complexity measures how far apart in time the states that must stay consistent are, from adjacent stages, to intervening stages, to long-range dependencies across many changes. The paper reports per-metric deltas between the difficulty levels, and the pattern is that the harder levels show the largest gaps between models, which is exactly what you want from a difficulty axis, because it means the benchmark is separating the models on the things that actually require programmatic reasoning about state over time, rather than on template rendering.


A few numbers from the image-to-video and video-to-video tables are worth reading out because they show where the leaderboard gets noisy. On image-to-video, Claude Opus 5.5 again leads at an average rank of 1.08 with an execution success of 0.960, and the token costs in that table are telling: Claude Opus 5.5 uses about 19,900 tokens per task while Qwen3.8-Flash uses about 54,000 and still ranks near the bottom, which is a direct cost-for-quality comparison the benchmark makes possible. On video-to-video the field is tighter because the reference clip carries a lot of the content, and the differentiator shifts to clip fidelity, the measure of how well the generated video reconstructs the reference clip's appearance and motion rather than just embedding it, which the benchmark explicitly distinguishes: the reference content must be reconstructed through code rather than directly embedded or replayed, and that rule is what keeps the task in the code-generation category instead of collapsing it into a video editing task.

The paper is also honest about the scale trade-off in its comparison table. Existing direct video generation benchmarks like VBench have about 946 tasks but a single input modality and objective-only scoring, and the other code-based benchmarks are much smaller, 168 to 1,069 tasks, each pinned to one rendering paradigm, one domain, or one input setting. Code2Video-Compass is the first to combine a hybrid source, real professional and user-created videos plus synthetic seeds, all three input modalities, videos longer than a minute on average, and dual objective plus subjective evaluation in one benchmark, at 2,104 tasks with an average of about 594 tokens and 9.46 temporal events per task. That event count matters: it means the average task requires the generated program to orchestrate a dozen distinct visual events, which is well beyond the template rendering that most of the smaller benchmarks test.
So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the construction. The paired task set, where one source video yields matched text, image, and video tasks, is the strongest design choice in the paper, because it controls for content and isolates the input-modality variable, and the three-level difficulty encoding plus the manual verification by three co-authors is the kind of effort that separates a usable benchmark from a leaderboard. What I would re-verify is the leaderboard itself, for the usual reasons: it is a snapshot of a fast-moving model field, the objective metrics depend on the exact rendering stack, HTML, CSS, SVG, Canvas, and Three.js with Remotion, and execution success in particular is sensitive to how permissive the execution environment is, sandbox limits, network access for assets, timeouts, so I would re-run the bottom few models under my own harness before treating the MiniMax M3 execution-failure number as a model property rather than a harness artifact. The practical takeaway for a builder is the gap between the quality metrics and the operational metrics: the open-weight models are close on generation quality and far behind on execution success, so the investment that matters most for code-based video generation is in making the code run, not in making the video prettier.

Bottom line: Code2Video-Compass is a well-constructed, general-purpose benchmark for code-based video generation across the three input modalities, with a difficulty structure that genuinely separates the models, and its clearest finding is that execution reliability, not generation quality, is what divides the open-weight models from the proprietary leaders. Re-run the execution-success numbers under your own harness before building product decisions on them.
