Intrinsic Distribution Matching, from Deyuan Liu, Yansen Han, and Kaiwen Zheng at Inclusion AI, Westlake University, and Tsinghua University, is a training paper for one-step and few-step text-to-image generation. The setting is distribution matching distillation, where you train a fast generator to match a reference distribution, and the specific question is whether you can add an explicit constraint on the statistics of what the generator produces, and get it from the generator itself rather than from an external model. The short answer is yes, and the headline numbers, on long-text rendering at two network evaluations, are among the best in their comparison set.

Let me set up the problem, because the terminology matters here. A standard diffusion or flow-matching model turns noise into an image by running the network many times, and each run costs real compute. One-step generation learns a single direct map from noise to image, one network evaluation, and the hard part is rendering the details, especially long text, where every character has to come out right with no second chances. Distribution matching distillation is a recipe for training that fast generator. The reference score, the local direction of increasing log density for the target distribution, comes from a frozen teacher. The score of the generator's current distribution has to be estimated, and the classic DMD setup learns a separate fake-score network just for that, which has to keep up as the generator changes. Recent work, TwinFlow and APEX, removed the separate network by using two internally separated velocity branches of one generator. This paper adopts that internal construction, and then asks the extra question.

The motivation comes from a decomposition, and it is the cleanest part of the paper. The reverse-KL field, the direction you move the generated distribution to reduce its divergence from the reference, can be split exactly into two pieces. One piece is a Gaussian moment component that depends only on the mean and the covariance. The other piece is a non-Gaussian residual, all the score structure beyond the Gaussian. The key fact is that two distributions can have identical means and covariances and still be different distributions, which means matching moments alone is not enough to make the full field vanish. So the right move is not to replace the learned score supervision with a moment constraint, it is to keep the full DMD correction and add a moment constraint on top, because the learned score estimate and a finite training budget can leave statistics misaligned that an explicit objective would pin down.

That is the whole method, and it is deliberately minimal. The first loss is the internal DMD loss, the full score-difference correction, regressing velocity and the clean-sample endpoint to detached targets built from the internal branch difference. The way the internal branch trick works is worth a sentence. The reference velocity branch and the model velocity branch are separated by negating the text condition vector, so the same network, run with the condition and with its sign flipped, gives you the two velocities whose difference, scaled by a function of the noise level, is the score difference. The correction is then re-noised and detached to build the regression targets, so the score supervision never sees a live gradient through the moving-average copy. The second loss matches statistics in feature space. The feature map is the generator's own exponential moving average copy, mean-pooled over its last-layer hidden activations, so you get one feature vector per sample, and you do it for both the generated samples and the reference samples. You match the means and the per-coordinate standard deviations of those features. The paper points out, correctly, that this is the squared two-Wasserstein distance between two diagonal Gaussians, so the moment loss has a clean geometric reading. The notable design choice is that no external encoder is involved. Feature extraction and score estimation both come from the generator and its moving average, so there is no separate discriminator, no separate score model, no frozen teacher encoder sitting in the loop.

The experimental setup is where the skepticism has to live. They train both full-parameter and low-rank adaptation versions on two backbones, Z-Image-Turbo at 6 billion parameters and Qwen-Image at 20 billion. The main evaluation is LongText-Bench, the Chinese and English long-text rendering benchmark, at two network evaluations, which is the honest few-step regime. On Z-Image-Turbo they report 94.25 percent on the Chinese set and 90.20 percent on the English set, the highest among the methods they compare at that budget, and they attribute the gain of about 2.6 percentage points on Chinese and 0.3 on English over the Z-Image-Turbo-plus-plus baseline to the added moment loss. The ablations are controlled and shared, so the same training setting is used to isolate the moment loss, the choice of EMA features versus external encoders, and the regression form. On the encoder question, the generator's EMA features come out competitive with an external DINOv2 encoder across the five reported metrics, which is a meaningful result, because it means you can drop a separate large vision encoder from the training loop without paying for it in quality.

The efficiency side is reported on a single A100 in bfloat16, and it is worth reading because it frames what the quality numbers actually cost. At two network evaluations the compared few-step methods span a wide range of throughput and latency, with the distilled small models in the 2 to 6 samples per second range and the 20-billion-class distillations in the 3 per second range. IDM sits in that same cluster, which is the point of the comparison. It is not selling speed, it is selling quality at a speed that is already there, and the moment loss does not add a network evaluation to inference, only a feature readout to training. That keeps the deployment story clean.

Where I would push back is in the framing and the scale of the win. The gains on the English long-text set are small, a third of a point, and the whole evaluation is anchored on long-text rendering, which is the one setting where statistical alignment of text regions probably matters most. The system-level table compares efficiency and quality across a dozen few-step methods, but the IDM entries are at specific network evaluation counts and the comparison is not always like-for-like, some entries need different models for different budgets and some are marked as non-canonical operating points. The decomposition, while clean, is an analysis of what the field contains, not a proof that the moment loss is the unique way to exploit it. The paper is honest that the moment loss is an additional feature-space objective motivated by the decomposition, not a separate estimate of the sample-space Gaussian or residual, and that distinction is the right one to keep in mind. It is a useful inductive bias, not a theorem.

The practical takeaway is real even where the theory is suggestive rather than decisive. If you are distilling a large text-to-image model down to one or a few steps and you care about fine conditional detail, particularly text, this is a cheap thing to add. The moment loss costs you a feature readout from a copy you already maintain for training, and it removes the temptation to bolt on an external encoder. The honest reading is that the decomposition tells you moments are constrainable, the ablations tell you the constraint helps a little, and the long-text numbers tell you the help is largest where you most need it.

My verdict is that this is a solid, appropriately scoped systems contribution with one genuinely clean idea, the Gaussian-plus-residual decomposition of the reverse-KL field, doing real work to justify an otherwise incremental loss. The results are competitive and the design is elegant in its self-containment, but the headline is a narrow win on one benchmark family at one budget, so I would treat the moment matching as a sensible default to include rather than a breakthrough that changes the distillation landscape.
