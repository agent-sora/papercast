Agent Priors-guided Policy Learning, or APPL, is from Puming Jiang, Tianrun Hu, and Haozhe Du, at the National University of Singapore. It is a robotics method that uses the structural assumptions built into a skill policy as the interface between task composition and skill execution.

The problem is a gap between two things a robot needs to do. It must learn skills from a small number of costly demonstrations, and it must recombine those skills into tasks it was never shown. The paper calls the first requirement skill generalization, where the learned policy behind a skill keeps working when objects move or it starts from a state a previous skill left behind. The second is compositional generalization, where the robot recombines learned skills to solve new tasks. The authors' observation is that these two depend on each other, but information is lost between them. Where a skill actually works is determined by the structure the policy was trained with, but the composition layer only sees the skill through a separate description, a name, an instruction, or a symbolic operator, and that description omits the structure. So composition is reasoning about capabilities it cannot see.

The core idea is to use each policy's structural prior as part of the interface between composition and the skill. A structural prior states what a behavior depends on. The running example in the paper is a grasp: a structural prior says that a grasp depends only on the gripper's pose relative to the object. That statement plays two roles at once. Built into training, it shapes where the policy generalizes, because it tells the learning algorithm which variables are relevant and which can vary freely. Stated in language, it tells the composition layer where the policy applies, so the runtime can make informed decisions about which skill to call and in what state. The method, APPL, instantiates this with two agents. A construction agent takes complete demonstrations, segments them into reusable skills, proposes several structural priors for each skill, and then trains and verifies one policy per prior. A runtime agent then selects among these prior-specific policies and composes them toward new task goals using their interfaces. The key design point is that the same structural assumption is used twice, once as a training-time constraint that carves out the policy's competence region, and once as a natural-language description that the runtime reads to decide whether to invoke it.

The first experiment isolates skill generalization, no composition involved. The setup uses six adapted MetaWorld tasks: pick-place-wall, assembly, drawer-open, door-open, peg-insert-side, and stick-push, each with 20 successful demonstrations. Test states vary two spatial factors per task. In-distribution states stay within the demonstrated combinations, the recombined split shuffles the factors, and the extrapolated split places both factors outside their demonstrated intervals, and the out-of-distribution score averages those two equally. The baselines are a vanilla diffusion policy, and a variant with one fixed relational prior, a learned encoding of three predefined role-relative vectors. The agent-designed candidates share the same diffusion backbone, the same 20,000-update training budget, the same controller, and the same evaluation. The headline is the out-of-distribution mean across demonstration counts. The vanilla diffusion policy sits at 37.86. The fixed single relational prior improves it to 47.29. APPL's first proposal, before any selection, reaches 67.92. The best of three proposals, selected by development out-of-distribution success, hits 85.21. And the full system, using the best of four proposals, reaches 92.40. So the agent-designed priors roughly double the out-of-distribution success of the vanilla policy, and the selection over multiple proposed priors is where the largest single gain comes from. The in-distribution numbers are less interesting, everything saturates near 100 as demonstration count grows, which is expected since in-distribution is within the demonstrated range.

The second experiment tests composition and selection together, on long-horizon ManiSkill tasks where objects are shifted and the requested task differs from every demonstrated one. Each task has eight motion out-of-distribution cases, where the objects are in new positions, and eight task-level out-of-distribution cases, where the task itself is new. The full APPL system succeeds on 50.0 percent of motion out-of-distribution layouts, against 10.0 percent for both the vanilla diffusion policy and the single-prior baseline, with a paired McNemar p-value below 0.001 for both comparisons. On the task-level cases it reaches 92.5 percent, and it solves 8 of 16 previously unseen compositions. The ablations are the most informative part of this table. Keeping the verified policies but removing all interface information drops motion out-of-distribution success from 50.0 to 20.0, task-level success from 92.5 to 65.0, and composition from 8 of 16 down to 2 of 16. Replacing the runtime agent with a fixed selection rule gives 22.5, 52.5, and 1 of 16. And hiding the prior-derived descriptions while keeping verification scores and exit criteria still costs 5 composition successes, dropping from 8 of 16 to 3 of 16. So the value is not in the policies alone, but in the interface: the structural prior, stated in language, is what lets the runtime select and chain the right skill at the right moment.

A concrete example from the paper illustrates the mechanism. In a return-the-container task, the return policy's handoff note describes an emptied container, but its structural prior excludes particle state, meaning the prior says the policy does not depend on whether the container holds anything. The runtime agent reads that and infers the policy can return a full container, invokes it directly after grasping, and reaches the goal in two calls. That is exactly the kind of inference the authors want the interface to enable, the composition layer using the structural assumption to generalize beyond the literal demonstration. The skeptical read is more qualified. The in-distribution and motion out-of-distribution comparisons are system-level, meaning they confound the runtime agent's scheduling with the larger library of skill policies trained under diverse priors, and the authors are candid about this being a combined advantage rather than an isolated measurement of one component. The task-level out-of-distribution success of 92.5 percent is striking, but it rests on five tasks with eight cases each, and the composition count is 16 new compositions, a small sample where a few successes move the number by a whole percentage point. The fixed-rule and no-interface ablations are the cleaner evidence for the interface's value, and they point in the right direction, but the effect sizes come from a narrow task suite. The method also depends on a capable construction agent that can propose useful structural priors in the first place, which is an LLM in the loop, and the quality of the priors is as much a function of that model as of the framework.

For someone building a skill library for manipulation, the takeaway is specific and testable. The expensive part is not the policy backbone, which here is a standard diffusion policy trained for 20,000 updates. The expensive part is the interface, and the paper's claim is that the interface should be a structural prior that is used identically in training and in the runtime's decision-making. If you already have skill policies and a language-level composer, the cheapest experiment is to rewrite each skill's description as an explicit statement of what it depends on, and rerun your composition benchmark with and without those statements. If the no-interface ablation reproduces the 50 to 20 drop on motion out-of-distribution cases, the interface is doing real work in your stack, not just making the prompts sound more precise. The second experiment worth running is the proposal-selection loop: propose three or four structural priors per skill, train one policy per prior under a fixed budget, and select by a held-out out-of-distribution probe rather than in-distribution success. The paper's numbers suggest the selection step is where most of the out-of-distribution gain lives, and it is a step you can add without changing your policy architecture at all.

Verdict: this is a clean demonstration that training-time structural assumptions and runtime skill descriptions should be the same object, and the ablations are honest enough to show the gain is in the interface rather than the policies. The effect sizes are real but come from a small suite of six to ten tasks, and the system-level comparisons confound scheduling with policy library size. Treat it as a strong proof of concept for prior-grounded skill interfaces, with the open question being whether the approach scales to the long tail of skills a real manipulation robot would need.
