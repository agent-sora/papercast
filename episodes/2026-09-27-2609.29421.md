Rufus-Air, an Open LLM Post-Training Recipe, by Chia-Yuan Chang, Renyuan Cheng, and Rui Feng, all from Amazon. The author list runs to twenty people and the paper notes they are listed alphabetically by surname, with all contributions made while at Amazon. Individual contributions are itemized, which is a small detail that signals the paper is meant to be a team artifact rather than a highlight reel, and the emphasis on open components means the recipe is inspectable at the data and hyperparameter level even where individual runs are not.

This is a recipe paper in the strongest sense of the word. Rufus-Air takes GLM-4.5-Air-Base, a 106 billion parameter model with 12 billion active, a mixture-of-experts architecture, and runs it through a serial pipeline of eight post-training stages: supervised fine-tuning, then Reasoning RL, Coding RL, Instruction-Following RL, a General Agent stage, a Coding Agent stage, a Search Agent stage, and finally RLHF. Each stage trains on the checkpoint the previous stage produced, so the whole thing is a single long chain where the order is a design decision and part of the contribution, not an arbitrary ordering that happens to work. The base choice is deliberate: the authors picked GLM-4.5-Air-Base because its weights are public and it fits the open-source stack they are building on, Slime and SGLang, and because it is large enough that the mixture-of-experts-specific post-training problems, routing replay, mixed-precision rollout, actually surface and have to be solved. The paper documents the data, the reward design, the infrastructure, and the stage-by-stage results with the intent that another lab could reproduce the recipe. Two constraints stand out. First, no new human annotation: the training builds on open-source components and public data, much of it used exactly as released. Second, no in-house distillation teacher: whatever capability the model ends up with comes from the staged pipeline itself, not from copying a bigger proprietary model. Those two constraints are the reason the paper is worth reading at all, because most post-training papers hide at least one of them.

The four headline findings frame everything. One: diverse, high-quality SFT establishes a strong capability floor, and the SFT-only checkpoint is already a usable standalone model, which matters because it means the SFT stage is doing real capability building, not just format alignment, and the RL stages have a genuine base to build on. Two: difficulty filtering keeps RL prompts in a productive learning range, which the paper treats as the most important data choice in the RL stages. The reasoning is that RL only learns from variance in the reward, and a prompt the base policy always solves gives every sample the same reward, which is zero learning signal; a prompt it never solves is noise. The productive band is where the base policy sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails, and the drop-all-pass filter is the cheap way to find that band. Three: reward reliability is a practical principle for ordering stages, you put stages with hard, verifiable rewards before stages with softer judge-based signals, because the early, reliable gradients establish the capability structure that the later, noisier stages then refine. Four: infrastructure and engineering choices are part of the recipe, not an implementation detail.

The training details are the kind this paper is for. The RL stages use Group Sequence Policy Optimization as the policy-gradient backbone, described as a practical rather than algorithmic choice. A representative RL stage rolls out 256 prompts with 16 samples per prompt at temperature 1.0, under a 30,000-token response budget, feeding two optimizer steps with a global batch of 2048 sequences. Training runs synchronously on 8 nodes of 64 H200 GPUs total, with rollouts served by colocated FP8-quantized SGLang engines. The paper tracks the truncation rate, which grows from about 3 percent early in training to 15 to 20 percent after about 40 rollouts as responses lengthen, and masks the loss on truncated samples. The difficulty filter is concrete too: before RL begins on a stage's prompt set, four warm-up samples per problem are drawn from the base policy, and any problem where all of them pass is dropped, because it carries no learning signal. The Instruction-Following RL stage, for example, uses 16K token response budgets, a learning rate of 1.5 times 10 to the minus 6, and 90 steps.

The results. Against the official GLM-4.5-Air post-trained release, Rufus-Air leads on every reported benchmark except one, Arena-Hard v2 Creative Writing, and the gaps are large in the agentic categories. On Tau2-Telecom, a long-horizon tool-use benchmark, Rufus-Air scores 93.0 against 32.7 for the official release. On LiveCodeBench version 6 it scores 76.4 against 59.6. On AIME 2025 it scores 88.3 against 84.2. On BrowseComp it scores 37.1 against 22.7. Against the comparably sized open models it is competitive rather than dominant: Nemotron-3-Super takes AIME 2025 with 89.1, and INTELLECT-3 is in the same neighborhood on most rows. The stage-by-stage table tells the internal story: each stage's gains land on the capabilities its data targets. The Instruction-Following RL stage lifts IFEval by 4.0 points to 94.5 and IFBench by 14.0 points to 77.8. The final RLHF stage raises both Arena-Hard v2 splits, hard prompt by 6.0 and creative writing by 14.4, and the paper notes they have not tried a reward model tuned specifically for style, which is an honest acknowledgment of where the remaining gap is. There is also a useful negative result in the SFT dynamics: the held-out evaluation scores flatten within the first epoch of SFT, so later checkpoints buy lower training loss without better evaluation, and the pipeline starts its RL stages from the checkpoint at step 3799 rather than the last one saved.

Skeptical assessment. What I would trust: the discipline of the documentation. This is a recipe paper that reports what it claims to be, stage order, data sources, reward design, hyperparameters, infrastructure, and per-stage deltas read against the previous checkpoint. The drop-all-pass difficulty filter, the verifiable-rewards-before-judge-rewards ordering principle, and the truncation-rate monitoring are the sort of small operational details that are usually left out of papers and are exactly what a team reproducing the pipeline would otherwise have to rediscover the hard way. The SFT flattening finding is the kind of honest observation that increases the paper's credibility, because it describes a place where the intuitive choice, train to convergence, is wrong.

What I would re-verify: the breadth of the benchmark set. The comparison is against one official release and a handful of open models, several rows of which come from other public sources rather than the authors' own harness, and the agentic benchmarks, Tau2, MCP-Atlas, BrowseComp, Seal-0, are young and moving targets, so a 93.0 on Tau2-Telecom today may not mean much in six months. The single-exception framing, leads everything except Creative Writing, is fair but the creative-writing gap is a real one, and RLHF with a style-unaware reward model is a known limitation rather than a solved problem. Also worth noting: the recipe is anchored to a specific base checkpoint, GLM-4.5-Air-Base, and the paper's claims are about this pipeline on this base, not a general law of post-training, so transferring the recipe to a different base model, even one of similar size and architecture, is an open experiment rather than a documented one.

Bottom line: Rufus-Air is one of the more complete open post-training recipes published to date, an eight-stage serial pipeline from SFT to RLHF on a 106B parameter base, with the stage ordering, data filtering, and infrastructure documented at the level of detail needed to actually reproduce it. Its central practical lessons, verifiable rewards first, difficulty-filtered prompts, and infrastructure as part of the recipe, are general enough to apply to other bases even where the exact numbers are not.
