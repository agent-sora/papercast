Dual Exam: Controlled Evaluation of Multimodal Agents and World Models, by Zhefan Rao, Meng Chu, and Xuan Zhang, from the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, the National University of Singapore, the Hong Kong Polytechnic University, the University of Tokyo, and Carnegie Mellon University, among a long list of contributing institutions.

The paper starts from a methodological problem that anyone pairing agents with world models will recognize. A multimodal agent can use a world model for simulated experience and action-conditioned predictions, and an agent's trajectories can provide feedback for refining the world model. The field is full of systems that couple the two, and a lot of the enthusiasm is about that coupling. But if you run a coupled system and it works, you do not know which component is doing the work, or whether either one would work at all if you swapped the other. The pilot study that motivates the paper makes this concrete: one agent and one world model, paired, on 150 cases across ten scenarios. The failure modes they observe are exactly the entanglement they predict. When the coupled system gets an answer wrong, the trace shows cases where the agent planned sensibly but the world model predicted the wrong next state, and cases where the world model predicted correctly but the agent misread its own simulation. Both failure types produce the same coupled outcome, so a coupled score cannot tell them apart, and a team iterating on the system cannot tell which component to fix. Looking at the coupled outcomes alone, you cannot diagnose where failures come from, because the agent's errors and the world model's errors are entangled in the same score. Dual Exam is the answer: a framework that evaluates the two components separately, each under controlled conditions, so you get evidence about each one before you reason about their interaction. The agent evaluation runs agents under a fixed environment response mechanism, and the world model evaluation runs world models under fixed action interventions. The coupling is deliberately not evaluated, because the paper's position is that you cannot evaluate a product of two unknowns until you have evidence about the factors.

The four domains are concrete and worth listing, because they show the breadth the category structure is trying to cover. The physical domain covers navigation and manipulation. The digital domain covers desktop interaction and game play. The social domain covers interaction and coordination with other actors. The scientific domain covers experiment, simulation, and parameter search. That spread is why the category structure is there: an agent that can play a desktop game well may be useless at experiment simulation, and a single index would hide exactly that. The 15 reporting categories are the paper's way of keeping those differences visible in the results, and the per-category reporting is what a practitioner would actually use when choosing an agent for a specific deployment. The Agent evaluation compares 10 agent configurations on 1,088 items across 15 reporting categories, spanning physical, digital, social, and scientific domains. That is a broad, category-structured suite, and the 15 categories are reporting categories, so the paper can tell you where an agent is strong and where it is weak rather than giving one blended number. The World Model evaluation comprises 400 cases across eight scenarios, 50 per scenario, with 15 of the 16 world model configurations covering all eight scenarios and one covering six. The headline results are deliberately kept separate: GPT-6 Astra attains the highest Agent Index at 31.3 under the selected case-count weights, while GPT-Image-2 leads the full-coverage world model Overall scores at 21.7. The paper is explicit that these are track-specific scores on different tasks and metrics, not directly comparable, which is the opposite of what a lot of benchmark papers do, namely publish one number that pretends the two capabilities are on the same scale.

What the two tracks actually measure The 10 agent configurations and 16 world model configurations span the current frontier of both component types, from frontier lab models to open-weight systems, which is what makes the leaderboards useful as a reference point rather than as an internal comparison. The case-count weighting behind the Agent Index is selected rather than uniform, and the paper is explicit that the aggregate ordering depends on that choice, so the index is best read as a weighted summary of the category table, with the category table as the primary artifact. The 50-cases-per-scenario structure of the world model track keeps the per-scenario variance honest: each scenario score is a 50-case estimate, large enough to rank, small enough that the authors report it as a scenario profile rather than a precision-claimed number. is worth spelling out, because the names hide the design. The Agent track, with its fixed environment response mechanism, decouples the agent from any particular world model: the environment responds in a fixed way, so the agent is being scored on its own execution, planning, and adaptation, with the world's behavior held constant. That is the controlled-condition idea. The World Model track does the mirror image: the actions are fixed interventions, so the world model is scored purely on how accurately its action-conditioned predictions match the resulting states. Neither track lets one component compensate for the other. The result the paper wants is a pair of independent capability profiles per configuration, and a cross-configuration ranking within each track. The finding that the aggregate ordering depends on the domain mixture is an honest one: there is no single best agent, only best-under-this-category-mixture, and the 15-category structure is what surfaces that. Agent strengths remain category-specific across the board, which means any deployment that pairs an agent with a world model should be looking at the relevant categories, not the index.

The scope limits are stated plainly, and they are the most important part of the paper. The evaluations characterize task-specific execution and state-transition performance, and they provide a basis for studying reliable agent and world model interaction. But the paper is careful to say that the arrows in its overview diagram describe potential uses of the coupling, not demonstrated gains from joint training. It does not claim that the agents and world models it evaluated actually improve each other when combined, because that would require the coupled evaluation it explicitly declined to do. Whether the component-level capabilities predict coupled performance is left as the open question the framework is built to address in future work.

The skeptical read. This is a benchmark and methodology paper from a very large author list, and the right standard for it is whether the controlled decomposition is real and whether the protocol is reproducible. The fixed-response and fixed-intervention design is the genuinely useful contribution: it is the standard way to attribute credit in a two-component system, and it is something the agent-plus-world-model literature has been skipping. The caveats. The absolute scores are low, an Agent Index of 31.3 and a world model Overall of 21.7, which tells you this is an early, hard regime, and the scores are only meaningful relative to the specific case-count weights and domain mixture the authors chose. The agent track and the world model track use different items, different scenarios, and different metrics, so the two leaderboards cannot be merged, and any reader who wants a single number to quote is asking the wrong question. The 150-case pilot that motivates the whole framework is a single pairing, so the entanglement argument, while correct in principle, is illustrated rather than statistically established. If you are building or buying agent-plus-world-model systems, the practical takeaway is to demand component-level evidence before you trust coupled demos, and this paper gives you a concrete template for what that evidence looks like: fixed-environment agent scores across named categories, fixed-intervention world model scores across named scenarios, and no blended number. Trust the methodology and the discipline of keeping the tracks separate. Treat the leaderboard numbers as a snapshot of a hard, early regime, not as a stable ranking you can plan around.
