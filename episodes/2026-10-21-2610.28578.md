The paper is Sage-A1.5, from Wenjie Lou, Tianshuo Peng, and Shiyang Feng, part of the Sage-A1.5 Team at the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. It is a technical report on a new foundation model: a thirty-five billion parameter Mixture-of-Experts agentic model that builds on their earlier Agents-A1. The stated goal is to move a model past solving individual tasks toward organizing work and revising its approach during a long execution. The three things they say they add are agentic orchestration, agentic verification, and a push toward open scientific discovery. The headline claim is that at only thirty-five billion parameters it outperforms substantially larger models on several long-horizon benchmarks while staying competitive on the coding and search ones.

The lineage matters here, because Sage-A1.5 is explicitly a continuation, not a from-scratch release. Their Agents-A1 was a thirty-five billion Mixture-of-Experts agent model whose core bet was to scale the agent horizon rather than the parameter count. They built a knowledge-action infrastructure that wires together external knowledge, tool calls, observations, and verification feedback, and that produced training trajectories averaging forty-five thousand tokens. Agents-A1 already matched or beat trillion-parameter models on some benchmarks. So the question this report answers is what happens when you take that same small, long-horizon base and ask it to do the organizational work that a single step-by-step agent cannot reliably do: manage subtasks, compare approaches, and revise decisions as evidence arrives. The authors are candid that longer execution does not always mean better results, because an agent can repeat unproductive searches, build on unsupported assumptions, or accept a conclusion before checking all the evidence.

The method is framed as two complementary dimensions. Capability coverage is the range of domains and tasks the agent can address, which is what prior domain-specific training expanded. Capability composition is the new dimension: how those capabilities are organized, coordinated, and adapted during execution. They develop composition through two learned capabilities, and I want to be precise about what each one is, because the framing as model capabilities rather than framework rules is the actual contribution. Agentic Orchestration is the model deciding how to decompose work, which subtasks to delegate, what context each subagent gets, and how to integrate the returned results. Agentic Verification is the model recognizing when progress has stalled or a conclusion needs checking, reviewing the evidence and assumptions, and adjusting its next actions. Critically, at inference time that verification happens inside the model's own reasoning and tool-use loop, not via an online supervisor or a separate verification tool. That is a meaningful design choice, because it means the self-correction is learned into the weights rather than bolted on.

Orchestration has two patterns, and they give them a clean notation. Exploratory composition is like a logical or: the model assigns multiple subagents different hypotheses or solution approaches to the same question, and their outputs are alternative candidates with supporting evidence that the main agent compares to narrow the investigation. Constructive composition is like a logical and: the model decomposes a task into complementary subproblems, each subagent contributes a distinct part, and the main agent integrates them while coordinating dependencies and checking coverage. Real tasks interleave both as evidence comes back. The protocol is a main agent that either investigates directly or delegates, spawning a concurrent batch of subagents with isolated contexts, each running its own model calls and tool interactions. When the batch completes, each subagent returns not just an answer but a structured self-assessment, the supporting evidence, and the remaining open issues. That structured feedback is what lets the main agent spot gaps and conflicts, and it doubles as a signal for trajectory selection during training.

The training is a three-stage pipeline. First, the model is trained on diverse domain data, coding, long-context reasoning, deep research, scientific reasoning, and co-working, using environment construction, trajectory selection, staged supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning to build up multi-domain teachers. Then those teachers go through a unified outcome-process post-training stage that fuses the capabilities across domains, reasoning modes, and agent harnesses into a single model. The report also describes a post-training stage on engineering tasks specifically, covering machine-learning-optimization tasks and software-engineering tasks, and a recursive task-evolution procedure for long-context reasoning. They emphasize unified training across domains, modes, and harnesses, which is the claim that a single model can carry all of this rather than a router over specialists.

Two details in the training setup are worth flagging because they are where the results come from. The first is that the orchestration is learned on tasks specifically constructed for it, and the construction is nontrivial. For constraint-guided long-horizon search, they build a star-shaped relation tree around a shared answer: they pick an answer entity, find the entities directly related to it, extend a relation chain from each of those, mask the proper names and identifying details, and combine the branch descriptions into a single question where the only way to answer is to investigate the independent constraints in parallel and combine the findings. That is a task whose structure forces delegation, which is what you want when you are trying to teach a model to delegate. For multi-source research synthesis, they build tasks that require dividing a broad objective into complementary assignments, comparing findings across sources, and filling gaps before writing a report. The second detail is the structured feedback contract. Each subagent is required to return a local conclusion, a self-assessment of its own status, the evidence sources with their support, and the open issues it could not resolve. That contract is not a cosmetic choice; it is the mechanism by which the main agent learns to detect when a subagent has stalled, and it is the same signal used downstream to select which trajectories are worth training on.

The numbers are the part to interrogate. Across twelve benchmarks, Sage-A1.5 matches or surpasses both the equally sized Agents-A1 and Ornith-1.5 on all twelve. Against larger models, it beats Kimi-K3, GLM-5.3, and GPT-5.6-sol on FrontierScience-Olympiad, CMT, PinchBench, and IFBench, and is competitive on SWE-Bench Pro and BrowseComp. The pattern is telling: the wins are concentrated in the long-horizon, multi-step, and verification-heavy benchmarks, which is exactly where the orchestration and verification capabilities are supposed to pay off, while it is more even on the single-trajectory coding and search benchmarks where a strong base model already carries most of the load. That is the result you would predict from their own framing, which is reassuring, but it also means the differential is doing the work of validating the method rather than a uniform capability lift.

Where I would be skeptical before adopting this. This is a technical report from a single lab with a large author list, and the benchmark suite is heavily self-selected and self-hosted, with several in-house or community benchmarks like PinchBench and ALE-CLI where the authors have visibility into the task construction. The orchestration and verification are described as learned capabilities, but the report does not cleanly separate how much of the benchmark delta comes from the composition training versus from simply more long-horizon SFT and RL on the same base. The verification claim is the one I would push hardest on, because in-model self-verification without a separate checker is where these models are most prone to confidently confirming their own errors, and the report does not give a failure rate for when verification does not catch a bad conclusion. The engineering gotcha if you try to replicate this is that the whole pipeline depends on the knowledge-action infrastructure and the structured subagent feedback protocol, so the orchestration behavior is not going to emerge from a plain multi-agent prompt; it is trained. It is a credible foundation model for long-horizon agentic work at a small parameter budget, and the coverage-plus-composition framing is a useful way to think about what to train next, but treat the cross-model wins as lab-reported until they land on a benchmark you run yourself.
