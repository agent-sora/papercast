Today's paper is AliceAI-Foundation-80B-A3B-Base, a technical report from the Alice AI Team at Yandex, led by Nikolay Skachkov, Ekaterina Redina, and Maksim Konoplev. It is a mixture-of-experts language model with eighty billion total parameters and roughly three billion active per token, trained from scratch and released under Apache 2.0. This is the base model behind Yandex's Alice AI assistant, and the report is unusually complete: data collection, augmentation pipelines, evaluation protocols, architectural ablations, and scaling-law experiments are all in the paper.

Let me start with the architecture, because several of the choices here are doing real work. The model has forty-eight transformer blocks, a hidden size of 2048, and one multi-token prediction layer. The mixture of experts uses 512 routed experts per layer with ten selected per token, plus one shared expert, and the routing uses the auxiliary-loss-free balancing scheme from DeepSeek-V3. Attention is hybrid: thirty-six of the forty-eight layers use Kimi Delta Attention, a linear-attention mechanism, and twelve use full attention, with one full-attention layer following every three linear layers, so three quarters of the depth is the cheap recurrent form and only the periodic full layers carry the quadratic cost. Finally, the paper replaces the conventional residual sum with Attention Residuals, a learned weighting of representations across depth rather than plain addition.

Training proceeded in four stages. First, general pretraining on 17.5 trillion tokens at an 8,000-token context. Then two context-extension stages, growing the window to 32,000 and then 256,000 tokens, with reduced learning rate and batch size. The final stage is dedicated to reasoning and agentic interaction, run at a 128,000-token context, which is the length the reported long-context numbers refer to. The authors explicitly note that the reported scores do not establish performance at the 256,000-token intermediate length, which is a nice honesty point: they trained to 256K but evaluated at 128K. They also report that extending the 32,000-token stage did not substantially improve the long-context benchmarks, which is the kind of negative result that saves a reader a lot of GPU time: the 32,000-token stage exists as a ramp to make the jump to 256,000 stable, not as a performance lever in its own right.

One implementation detail the report calls out is the optimizer: Muon, not Adam. They also identify the limitations of held-out training loss as a proxy for downstream performance and introduce an auxiliary loss on the mixture-of-experts outputs to stabilize training at the predicted learning rate. In other words, they hit instability at the learning rate their scaling laws predicted, and they added a targeted loss to fix it rather than just slowing the run down.

On the data side, the report describes domain-specific data collection and augmentation pipelines in detail, which is where most of the practical value of the paper lives for a team planning its own run. Domain-specific data selection and augmentation are credited with improving factual knowledge, mathematics, and programming skills, and agent trajectories were injected during pretraining itself, not added as a post-hoc stage. The claim is that this pretraining-time exposure improves agentic benchmark performance after supervised fine-tuning, which is a cheaper alternative to the usual recipe of adding an entire agentic RL phase later. The authors also ran scaling-law experiments to pick the predicted learning rate, and the auxiliary loss on the mixture-of-experts outputs exists specifically because training at that predicted rate was otherwise unstable: they did not back off the learning rate, they fixed the objective. That is a useful pattern to note, because scaling-law-predicted learning rates frequently turn out too aggressive in practice, and the standard response is to just slow down and eat the extra compute. Their evaluation protocol for base-model reasoning uses pass-at-k with many independent generations, since base models produce formatting errors and per-sample results vary. They use zero-shot prompting on hard math and let the model choose how long to reason, and they note this protocol is informative for their model, Qwen3.5-35B, and Nemotron-3-Super, but substantially less so for GLM-4.5-Air and DeepSeek-V4-Flash in zero-shot, which makes cross-model comparisons on those numbers less reliable.

Now the headline numbers. In zero-shot reasoning, the released model reports 88.7 percent pass-at-8 on IMO-AnswerBench and 82.9 percent pass-at-8 on LiveCodeBench versions 5 and 6, which they frame as leading among open base models of comparable size. The pass-at-8 framing matters: with eight samples, the bar for at least one correct answer is meaningfully lower than pass-at-1, so treat these as ceiling-style numbers rather than median capability.

For factual knowledge, Yandex introduced and released two new benchmarks. WikiWebFacts tests short factual questions drawn from encyclopedic sources and search queries, and HardMultiQA tests harder factual questions inspired by real user interactions, with a heavy Russian-language component. Their model scores 86.5 percent on WikiWebFacts and 67.9 percent on HardMultiQA. The comparison targets are interesting: DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base, a 284-billion-total, 13-billion-active model, scores 83.2 and 65.4, and Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B-Base, 120 billion total with 12 billion active, scores 72.8 and 54.5. So an 80-billion model with 3 billion active parameters beats two substantially larger models on these factual benchmarks. That is a strong result, but there is a caveat you should keep in mind: the benchmarks were built by the same team, with Russian-language sources front and center, which is where a Yandex model has the most incentive to invest. The fact that they released the benchmarks and protocols is good practice, but the comparison set is self-selected.

Long-context evaluation goes to 128K tokens and emphasizes practical multi-document and long-conversation use rather than isolated needle retrieval. They use LongMemEval for locating, combining, and updating information in long dialogue histories, and they extend FinQA to 128K by packing many financial documents into one context so the model has to locate the relevant information before computing. One detail worth flagging: their base-model prompt reuses a single long dialogue for four questions, keeping demonstrations inside the context budget, which is a realistic constraint that a lot of long-context reporting ignores. For agentic work, base models are not natively agent-ready, so all compared base models receive supervised fine-tuning on the same agentic dataset before evaluation, and the suite includes tau-squared-bench, VitaBench, DeepPlanning, and SWE-bench Verified under the OpenHands framework, plus proxy benchmarks that score individual steps of an agent trajectory when the model cannot yet complete a full interaction. That proxy-benchmark design is a practical concession to reality: base models will fail to finish a whole multi-turn service interaction, and scoring only completed trajectories would leave most of the signal on the floor. Full reinforcement learning improves absolute scores further, but they argue the differences between pretraining configurations are already visible after supervised fine-tuning alone, which is a useful claim for anyone deciding where to spend post-training budget.

Where does this land skeptically? The architecture is a coherent synthesis of 2026 components: Kimi Delta Attention for cheap long-range state, periodic full attention for exact retrieval, Attention Residuals, auxiliary-loss-free MoE routing, Muon, and multi-token prediction. None of it is a single novel idea, and that is exactly the point of a foundation-model report: the value is in the full recipe being published with enough detail to reproduce. The 17.5-trillion-token pretraining budget and the agentic trajectory injection during pretraining are the two ingredients most worth studying if you are planning an open MoE base model, because both are cheap to try in smaller experiments. The factual-knowledge wins over larger models are the most exciting and the least independently verifiable.

The verdict: this is a well-documented open base model that demonstrates a small active-parameter MoE with hybrid linear attention can hold its own against much larger rivals, at least on its makers' own factual benchmarks. Its main open question is how the pass-at-8 reasoning numbers and the Yandex-authored factual benchmarks hold up under third-party reproduction.
