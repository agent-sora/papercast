Does Scaling Toward AGI Depend on What System-1 Lets System-2 Execute, by Qi Wang, Yun Qu, and Lizhou Cai, all at Tsinghua University, with the senior author Xiangyang Ji, is a forty-five page perspective and theory paper, not an algorithm release. The claim is that the next stage of scaling may not come from making the expensive executive, which they call System-2, bigger or longer, but from co-designing it with a cheap, semantically aware predictor, System-1, that scores every candidate interaction before execution and lets only the worthwhile ones through. Their headline result is an accounting identity: if you screen perfectly and execution costs are equal, the compute you spend on execution drops by a factor approaching one over rho-u, where rho-u is the fraction of candidate interactions that are actually useful. The rest of the paper is a careful statement of when that saving holds, when it shrinks, and what a "useful" interaction even means.

The motivation is the vanishing marginal return of undirected interaction. They start from the standard scaling law, loss falling as a power law in cumulative compute, and note that the gain from one more unconditional interaction goes like alpha times compute to the negative alpha minus one, which tends to zero as you spend more. So the question stops being how many interactions you can run and becomes which interactions justify their cost. They walk through three concrete waste cases. In reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, group relative policy optimization samples a group of responses per prompt and scores each against the group mean and standard deviation; when every response gets the same reward the group-relative advantage is exactly zero, so the prompt gives no policy gradient signal at all yet still consumed a full group of rollouts. They call this advantage collapse, and it is the single most concrete problem the whole framework targets. In the physical regime, rollouts are genuinely expensive because you cannot parallelize real robot time, so in high-dimensional task spaces which tasks you evaluate matters as much as how many. And they frame a "super parrot hypothesis," a three-stage picture where a model moves from imitating text, through chain-of-thought reasoning humans can follow, to a hypothesized third stage of self-coded representations and self-evolution, where an increasing share of what the model learns from is produced by the model itself. In that regime, every self-generated interaction is paid for with the model's own compute, so deciding which self-generated interactions are worth executing becomes a central efficiency problem.

The core object is what they call conditional interaction, a predict, gate, execute, update loop. System-1 takes a candidate, a prompt, a task configuration, a query, or a tool call, with its context, and returns a score rather than a solution, at a prediction cost that is much smaller than the execution cost. A threshold, which they write as kappa, admits only the candidates whose predicted utility clears it; System-2 executes those; and the realized outcomes are fed back to refit System-1. The selection policy itself is a normalized exponential of the predicted utility, times an indicator that the utility is above the threshold, with a temperature parameter beta controlling how sharply it concentrates on the best admissible candidate. As beta goes to infinity it degenerates to the greedy argmax rule, and as beta goes to zero it becomes a pure filter that admits everything above the threshold without ranking. There is a clean limiting-regime result: when the best candidate has a gap to the next-best admissible one, a modest choice of beta selects it with probability at least one minus delta, and if it is the only admissible candidate it is selected with probability one for every beta. When nothing clears the threshold, the policy abstains with probability one and executes nothing.

The formal contribution is the interaction information efficiency, or IIE, metric, which is the utility obtained per unit of system-level cost, counting both the prediction cost paid on every screened candidate and the execution cost paid only on the ones that run. The key proposition is the IIE gain. Under a perfect, free predictor, the ratio of conditional IIE to unconditional IIE is one over rho-u, and it exceeds that when the dead, non-useful interactions actually cost more to execute than the useful ones. With an imperfect, costly predictor, the same ratio gets multiplied by the inverse of a factor that grows with the false positive rate, the false negative rate, and the per-candidate prediction cost, so the saving is monotonically eroded by any of the three. A subtlety they flag: false negatives do not directly dilute the ratio, because a suppressed interaction is simply not paid for, but they reduce the yield, the utility per screened candidate, by the factor one minus the false negative rate. So an aggressive gate can raise the efficiency ratio while starving the learner of signal, which is why they insist efficiency and yield must be reported together.

The second load-bearing result is the screening efficiency proposition. Against the reactive baseline, which must execute an interaction before discovering it was useless, predictive screening acquires the useful subset at a cost of rho-u plus r, where r is the ratio of screening cost to execution cost, so the speedup approaches one over rho-u as r goes to zero. They give a concrete transformer accounting: rolling out a pool of n candidates to find the useful B of them costs on the order of n times group size times sequence length times the square of the model width, while predictive screening costs the encoding plus the prediction plus only B full executions. The memory result is the practical one for someone actually building this: to pick the top B of n candidates, synchronous reactive evaluation needs peak memory on the order of n times the full rollout state, while predictive scoring streamed in micro-batches needs peak memory that is independent of n, limited only by the B executed rollouts plus a small batch of embeddings. That means the candidate pool is then bounded by screening time, not by KV cache or replay buffer size, which is a real constraint when you want to evaluate a very large pool.

The scaling hypothesis is the paper's actual bet. They define informative compute as the integral, along a training run, of the fraction of compute currently spent on useful interactions. Under unconditional training that fraction is the cost-weighted pool density. They state a horizontal rescaling proposition: if the density is constant and the screen is perfect, the conditional loss curve is exactly the unconditional curve shifted left by the log of one over rho-u, which is the precise content of the multiplier used everywhere. Then they make an empirical hypothesis, that rho-u is non-increasing as the system improves, because mastered interactions leave the useful set, and that this decay may partly explain the diminishing returns and the scaling exponent, so that scaling is better described in informative compute than in total compute. They are unusually honest about the epistemic status: the IIE accounting, the screening and memory results, the regret bounds, and the co-scaling under fixed error curves are proved under stated assumptions; the application-specific savings are supported by citing existing predictive-sampling papers, not by new experiments; and the dominated frontier, the decay of rho-u, and a changed scaling exponent are explicitly labeled as hypotheses, not results.

There is one structural limitation they foreground, and it is the strongest objection to the whole approach. The gate censors its own training data: System-1 is supervised only by the interactions it lets System-2 execute, so its blind spots are exactly the candidates it wrongly suppresses, and those candidates never run, so the errors are never observed. Their minimal safeguard is audit sampling, periodically executing a small random fraction of the suppressed candidates so the false negative rate can be estimated, and re-estimating rho-u on an audit pool drawn independently of the gate. But this is a patch over a fundamental information problem, and the paper does not solve it. There is also a co-scaling proposition with a very long list of assumptions, saying that as you make System-2 more expensive, the optimal size of System-1 is non-decreasing, but they state plainly that it does not give the rate, that the assumptions do most of the work, and that a convergence guarantee for the general coupled loop remains open.

My verdict is that this is a clean and well-organized unification of a large and scattered literature on predictive prompt and task sampling, with the IIE accounting and the memory-independence result as the two pieces I would actually trust and reuse, and rho-u as a genuinely useful vocabulary for describing where training compute is leaking. The risk is that it is a perspective paper: the headline one over rho-u number is an idealized ceiling, not a measured speedup, and the central hypothesis that informative compute is a better scaling coordinate is currently a conjecture the authors themselves have not tested end to end.
