Learning to Act with World Models by Valuing What Comes Next, by Chenghao Gao, Zhi Zhang, and Dian Jin, from the National University of Singapore, UCLA, and Fudan University, is a method for training language-model agents with sparse terminal rewards, called WM-PPO, and its result is a 24 point gain on ALFWorld, raising success from PPO's 63.5 percent to 87.5 percent with a comparable number of real environment steps, and a 13.6 point gain on the harder ScienceWorld, from 39.3 percent to 52.9 percent. The idea in one line: PPO's critic already learns to value states by the probability of eventual success, so apply that critic to the next states predicted by a world model for candidate actions the policy has not actually taken, and use the resulting comparisons as extra training signal for both the policy and the critic.

The motivation is the information gap in sparse-reward agent training. When an agent gets a single binary reward at the end of a task, the intermediate steps carry almost no direct feedback. But most of the missing information is implicit in the dynamics: each state transition changes the conditions for success long before the reward arrives. The paper's example is the classic ALFWorld task of cleaning a plate and putting it in a cabinet. The agent finds the plate, picks it up, and puts it away without cleaning it. The terminal reward marks the whole attempt a failure, and it does not say which steps helped or what action would have left the agent better placed. The world model side of this is well understood: a model trained on real transitions can predict what each action changes. But the paper's key observation is that a predicted observation does not by itself tell you whether success became more likely. A transition that produces a very different observation may leave the chances of success unchanged, like opening a cabinet that reveals a long list of irrelevant objects. And a transition that changes almost nothing may establish a critical prerequisite, like the brief confirmation message from heating a mug that a later task requires. So the right quantity to value is not the predicted observation but the predicted state's probability of eventual success, and PPO's critic is already estimating exactly that.

The setup is a natural-language goal in a text environment. The state is the content of the agent's prompt: the goal, the five most recent observations and actions, the current observation, and the available actions or objects. The only reward is a final reward of 1 or 0, given if the task completes within 40 steps, so with discount factor 1 the value of a state is literally the probability of eventually succeeding under the current policy. The advantage of an action is then the expected change in that success probability caused by the action. PPO estimates this advantage only for the executed action, through the critic's values along the executed path. Estimating it for the alternatives requires the alternatives' transitions, which is exactly what a world model can predict.

The paper runs three diagnostics on ALFWorld before introducing the online method, and this is the part I would read most carefully if you are evaluating whether to adopt the approach. They take a PPO policy and critic after 24 iterations, at 53.6 percent success on the evaluation games. At every step the policy samples four candidate actions, each distinct candidate is executed for one step in a copy of the environment, and the critic values each resulting real next state. Executing the candidate with the highest-valued successor raises success from 56.9 percent under majority vote to 70.4 percent, a gain of 13.5 points with a standard error of 2.8, while executing a single sampled candidate gives 54.4 percent. A control that selects by the critic only when a candidate completes the task immediately and otherwise falls back to majority vote reaches 59.5 percent, which shows most of the gain comes from valuing intermediate successors, not terminal ones. So the critic genuinely carries information about intermediate transitions that the policy's own choices are not using. The second diagnostic checks the reliability of the critic's ordering against real success, split by the gap between the two values, and the agreement rises with the gap, which motivates the gating mechanism that comes next.

The method itself is deliberately small. All models are LoRA adapters on Qwen 3 4B Instruct. At each state along the main training trajectory, the policy samples several candidate actions and executes the first as usual, exactly as PPO would. The world model predicts the next observation for each candidate, and the critic values each predicted successor, giving a transition value per candidate. There are two channels. The policy channel turns comparisons among the transition values into auxiliary advantages inside PPO's clipped objective, so the policy is pushed toward candidates whose predicted consequences are better. The value channel uses the mean transition value as an additional one-step target for the critic at the current state, which then feeds back into the advantages used in later policy updates. Because value estimates in LLM reasoning are known to be noisy and biased, both channels pass through a shared gate. For each training task they collect one main episode plus three branches, each trying a different action at a randomly chosen step, and the gate weights both channels according to how often comparisons with similar value margins agreed with real branch outcomes earlier in training. Real returns remain the critic's main supervision. The world model is the only learned component added to PPO, it reuses the real transitions collected during training, there is no teacher model or privileged information, and only the policy is used at inference.

The results. With a comparable number of real environment steps, WM-PPO raises success from 63.5 to 87.5 percent on ALFWorld and from 39.3 to 52.9 percent on ScienceWorld. It matches the strongest PPO refinements they tested on ALFWorld and beats the best of them by 6.5 points on ScienceWorld, where tasks require establishing intermediate conditions before committing to an answer, which is exactly the regime the transition values should help. The analysis also shows the trained policy more often completes prerequisite steps before placing an object or committing to an answer, which is the behavioral signature you would expect if the value feedback is working as intended.

The skeptical assessment. First, the diagnostic that choosing among candidates by the critic's value of the predicted successor works, the 54.4 to 66.4 to 70.4 percent ladder, is the cleanest evidence in the paper, but it is also a test-time selection result, and the gap between the trained world model and the true next state, 66.4 versus 70.4, tells you how much of the critic's signal the world model can actually recover. That 4 point gap is the tax you pay for imagination, and I would want to see it under longer horizons and noisier environments where the world model's error compounds. Second, the shared gate is the right instinct, but it is also a moving target: it reweights both channels based on earlier training, which means early-training noise sets the channel weights, and I would want an analysis of how sensitive the final success is to the gate's schedule. Third, the 4B model scale is where the results live, and value-based methods are notoriously scale-sensitive because critic bias changes as the model grows. The claim is that no teacher is needed, which is true, but the method is not claiming parity with teacher-distilled approaches, and the comparison table should be read in that light. The strongest part is that the only added component is a world model trained on the same real transitions PPO already collects, which makes this cheap to drop into an existing PPO pipeline, and that is the property I would test first.

Bottom line: WM-PPO is a minimal, well-motivated addition to PPO that converts the critic's existing success-probability estimates into feedback for actions the agent never took, and the 24 point ALFWorld gain at matched interaction budget is a real result. If you are training language agents with sparse rewards, this is the first variant I would try, starting with the one-step diagnostic to check whether your critic already carries the signal.
