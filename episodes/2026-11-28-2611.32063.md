Today's paper is Integral DeltaNet, from Junsoo Oh of KAIST's AI graduate school, with Jihun Yun and Junhyuck Kim at KRAFTON AI, and Hanbaek Lyu in the mathematics department at Wisconsin–Madison. It is a paper about linear attention, the family of mechanisms that replace quadratic softmax attention with a fixed-size recurrent state so that computation scales linearly in sequence length and memory stays constant during decoding. Linear attention has become genuinely competitive: recent variants are now inside frontier-scale models like Qwen3-Next and Kimi K3. But it has a known weakness, and this paper targets it directly.

The weakness is memory collision. Compressing the entire past into a fixed-size state means old key-value associations overwrite each other. The delta rule in DeltaNet was introduced to mitigate this by updating the stored value for a key rather than blindly adding to it, and Gated DeltaNet and Kimi Delta Attention add gating on top. The authors' argument is that these mechanisms solve collisions by discarding, and in doing so they also discard information that future predictions might need. The decay and gating that prevent overwrites are the same mechanism that erases the old value before it is gone. You cannot keep the association and also keep a copy of the history with a single state.

Their answer is to maintain two complementary recurrent states. The first is a fast-updating delta state that captures the current key-value associations, exactly the role the delta rule already plays. The second is an integral state: a weighted running history of the delta state itself, so the model retains what the fast state used to hold before each update overwrote it. Concretely, the integral state is an exponential moving average of the delta state, with a per-key-channel integration rate that controls how fast the history follows the current state. A small rate keeps the old history alive for a long time, while a large rate makes the integral state track the fast state closely. Two design choices make this cheap to adopt. The delta-state update rule is left completely unchanged, which means the integral state is purely additive to the architecture: you can drop it into an existing Kimi Delta Attention or Gated DeltaNet-2 layer without re-deriving the update dynamics. And the two states are combined only at readout, through a channel-wise gate that decides, per key channel, how much of the query goes to the fast state versus the history. The paper also sketches a natural future extension: using the integral state to guide the delta-state updates themselves, analogous to how schedule-free optimizers use averaged iterates, but that is left as future work rather than implemented. The whole thing comes with a chunkwise parallel formulation, which matters because it means training stays hardware-efficient on the parallel side while inference uses the recurrent form. So you get the standard training-inference asymmetry that the other linear-attention variants have, without sacrificing the new state.

The evaluation is done from scratch at two scales: 340 million parameters trained on 15 billion tokens, and 1.3 billion parameters trained on 100 billion FineWeb-Edu tokens, which is a serious budget for a mechanism paper. The baselines are the full relevant family: a transformer with quadratic attention, Mamba-2, DeltaNet, Gated DeltaNet, and Kimi Delta Attention, plus a Gated DeltaNet-2 variant with independent erase and write gates.

At the 1.3-billion scale, the numbers are consistent rather than spectacular. Their Integral version built on Kimi Delta Attention gets a WikiText-2 perplexity of 15.00, the best in the table, against 15.33 for plain Kimi Delta Attention and 15.73 for DeltaNet. LAMBADA perplexity is 9.16, again best, versus 9.63. On the nine-task commonsense average, Integral Kimi Delta Attention scores 56.1, beating Kimi Delta Attention's 54.6, Gated DeltaNet-2's 54.2, and even the quadratic transformer's 54.7. The win over the quadratic transformer is the one to sit with: a linear-attention mechanism beating full softmax attention on average commonsense accuracy at one point three billion parameters, with the integral state contributing the difference. At 340 million, the picture is the same but tighter: the Integral variant averages 46.5 on the commonsense tasks against 46.1 for Kimi Delta Attention and 45.3 for Gated DeltaNet. On the retrieval side, the numbers make the mechanism claim concrete. On six real-world retrieval tasks, the Integral version of Kimi Delta Attention averages 37.3 percent at the 1.3-billion scale, against 35.4 for plain Kimi Delta Attention, 32.8 for DeltaNet, and 28.6 for Mamba-2, though the quadratic transformer still leads at 45.6, so retrieval remains where softmax attention holds a real edge. The synthetic long-context results show particularly large gains on multi-needle tasks: in the RULER multi-key-value needle test, finding several items at once is exactly where a fast state that keeps overwriting itself fails, while the integral history preserves the overwritten associations. The single-needle variants improve too, with the largest relative gains on the repetitive-text setting. They also test length extrapolation beyond the 4,000-token training length, and the 1.3-billion model trained at 4,000 tokens is the one that extrapolates best among the compared recurrent models on their perplexity measurements. The paper also includes a small worked example that formalizes the collision-versus-forgetting trade-off. It constructs three keys, with the first key equal to the sum of the other two, and a query whose correct answer flips from No to Yes on the later keys. Under that construction, plain linear attention makes the wrong retrieval call on two thirds of the possible queries, because the initial No association dominates the readouts for both later Yes keys. Mamba-2, DeltaNet, and Gated DeltaNet all reduce that risk to one third: decay and targeted overwriting suppress the interference and recover both later answers, but they forget the first No answer in the process. The authors note the limitation is structural, not tunable: because the first key is the sum of the other two, any single-state linear readout satisfies an identity that forces two Yes-favoring readouts to also make the first one favor Yes, so even optimally set gates cannot reach zero retrieval risk. That is the cleanest possible statement of what the Integral State is for.

The efficiency section is where this paper will actually be cited. They measure training throughput and activation memory of the 1.3-billion models on an Nvidia B300 at a fixed token budget without model-level activation checkpointing, and decoding memory and per-token latency at batch size 8 across context lengths, with latency measured via CUDA graph replay. They provide two implementations: a throughput-first version that stores intermediates for reuse, and a memory-first version that recomputes them during backpropagation. The throughput-first build keeps training speed close to the backbones it extends, while the memory-first build cuts activation memory at a moderate throughput cost. The decoding numbers are the clean ones. Across context lengths from 4,000 to 64,000 tokens, the recurrent models hold flat decoding memory and latency, exactly as the architecture promises: plain Kimi Delta Attention decodes at 2.85 gigabytes and 3.34 milliseconds per token at every length, while the Integral version sits at 2.99 gigabytes and 4.63 milliseconds. So the extra state costs about 0.14 gigabytes and roughly 1.3 milliseconds per token, a constant overhead, and the context-length scaling behavior is preserved. For comparison, the quadratic transformer climbs from 7.77 gigabytes and 5.18 milliseconds at 4,000 tokens to 83.7 gigabytes and 42.6 milliseconds at 64,000, which is the gap this whole line of work is trying to close.

Where does this sit in the family? This is the second wave of linear-attention variants that respond to the first wave's erasure problem. The first wave added the delta rule and gates; this wave adds a second state that remembers what the first state overwrote. It is a clean, well-motivated idea, and the from-scratch training at a hundred billion tokens is the part that separates it from a toy ablation. The skeptical caveats: the perplexity and accuracy margins over the best baseline are in the low single digits, the comparisons are all at one billion parameters or below so scale behavior is unverified, and the adaptive readout combination adds parameters and a training objective that a production team will have to re-validate.

The verdict: Integral DeltaNet is a principled fix for the central failure mode of linear attention, backed by the largest from-scratch linear-attention training I have seen in a recent mechanism paper. Expect it to show up in the ablation tables of the next generation of hybrid models more than in a leaderboard of its own.
