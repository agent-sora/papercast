LLaDA-Image: Building Strong Image Generators with Fully Open Training Recipes, by Chuyan Chen, Haoxing Chen, and Kun Chen, from the AGI Research Center at Inclusion AI. This one is here both because it has the day's highest true upvote count and because it lands under the standing rule for foundation-model releases: it is a new image-generation base model trained from scratch, and it ships open, which is the part that makes it worth your time rather than just another leaderboard entry.

The framing. The paper pairs two things that do not usually get paired this cleanly. One side is a 6 billion parameter Diffusion Transformer, a DiT, trained from scratch as the generation engine. The other side is a frozen vision-language understanding module built on the LLaDA 2.0 Mini diffusion language model backbone, which supplies the semantic and instruction-following prior. The two are coupled so that the understanding module conditions the generation, rather than the generation model trying to learn its own text comprehension from scratch. The key design decision is about data ordering: instead of leaning heavily on paired image-text data from the very start, the authors first build a strong visual generative prior through image-only pre-training and mid-training, and only then move to paired image-text supervised fine-tuning. The stated motivation is that a model that has already learned to generate plausible images from images alone does not have to burn its capacity learning basic visual structure from scratch on the paired data.

The data is the part worth noting in implementation terms. The generation pipeline uses 220 million samples, and 98 percent of those are real images, not synthetic. That is a deliberately high real-data ratio, and the paper reports that even in the paired image-text supervised fine-tuning stage, real images still make up above 70 percent of the data, with the remainder being more than 90 percent of the total in some configurations. The data is also staged by resolution: the image-only pre-training stage crops images resized to 256, while the mid-training and subsequent fine-tuning stages use higher-resolution crops, so the model starts by learning low-level visual structure cheaply and only pays for high resolution once it has a prior to build on. If you are running your own image generator, the real-data ratio is usually the number you want to push higher and the number that is hardest to sustain at 220 million samples, so the claim here is partly a data-engineering claim, not just an architecture claim. The bridge between the image-only stages and the paired image-text stages is itself a design choice: the paper argues that the image-only pre-training and mid-training give the diffusion transformer a generation space it can then condition on text, rather than trying to learn generation and conditioning simultaneously from the very first batch.

The optimization choices are the second implementation-relevant detail. Throughout the DiT the authors use a parameter-free RMSNorm, so there are no learned gain or bias parameters in the normalization layers, and they optimize with the Muon optimizer rather than AdamW. The paper credits this combination with stabilizing training at scale and making the run efficient and reproducible. Parameter-free normalization plus a momentum-based matrix optimizer is a specific recipe, and it is the kind of choice that is easy to dismiss until you try to reproduce a large DiT run and watch your loss curve do things.

The release has a distillation story as well. LLaDA-Image is distilled into LLaDA-Image-Turbo, which enables fast inference in 2 to 4 sampling steps, and the distillation uses a twin-flow formulation. The Turbo model is built on distribution-matching distillation, following the signed-time construction from the TwinFlow line of work, so the student is trained to match the teacher's output distribution across the sampling trajectory rather than just matching final images. So the practical artifact is a two-speed system: a high-quality base model for when you can afford the sampling budget, and a turbo variant for when you cannot. This is the part that matters for real deployment, because a 6 billion parameter diffusion model at full sampling budget is often too slow for interactive use, and the 2 to 4 step variant is what makes the model usable in a product setting.

The benchmark results are the state-of-the-art claim. On Qwen-Image-Bench, LLaDA-Image scores 53.53 on the English track and 53.38 on the Chinese track, and the authors state this is a new state of the art among open-source models on both tracks. The two numbers being within a fraction of a point of each other is a nice sign of cross-lingual robustness, because image generation benchmarks often show a noticeable gap between the English and Chinese evaluations, and a model that is strong on one and weak on the other is not a general generator. The Qwen-Image-Bench is a composite of sub-scores covering generation quality, instruction following, and editing, so the 53 point figure is an aggregate that reflects the joint capability the paper is claiming rather than a single image-statistics number. The paper also emphasizes that the model produces highly photorealistic images while following fine-grained editing instructions, so the claim is about joint generation and editing quality, not just FID-style image statistics, and the fine-grained editing instruction following is the part that is hardest to fake in an evaluation.

The open-sourcing is the part that changes how you should use this paper. The authors release the model weights, the training code, and the detailed recipes, which means the 220 million sample data construction, the image-only pre-training to paired SFT ordering, the parameter-free RMSNorm plus Muon setup, and the Turbo distillation are all something you can actually read and attempt to reproduce. For a foundation model release, the value of the paper is arguably the recipe, not the benchmark, because the benchmark tells you it is strong but the recipe tells you how to be strong, and most releases only give you the first half. The fully open stance is also what makes the 98 percent real-image claim checkable: because the data pipeline is released, a reader can verify the real-versus-synthetic ratio in the actual training data rather than taking the number from the paper, which is the difference between a claim and a reproducible recipe.

The skeptical read. The state-of-the-art claim is scoped to open-source models on Qwen-Image-Bench, which is one benchmark and one leaderboard, so the number should be read as a strong open-model result rather than an absolute ceiling, and you would want to see it on at least one independent evaluation before treating 53.5 as a durable position. The 6 billion parameter scale is mid-sized for image generation, so the recipe's transfer to larger DiTs is an open question; the parameter-free RMSNorm and Muon choices are validated at 6 billion, not at the scale where training instability typically bites hardest. The 98 percent real-image ratio is impressive but the paper does not fully break down how the synthetic 2 percent contributes to specific capabilities, so the real-data claim and the capability claim are somewhat entangled. And the Turbo distillation, while useful, is the part least likely to transfer cleanly, because 2 to 4 step distillation is sensitive to the exact teacher and the sampling distribution, and a recipe that distills well at one scale often needs re-tuning at another.

The bottom line: this is a competent, well-documented foundation image model, and the fully open weights, code, and recipes are what make it a genuine contribution rather than a leaderboard entry. The defensible core claims are the open-source state of the art on Qwen-Image-Bench, the high real-data ratio, and the parameter-free normalization plus Muon training recipe. The limits are the single-benchmark, single-scale scope, so treat the 6 billion recipe as a strong open starting point and the distillation as a component you will likely need to re-derive at your own scale.
