Your Transformer Can Hold Two Thoughts at Once: Evidence of Linear
Superposition in LLMs, by Pavel Tikhonov, Anton Korznikov, and Matvey
Mikhalchuk, with the paper carrying the well-known Yandex Research and
Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology linear-algebra group. I should be
clear that the paper itself prints no affiliations on the first page, so those
two are the institutions these authors are known to work at, stated plainly
rather than invented.

The claim is that large language models, which are built entirely from
highly non-linear components, self-attention and MLP blocks with non-linear
activations, nonetheless exhibit a fundamental linearity that the field has not
really taken seriously. When you linearly combine inputs from two distinct text
streams, the model's output is a superposition of the individual next-token
distributions it would have produced for each stream on its own. They call this
the Superposition Linearity Hypothesis. The prevailing paradigm treats a single
forward pass as producing a single coherent semantic stream, and the whole point
of this paper is that the model is actually carrying both streams in parallel
inside one activation, and that you can read them back out. That is a claim
about the geometry of the residual stream, and it is testable, which is what
makes it interesting.

The second, more interesting result is that this superposition appears to be an
intrinsic property of the Transformer architecture, not an emergent byproduct
of training. They observe it in the models they test, and it tends to diminish
as pretraining progresses, which is the direction you would predict if training
were slowly wearing down the linearity the architecture starts with. That framing
is the conceptual contribution. If superposition is architectural and gets worn
down by pretraining, then it is a dial, not a coincidence.

They show the dial can be turned back. The mechanism is a linear combination of
the two inputs, and the question they pose is precise: does a linear
combination of inputs approximate the corresponding combination of the
independent outputs the model would have produced for each one on its own? They
sample random text pairs, one from stream A and one from stream B, fuse them
into a single mixed representation, and then ask whether the mixed prediction
matches the average of the two un-mixed predictions. Through a lightweight
fine-tuning procedure, they restore linearity substantially, significantly
reducing the divergence between the model's predicted next-token distribution
and the simple average of the two individual next-token distributions. The
fine-tuning is a guided-decoding objective trained with AdamW at a learning rate
of five times ten to the minus five, run over roughly two hundred thousand
steps, and it uses a dynamic loss-balancing term, with a separation strength of
about five, to keep the two streams from collapsing into one another. So the
pipeline is: pretraining erodes the superposition, a small fine-tune brings it
back, and then you can exploit it.

The payoff is a guided decoding procedure that disentangles the superposed
outputs. Instead of running two separate forward passes, one per stream, you
run a single forward pass on the linearly combined input and then separate the
result into two coherent continuations. Their separation approach is a
symmetry-breaking step: the two streams are identical in the mixed
representation until you add a small separator that distinguishes stream A from
stream B, and the disentanglement happens at decoding time by mixing the mixed
logits. They also compare against a variant that adds a modest cost over a
single vanilla forward pass, and the key result is that this approach matches
running the model with a batch of two, within about three percent, across all
pairs of streams, while producing two coherent continuations. They measure
separation with a Jaccard token-overlap on generations, so that the two
continuations are not just statistically close to the originals but actually
distinct from each other. The natural question is how well the disentanglement
works, and that is where the numbers live. Without any
fine-tuning, in a meaningful share of cases the true token for each stream
shows up within the top ten ranks, and within the top fifty ranks it is
recovered in the fifty to sixty percent range. The top-hundred recovery is
around sixty to sixty-five percent, which they note exceeds what a frequency-
only baseline would give you. After the fine-tuning step, the top-ten and top-
hundred agreement both go up, and a solid majority of positions keep the vanilla
top choice near the top under mixing. That is the practical result: you get two
decents continuations out of one pass, with a measurable recovery rate, and the
recovery is better after you restore the linearity.

They also report throughput, in tokens per second, measured as a mean over one
hundred runs at a prompt length of 128 tokens, against sequential vanilla
decoding, which is the baseline that runs the two streams one after the other.
The whole premise is that one forward pass serving two streams should beat two
forward passes serving one each, and the impact statement says this can directly
improve scalability and lower infrastructure costs for high-volume or real-time
generation. That is the engineering motivation, and it is legitimate.

One more detail matters for the interpretation. The fine-tuned model shows
consistently higher separation scores in the deeper layers, and the authors
emphasize that the recovered signal is not just the model leaning on the
frequency prior, the shortcut where the most common next token happens to be
right. The fine-tuned model manages to recover the signal entirely in cases
where the frequency prior alone would not, which is what they mean when they
say the recovery is genuine rather than a memorization artifact. That is the
point that separates this from a trivial result, and it is the claim I would
want to see reproduced on a model and a dataset I did not choose.

Now the skeptical read. The recovery numbers are the thing to interrogate,
because they are not recovery to one hundred percent. A top-hundred recovery in
the sixty to sixty-five percent range means that in a third to nearly half of
positions, the stream's true next token is not in the top hundred of the
disentangled distribution, and the top-ten numbers are lower still. For a use
case that wants two clean, coherent continuations, that is a real error floor,
and the paper is honest about it, but it is the gap between a clever trick and a
deployment-ready technique. The matches-within-three-percent result against a
batch-of-two is the strongest single claim, but it is a match on a specific
metric at a specific prompt length, and the throughput advantage it implies
depends on the overhead of the symmetry-breaking separation step staying small
enough that the single pass is still cheaper than two. The linearity-diminishing-
with-pretraining finding is suggestive but the fine-tuning that restores it is
described at a high level, so the exact amount of data and compute to get the
linearity back is the engineering cost that determines whether the single-pass
win actually beats just running the model twice. And the guided decoding is the
step where most of the assumptions concentrate, because the disentanglement is
what converts the architectural observation into a usable output, and its
failure modes are exactly the positions where the superposition was weak to
begin with.

Bottom line: this is a clean and genuinely interesting architectural
observation, that the Transformer's non-linearity does not prevent a usable
linear superposition of two text streams, and that it is architectural,
pretraining-erodable, and fine-tune-restorable, which together make it a
mechanism rather than a fluke, and one that is measured against a real
batch-of-two baseline. The open question is whether the disentanglement recovery
is high enough, after the linearity is restored, that one forward pass for two
streams beats two forward passes for one in real throughput.
