Title: GraphForge: Training Working Agents with Graph-Anchored Workspace Synthesis

Arxiv: 2609.38923

GraphForge is from Qisheng Su, Hanchen Wang, and Guanru Zhu, with the team spanning the University of Science and Technology of China, Fudan University, the Shanghai Innovation Institute, and the Shanghai AI Laboratory. It is a data-synthesis pipeline for training working agents: models that read diverse files, coordinate tools, and produce deliverables.

The problem is data. Training an agent to do real work requires tasks built on many realistic files with results you can actually verify, and the paper argues there are few pipelines that produce that kind of data. The two existing approaches each fail in a different way. One generates the files with a model, and the files lack realism and diversity. The other builds tasks on real files, but without task-specific verifiers, so nobody checks whether the result is right. GraphForge's answer is to ground both the task and its verification in real files through an evidence graph. For each task, the system starts from an occupation-grounded seed, chosen to control the diversity of the resulting work. It then assembles a workspace of real files around that seed and builds an evidence graph over the relations between those files. The task statement and the grading rubrics are both derived from the graph, so every requirement in the task is backed by specific files in the workspace, and every rubric criterion is anchored to the files needed to verify it.

The pipeline has a validation stage that matters more than it might sound. After the task and rubrics are derived from the graph, a strong teacher model does an initial rollout on the task. That rollout tests whether the task is actually executable, and a revision agent then repairs both the task and the rubrics against the original files before any training trajectories are collected. So the task is not just generated; it is stress-tested for solvability and its grading criteria are checked against the files they claim to verify. The output of the whole pipeline is a set of trajectories, and the paper reports using 2,169 of them for the main fine-tuning runs.

The results. The construction funnel starts with 3,638 materialized tasks, of which 2,169 end up as validated training trajectories, roughly 60 percent of the materialized set. Supervised fine-tuning Qwen3.6-27B on those 2,169 trajectories brings GDPVal to 1445.7, a gain of 65.7 points, under the OpenHands harness, and Workspace-Bench-Lite to 63.7, up 7.7, and SpreadsheetBench II to 24.0, up 13.7, under Claude Code. The same data also improves a second base model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, which the authors take as evidence that the trajectories generalize across base models rather than fitting one architecture. Then there is a second stage: rejection fine-tuning on the SFT model's own rollouts, with candidates selected by the evidence-anchored rubrics. That yields further improvements on all three benchmarks, suggesting the rubric-based selection keeps working as a quality filter even after the model has already seen the data.

The paper also does some analysis that I would consider the more interesting part of the contribution. First, a train-test overlap audit: they compare the 39,201 files in the training corpus against the 260 files in GDPVal's test set, find zero shared files, and zero substantive text overlap at the 13-gram level. At the occupation level, 13 of 44 GDPVal occupations are covered by the training data, and they run a grouped comparison: on covered occupations the SFT model wins 43 of 65 head-to-head comparisons, and on uncovered occupations it wins 109 of 155. The win rate on uncovered occupations is actually higher, which is the result you want to see. If the gains came from memorizing GDPVal content, you would expect the opposite pattern. The authors' read is that the fine-tuning transfers working skills rather than benchmark knowledge. Second, a judge sensitivity probe: they deliberately delete the worksheet that a rubric criterion cites, and the score for that criterion drops by 0.377 on average while all other criteria move by about 0.016. That is a decent localization result: the judge is reading the cited file and its judgment is anchored to it. The negative result is worth stating honestly: corrupting individual rows, numbers, and citations inside the cited file barely moves the scores, and the authors attribute that to the capability limit of the judge model, GLM-5.2, which catches structural failures but not fine-grained content errors.

Now the skeptical read. The headline GDPVal number is a large absolute gain, but it is measured with an LLM-based rubric judge, and the paper itself shows that judge misses fine-grained corruptions of the very files it is supposed to verify. So the verification layer that makes this pipeline more trustworthy than the alternatives is itself imperfect in exactly the regime where file-level detail matters: spreadsheets and numeric deliverables, which is also where SpreadsheetBench II starts from a low 24 percent. The data is real, which is the whole point, but "real files" here means crawled files organized around occupational seeds, and the coverage of the test distribution is partial by their own audit, 13 of 44 occupations. The fine-tuning setup is also modest in scale by the standards of the models it is being applied to: 2,169 trajectories, which is a data-efficient result but one that does not yet speak to what happens at 100 times that volume, or whether the evidence-graph construction cost scales favorably.

Verdict: this is a concrete and well-audited data pipeline whose overlap checks and judge-sensitivity probes are more careful than most in the synthetic-agent-data space, and the cross-model transfer result is the strongest single piece of evidence for its value. The open question is whether the rubric-anchored verification holds up on fine-grained numerical work, where both the base benchmarks and the judge are weakest.

A few implementation details worth flagging for anyone who wants to reproduce or test this. The two-stage training is deliberately sequential: SFT first, then rejection fine-tuning on the SFT model's own rollouts, with the rubric deciding which rollouts are kept. That second stage is cheap in data and is where the rubric's anchoring does its most important work, since it is filtering the model's own behavior against the source files. If you were building a competitor pipeline, that stage is the one to study: the evidence graph is what makes the filter task-specific rather than a generic quality score. The harness choice also matters and is stated explicitly: GDPVal is evaluated under OpenHands and the other two benchmarks under Claude Code, so the reported numbers are coupled to those agent scaffolds, and a different harness would change the absolute levels. The paper does not sweep across harnesses, so treat the cross-benchmark comparison as indicative rather than a controlled comparison of the models alone. And the revision step, where a revision agent repairs the task and rubrics after the initial teacher rollout, is where a lot of the engineering cost of the pipeline lives; the funnel numbers suggest that most tasks survive unchanged, with 81.6 percent of rollouts reused without modification, which is encouraging for cost but also means the repair path is exercised on a minority of the corpus.

For anyone planning to test this idea in their own setting, the natural experiment is the covered-versus-uncovered split. Reproduce the audit on your own task distribution: take the files your training corpus draws from, check overlap against your evaluation set at both the file and the n-gram level, and then measure the fine-tuned model separately on tasks whose occupations or file types appear in training versus those that do not. If the uncovered win rate does not at least match the covered one, the data is memorizing your distribution rather than teaching the working skills the paper claims. The second test is judge robustness: build a small perturbation suite, structural failures like deleting a cited file, and fine-grained ones like corrupting a number inside a cited cell, and check where your judge model sits between those two. The GraphForge result, a 0.377 drop for structural failures against about 0.016 for fine-grained ones, is a useful baseline to compare against. If your judge clears both regimes, you can trust the rubric-filtered rejection stage more; if it only clears the structural regime, your second training stage is filtering on coarse signal, and you should budget for that in how much weight you give the reported gains.
