Work Agents: Systems, Capabilities, and Optimization, by Zishan Xu, Enjun Du, and Mingqi Gao, from Shanghai Jiao Tong University, the University of Hong Kong, and Tsinghua University, with the full author list spanning the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Guangzhou, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Zhejiang University, Sun Yat-sen University, and Central South University.

This is a position and framework paper, so it makes no benchmark claims and reports no numbers of its own, and the honest way to evaluate it is whether the structure it proposes organizes the field better than the structures in use. The paper runs to over 90 pages, and the length is itself a signal of its ambition: it is not proposing one result but a shared vocabulary for a research area that has been advancing along separate tracks, the long-horizon task agents, the computer-use agents, and the office-workflow agents, each with its own benchmarks, its own evaluation habits, and its own theory of what success means. The starting premise is that foundation models are moving from information generation to digital work execution, completing tasks across tools, applications, and workflows, and that the research so far has no unified perspective on how an agent reliably accomplishes delegated work. The paper's answer is a work-centric, system-level view. A work agent is a system combining a foundation model and a runtime harness that executes tasks in stateful workspaces and delivers verifiable outcomes. The two words doing the most work in that definition are stateful and verifiable. Stateful, because real work persists beyond a single turn: the agent must carry state across waiting periods, external events, and handoffs, and decide at each checkpoint which parts of that state are still live. Verifiable, because the measure of success is a checkable outcome in the workspace, not a plausible-sounding response.

The paper then introduces two axes for characterizing working conditions, and this is the core of the taxonomy. The first axis is work persistence, the degree to which a task must be maintained over time, across interruptions, external state changes, and follow-ups, rather than resolved in a single exchange. The second is work openness, the degree to which the environment is open, with unknown objects, changing requirements, and outcomes that cannot be fully specified in advance, as opposed to a closed, fixed workspace. Combining the two gives a space in which different deployments live, and the paper argues that which region your workload occupies determines which capabilities matter and which optimization approach is the right one. On top of the two axes it classifies digital work into five forms by task scope and organization: the conversation level, producing an informational answer or interpreting evidence into a response; the atomic action level, creating or modifying a local work object and checking the change; the multi-step level, completing an objective through intermediate results and managing the dependencies and feedback between actions; the workflow level, carrying work through functional stages and coordinating the handoffs; and the workspace level, locating and connecting related objects across a whole workspace while tracking their dependencies. The point of the five forms is that they are a ladder of scope, and a system that works at one rung has not demonstrated competence at the next.

From the framework the paper derives five core capability families required for reliable work execution: perception and understanding, reasoning and decision making, planning and coordination, execution and state management, and verification, recovery, and replanning. The last one is the one most agent frameworks under-weight, and it is where the paper's emphasis on verifiable outcomes pays off, because verification and recovery are what turn a sequence of actions into a delivered outcome. The paper then positions work agents against the adjacent paradigms it wants to subsume rather than replace: long-horizon agents, which stress the temporal dimension; computer-use agents, which stress the interface between the model and a running application; and office agents, which stress the document and file substrate. Its claim is that these are special cases of work execution, distinguished by where the persistence and openness sit, rather than separate problems with separate solutions, and it works through the relationships between them explicitly, including the cases where one paradigm's assumptions break down in another's deployment. A concrete illustration of why the state-management family is its own category: a task that spans a waiting period, where an external event changes the workspace while the agent is idle, demands that the agent re-establish which of its prior commitments are still live when it resumes, and that is a different competence from planning the steps in the first place, which is why the paper refuses to fold it into planning. The paper then treats optimization as a three-way split, which is the part most likely to be useful to a practitioner. There is model optimization, changing the weights, through training data, reward design, and the standard post-training stack. There is harness optimization, changing the runtime around the model, the context management, tool execution, and verification apparatus, with the model held fixed. And there is joint optimization, where the two are co-designed, because a capability that the model cannot supply may be supplied by the harness, and a harness that compensates for a model weakness can hide the weakness from your evaluation. The paper closes with an outcome-oriented critique of current evaluation, arguing that most benchmarks score the response rather than the verifiable work outcome in the stateful workspace, and with a set of open directions: sustained work delegation, where the agent is given a standing assignment rather than a one-shot task; adaptation to genuinely open environments, where the objects and requirements are not known in advance; and the feedback loop between executing work and improving the capability that executes it, which the paper flags as the frontier where the model and the harness stop being separable.

The skeptical read. This is a survey, so the standard test is whether it adds a structure you could not have drawn yourself, or whether it repackages the long-horizon versus computer-use versus office-agent distinctions already in circulation. In its favor, the persistence and openness axes plus the five-form ladder are a cleaner way to say what a workload actually demands than the usual capability lists, and the explicit three-way split of model, harness, and joint optimization is the framing I would want in a design review, because it forces you to say which layer you are actually changing when you claim an improvement. Against it, there is no experiment, no benchmark, and no number in the paper, so every claim is a claim about how to organize the field rather than a measured result, and the five-form classification and the five capability families are author-selected with no evidence that the boundaries are the right ones or that a real deployment maps cleanly onto a single rung. The risk with this genre is that it reads as authoritative because it is comprehensive, and the thing to check before you build a roadmap on it is whether the persistence and openness axes survive contact with your actual workloads, because if your tasks are mostly one rung of the ladder, a lot of the framework is overhead. The bottom line: this is a reasonable and useful map of the work-agent space, organized around stateful workspaces and verifiable outcomes, and its three-way model, harness, and joint optimization split is the part worth keeping. It is not evidence and it will not move a metric, but if you are trying to decide where to invest across a capability, a harness, or their interaction, it gives you a defensible vocabulary for the decision, and the five-form ladder is a reasonable first map to test your own workloads against before you commit to a single agent architecture, because the persistence and openness coordinates are cheap to compute for your own tasks and cheap to be wrong about.
