The paper is DeltaStreamer: Towards Human-Like Agentic Streaming Video Understanding, from Boyuan Sun, Deshui Miao, and Wenzhao Gao, with the first author at the Video Coding and Intelligent Perception lab at Nankai University, the core team at ByteDance, and collaborators at IQuest Research and the Institute of Automation at the Chinese Academy of Sciences. It is an agentic framework for understanding video that arrives as a live stream rather than as a finished clip, where the system has to build its memory without knowing what questions will come later, and can only answer a question once the relevant moment has already passed.

The problem is specific. In offline video understanding you have the whole sequence and can look at any frame. In streaming, frames arrive one at a time, you cannot see the future, and a question might arrive at the one minute fifty one second mark about something that happened at the three second mark. So the system has to retain evidence before it knows which details will matter. The prior approaches fall into a few families, token compression, key-value cache reuse, and multi-turn dialogue, and the authors argue they share two failure modes. First, they record what is present but leave what is changing implicit, and because consecutive frames are mostly similar, the repeated representations eat the token budget and obscure exactly the transitions that distinguish one event from the next. Second, once a frame has passed, the only evidence is whatever was retained, and if a detail was dropped during compression, there is no way to recover it when a later question needs it.

Their framing is that streaming video understanding is an active information-gathering problem, not a passive encoding problem. The system should watch frame by frame the way a human does, continuously observing and updating a structured memory, and then, when a question arrives, actively probe that memory, decide whether the retrieved evidence is enough, and go back to revisit earlier frames to recover pixel-level detail. That last capability, targeted replay, is the centerpiece.

The first mechanism is delta-augmented observation. Before any query arrives, a vision-language model looks at the current frame and the preceding sampled frame together with a snapshot of the current memory, and emits two things: a structured scene description with fields for description, actors, objects, actions, optical character recognition, and scene, and a delta, which is a textual description of what changed between the two frames, like an object appearing or changing state. Because consecutive frames produce similar descriptions even when something important shifts, the delta is what makes the change explicit instead of buried. They also compute a textual novelty score for each observation against recent history using a weighted Jaccard similarity across fields, which drives salience weighting for retrieval and flags repeated content, while low-salience records are still stored rather than dropped.

The second mechanism is a traceable hierarchical memory organized as a directed acyclic graph with three layers. Working memory holds the fine-grained delta-augmented observations, episodic memory summarizes clusters of related observations into event summaries, and long-term pins hold compact summaries of persistent facts. The layers are linked with bidirectional source pointers, and an entity index maps actor and object names to their observation times, so a record at any level can be traced back to the specific observations and the specific video frames that support it. When a query arrives, retrieval backtracks through this graph by keyword overlap and salience, following source pointers to build evidence paths, and a render step assembles the memory snapshot that gets fed to the model, trimming older verbatim entries toward a text budget while keeping the summaries and the recent observations, with omitted records left in storage and still retrievable.

The third mechanism is the query-driven planner. When the question lands, the planner does a structured analysis of what the query needs: the target, the temporal locus, whether the evidence is in past, current, or incoming frames, and the type of evidence required. That plan persists across subsequent reasoning and guides memory retrieval, continued observation, and tool use. And when the retrieved descriptions lack the decisive visual detail, the agent follows the source links back to the relevant frames and replays them, recovering the pixel-level evidence that the textual memory had omitted, so the final answer is grounded in the actual pixels rather than in a lossy summary.

The replay step is where the system earns its "human-like" name, and it is worth spelling out why it is non-trivial. A human watching a stream can, when asked a question, mentally rewind to the relevant moment and re-examine it with full visual fidelity. A passive streaming model that only kept compressed text cannot do this, because the pixels are gone by the time the question arrives. DeltaStreamer preserves the linkage from every memory record back to its originating frames, so when the planner decides the textual evidence is insufficient, it does not have to guess; it can pull the exact frame range, render it, and reason over the actual image. This means the fidelity of the answer is bounded by the original video, not by the compression ratio that was chosen at observation time, which is a fundamentally different guarantee from a method whose accuracy degrades silently whenever its memory budget is too tight. The planner's role in deciding whether replay is warranted is what keeps this from being wasteful; it is a targeted, query-driven recovery, not a blanket re-encode of the whole stream.

The results are on two benchmarks. On OVO-Bench, which tests real-time visual perception, backward tracing into past events, and forward active responding, DeltaStreamer scores an overall sixty-six point zero seven percent, a gain of five point three two points over the strongest agentic streaming baseline, EventMemAgent. The gain is spread across all three task families rather than concentrated in one, which suggests the delta and replay design is helping the general streaming loop rather than a single narrow question type. On StreamingBench, which covers a broader set of streaming interaction capabilities, it scores fifty-nine point five three percent, exceeding the STRIDE baseline by one point nine five points. Ablations attribute the OVO gain to two design choices: the delta recording itself, where a no-delta variant scores sixty-one point one two against sixty-six point zero seven for the full system, and the hierarchical memory, which reaches sixty-six point zero seven compared with sixty-one point two five for a flat memory layout.

What I would trust is the diagnostic framing, which correctly identifies that the two real failure modes of streaming understanding are losing the transitions and losing the recoverability of dropped evidence, and the delta-plus-replay design is a direct answer to both. The ablations cleanly isolate the delta recording and the hierarchical memory as the load-bearing pieces rather than the fancier tooling. What I would re-verify: the gains over the nearest agentic baselines are modest, a few points on one benchmark and under two on the other, and the method is a VLM-in-the-loop agentic system, so the cost of running observation, planning, retrieval, and replay per query is substantial, and the paper does not give a fine-grained latency or token-cost breakdown that would let you predict whether replay is affordable at the frame rates real deployment demands. The benchmarks are also relatively small and the baselines span a wide range of maturity, so the absolute numbers should be read as direction-setting.

Two-sentence verdict: DeltaStreamer is a well-motivated agentic design for streaming video that correctly targets the transition-loss and recoverability problems, and the delta-plus-targeted-replay combination is a genuine improvement over passive compression. The gains over current agentic baselines are real but small, and the per-query cost of the observe-plan-replay loop, which the paper does not break down, is the open question that will determine whether this is practical outside the benchmark.
