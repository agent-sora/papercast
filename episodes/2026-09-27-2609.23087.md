Neural Spectral Capacity, Measuring and Designing Architectures from Network Specification Alone, by Chenyu Zhu, Ruoyu Zhao, and Zhichao Lu, from the Department of Computer Science at City University of Hong Kong. This is a small, tightly scoped theoretical paper, three authors, one scalar, one solver, and three empirical demonstrations, and the tight scope is what makes it useful.

The problem is that modern transformer design and compression both reduce to the same question: under a fixed budget, how do you allocate capacity across the architectural components? At pretraining time that means choosing depth versus width, the feed-forward ratio, attention head sharing such as grouped-query or multi-query attention, and per-layer expert allocation in mixture-of-experts models. At post-training time, structured pruning poses the same question in reverse: given a deployment budget, which components do you shrink? The standard scalars for these decisions are parameter count and FLOPs. The paper's complaint is that both of them capture size and compute but not structure. Two architectures with identical parameter budgets but different depth, width, head, or feed-forward allocations get identical scores from both scalars, even though they behave differently once trained. The paper wants a scalar that sees the structure without needing to actually build the model, which matters because at large scale instantiating each candidate is expensive and the search space is too large to explore by sampling.

The proposal is Neural Spectral Capacity, or NSC, a closed-form scalar grounded in the singular-value spectrum of each weight matrix. The key mathematical fact that makes this work is the Marchenko-Pastur law: under standard random initialization, the empirical singular-value distribution of a weight matrix is determined entirely by the matrix's aspect ratio, its row-to-column ratio. That means the spectrum, and therefore NSC, is computable from the architectural specification alone, the dimensions of each layer, with no model instantiation, no data, and no gradients. You do not need to allocate a single GPU to compute it. The second structural property is that NSC is additive across layers. That additivity is what unlocks the solver. The intuition for why a spectral scalar is more structure-sensitive than a count scalar is that the singular values of a randomly initialized layer encode how the matrix distributes its capacity across its input and output dimensions, and two layers with the same number of parameters but different aspect ratios distribute that capacity differently, one is a wide, shallow mapping, the other a deep, narrow one, and they behave differently under training even though they are identical on a parameter-count basis. NSC is the scalar that sees that difference at initialization time.

The solver is NSC-DP. The scalar itself decomposes multi-head attention per head and then sums the per-layer contributions across the whole network, which is what makes the layer-wise additivity clean. Because NSC sums across layers, the constrained maximization, pick the depth, width, head count, and feed-forward ratio that gives the highest total score under a parameter or FLOPs budget, reduces to a bounded knapsack problem, which is exactly solvable by dynamic programming. The paper implements this as Algorithm 1: it enumerates the shared structural choices and, for each, solves the inner layer-level knapsack, so the returned architecture is the global maximum of the proxy rather than a local one. It reports that this runs on a single CPU core over search spaces of up to 10 to the 32 candidate architectures. That last number is the point of the whole method: existing training-free architecture search is black-box and heuristic, it samples architectures and scores them, so it can never certify optimality. NSC-DP gives you a certificate, the answer is provably the best one under the scalar, and it runs in seconds rather than hours. The paper is explicit that this guarantee is something black-box search over training-free proxies cannot provide.

The empirical results are three in number and each is targeted. First, ranking quality. NSC outperforms parameter count, FLOPs, and representative training-free proxies at ranking architectures, across seven transformer and CNN families. The specific number: on FlexiBERT, considering only pairs of architectures whose parameter counts differ by less than 10 percent, the rank correlation of NSC is 0.505, while parameter count collapses to 0.082. In other words, in exactly the regime where the standard scalar is useless, because everything has nearly the same parameter count by construction, NSC still separates the architectures. This is the demonstration the whole paper is built on, because it is the only regime where a structure-sensitive scalar can be distinguished from a structure-blind one: if two architectures have the same parameter count, parameter count cannot rank them at all, so any positive correlation NSC shows is pure structural signal. Second, design. NSC-DP discovers a transformer-XL-style architecture on the WikiText-103 task that beats the human-designed baseline, and it does so in 2 seconds of CPU time. Third, compression. The method prunes LLaMA-7B down to the best 5.7B model across eight commonsense reasoning tasks, with no calibration data at all, and it does so roughly 5900 times faster than the strongest training-free proxy baseline. The speedup number is less interesting than the no-calibration-data part, because calibration-data-free pruning is the property that makes structured pruning deployable in a setting where you do not have a representative evaluation set on hand.

Skeptical assessment. What I would trust: the math. The Marchenko-Pastur argument is a clean one, the singular-value spectrum of a randomly initialized matrix is fixed by its aspect ratio, and deriving a structure-sensitive capacity scalar from that is the kind of result that is either right or not, not something you tune. The knapsack reduction and the optimality guarantee are the strongest part of the paper, because they turn architecture selection from a heuristic search into a solved optimization with a certificate, at a computational cost that is trivial by any modern standard. The FlexiBERT number, 0.505 versus 0.082, is also the right way to demonstrate the contribution: it shows the scalar working precisely where the incumbent scalar provably cannot.

What I would re-verify: the gap between the proxy and the trained model. NSC is computed at random initialization, which is the whole point of being training-free, but it is ultimately a score on the untrained architecture, and the paper's evidence that a high NSC at initialization predicts a good trained model is the empirical section, not the theory. The transformer-XL result is a single task, WikiText-103, and the pruning result is a single model family, LLaMA-7B, on commonsense reasoning. Before I would use NSC-DP to make a real pretraining budget decision, I would want to see the initialization-score-versus-trained-performance correlation reported as a regression across a proper grid of trained checkpoints, not just the three demonstration cases. The 5900 times speedup is also a speedup against a baseline that is doing something more expensive, and the comparison is only meaningful if the proxy baseline's calibration cost is the actual cost you are paying in your own workflow. The paper's own framing, a training-free proxy, is the honest one, and the claims are scoped to that, but the gap between a proxy score and a deployed model is where the real risk lives, and it is the part the demonstration does not fully close.

Bottom line: Neural Spectral Capacity is a small, rigorous contribution that turns the structure-blindness of parameter-count and FLOPs scoring into a solvable, certifiable optimization, and its practical value is immediate: you can rank, design, and prune transformer architectures from a specification sheet on a laptop, in seconds. The Marchenko-Pastur derivation and the dynamic-programming optimality guarantee are the durable results; the three empirical demonstrations are promising but scoped, and I would want a broader trained-model correlation before staking a pretraining budget on the proxy. The paper is the right size for what it claims, and the spec-sheet-only property is genuinely novel.
