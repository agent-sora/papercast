Title: BYOD: Build Your Own DLM - Efficient Autoregressive-to-Diffusion Language Model Conversion
Authors: Ruurd J.A. Kuiper, Robert A. Bagheri
Labs: University Medical Center Utrecht, Utrecht University
Arxiv: 2610.41574
Day: 2026-11-03
Upvotes: 3
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41574
---

Build Your Own Diffusion Language Model, or BYOD, by Ruurd Kuiper and Robert Bagheri, from the University Medical Center Utrecht and Utrecht University, is a two-author recipe for converting an existing autoregressive language model into a diffusion language model without retraining it, using LoRA adapters that make up a small single-digit fraction of the final parameter count while every parameter of the base model stays frozen. The motivation is the gap in the field. Diffusion language models have real properties that autoregressive models do not: parallel token updates, flexible generation from partially masked inputs, and bidirectional attention, which opens up editing and infilling that a left-to-right model structurally cannot do. But the leading diffusion language models, LLaDA and its peers, got there by expensive pretraining or full-model fine-tuning, which is out of reach for most groups and a non-starter if you already have a strong autoregressive checkpoint you do not want to throw away. BYOD's claim is that you can induce diffusion generation in a frozen autoregressive model with a lightweight adapter, and the paper delivers that claim on four different model families.

The scope is what makes the result interesting rather than just cute. The four conversions are Gemma 2 at 9 billion, Llama 3.1 at 8 billion, Qwen 2.5 at 7 billion, and Ministral at 8 billion, and the authors note that this is, to their knowledge, the first reported autoregressive-to-diffusion conversion of a Mistral family model. The recipe is architecture-agnostic, no per-model tuning, which is the property that turns a single-model result into a recipe. The adapters are 4.1 to 5.8 percent of the final parameters, and each conversion runs for 25,000 iterations on at most 102.4 million tokens, processed in 3.1 to 4.0 hours on a single Google Colab GPU. That last number is the one a practitioner would bookmark: a diffusion conversion of an 8-billion model on a single consumer-class GPU in a few hours, as opposed to the pretraining or full fine-tuning budgets the alternatives require. The paper even builds a FLOPs-based estimator, calibrated against reported accelerator-hours, to predict the cost of converting other models, which is the kind of detail that says the authors think in terms of reproducibility.

On quality, the honest picture is mixed, and the paper is candid about it. On a nine-task matched evaluation covering reasoning, math, and code, BYOD-Gemma is the strongest conversion, reaching 50.6 percent average accuracy against 51.9 percent for LLaDA 8B Instruct in the same harness, which is within a point and a half of a dedicated diffusion model, and that is the headline. But the generation quality at low budgets is the weak spot, and the paper names it as the principal limitation: the perplexity of the BYOD conversions when generating 128 tokens in 64 denoising steps is 8.3 to 11.1, against 4.1 for LLaDA and 2.2 to 3.0 for the autoregressive parents. A perplexity in the double digits is the number that says the open-ended generation is not yet as good as the thing being converted, and it is where the method is most clearly a bridge rather than a destination. The controlled-attention test is the one that validates the core claim: all four converted models generate from fully masked sequences and use bidirectional context as designed, which is the structural property that justifies the whole conversion, and the knowledge-retention results show the parent model's knowledge survives the conversion, with the gaps concentrated in mathematics and code, exactly where diffusion generation is weakest.

There is a small but genuinely informative set of probes in the paper that I would weigh more than the benchmark table. The controlled-attention test, where the converted models demonstrably use bidirectional context, is the causal check that the conversion did what it claimed. The knowledge-retention probes, where the models are given a clue and asked to reproduce a target, show graded retention: the converted models copy the visible target in a high fraction of cases while the autoregressive parents do not, which is a clean demonstration that the bidirectional property changed the model's behavior in the intended direction. And the ablation on training length, where longer conversion training gives small but uneven gains, is the honest result that says the recipe has headroom, most of it in math and code, and that a few more million tokens of conversion training is the obvious next experiment. The data mixture for the conversion is also specified, a one-million-example mix weighted across instruction following, multiple-choice reasoning, mathematics, and code, which is the reproducible detail a copyist would need.

The honest caveats. This is a conversion, not an improvement: the resulting models are diffusion versions of their parents, and the perplexity gap says the conversion costs generation quality at low budgets, which is the price of the bidirectional and parallel properties you get. The benchmark margin over the dedicated diffusion model is within a point and a half, so the case for BYOD is cost and access, the ability to convert any strong autoregressive checkpoint on a single GPU, not superiority. The evaluation is nine tasks at 8-billion scale, so the generalization to larger models, where the adapter fraction and the training budget would both shift, is untested. And the two-author, single-institution provenance is worth noting in the other direction: this is a clean, well-documented recipe from a small group, with code and interactive demos released for all four conversions, which is the opposite of the opaque-system-report failure mode, and it is the kind of result that is easy to verify and easy to build on.

To put the cost claim in perspective, the comparison that matters is not against a diffusion model trained from scratch, which nobody with a single GPU would do, but against the fine-tuning path that BYOD replaces. A full-model fine-tune of an 8-billion checkpoint to induce a new generation mode would touch every parameter, need a multi-GPU budget, and risk degrading the very capabilities you are trying to preserve. BYOD touches 4 to 6 percent of the parameters, freezes the rest, and finishes in under four hours on one Colab GPU, which puts the conversion inside the budget of a single afternoon of experimentation. That is the real unlock, and it is why the four-family result matters more than any single score: it says the adapter recipe is not tuned to one tokenizer or one attention scheme, so the next group does not have to re-derive it for their favorite backbone. The honest frame is that BYOD converts a capability, not a model: you get the bidirectional and parallel generation properties, you keep the parent's knowledge, and you pay for the conversion in open-ended perplexity at low budgets. Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on what you want diffusion for, and the paper does a good job of making the trade visible instead of burying it.

My verdict is that BYOD is a practical, well-executed recipe that makes diffusion generation a property you can attach to an existing autoregressive model in hours on one GPU, and the four-family scope is what elevates it from a single-model trick to a general method. It is the paper to read if you have a strong autoregressive checkpoint and a reason to want bidirectional generation, and it is the paper to read if you are trying to decide whether the diffusion turn is worth the perplexity it costs. But the double-digit perplexity at low budgets is a real cost, and the benchmark result is a tie with the dedicated diffusion model rather than a win, so this is the on-ramp to diffusion language models, the cheap way to get in and experiment, and the gap it must next close is the open-ended generation quality at the budgets where diffusion models are supposed to shine.
