Learning Beyond What You Sample: Off-Policy-Aware Cross-Model Trajectory Exchange for RLVR. The first three authors are Doohyuk Jang, Yoonsik Park, and Gyouk Chu, from KAIST, with corresponding author Eunho Yang, who is also affiliated with AITRICS.

The starting point is a well-known failure mode of RLVR. Group Relative Policy Optimization learns from reward variation inside each group of sampled responses. When every response in a group fails, all group-relative advantages are zero, and the group contributes no policy-gradient signal at all. The standard fixes are to drop the group, replace the prompt, reshape the rewards, or sample more responses, and all of them stay inside the learner's own exploration. The observation that motivates this paper is that the open-source model ecosystem makes a different fix available: a successful response missing from one model's rollouts may already exist in another model's. The authors verify this empirically with independent GRPO runs of two small base models, SmolLM3 3B and Qwen3 1.7B, which have distinct pretraining histories. Across training, SmolLM3 solves 47.9 percent of the prompts on which Qwen3 fails on all eight rollouts, and Qwen3 solves 18.7 percent of SmolLM3's all-fail prompts. The complementarity persists throughout training, which is the dotted-line trend in their first figure. The paper's framing is deliberate: this is mutual exchange without a designated stronger teacher, not distillation from a bigger model.

The method is GRAFT, Gated Replacement of Answer-Failed groups with peer Trajectories, and it has to answer two questions: which prompts get peer supervision, and how does the receiver learn from peer responses. For the first, GRAFT selects prompts on which the receiver fails entirely and the peer produces both successful and unsuccessful responses. The requirement that the peer has both outcomes matters: an all-success peer group has no within-group reward contrast, and an all-fail peer group offers nothing to imitate. The selection also balances exchange volume across the two directions, so one model does not become a permanent donor. For the second, GRAFT replaces the selected receiver group with the corresponding peer group and retains the advantages computed in the peer's own rollout, preserving within-peer reward contrast without pooling rewards across models. Cross-model mismatch is controlled at two levels. Sequence-level compatibility gating down-weights peer sequences that are a poor match for the receiver, with a gate threshold of 0.8 used across all pairs. Token-level importance ratio clipping bounds how far the receiver's per-token weights can drift when learning off its own policy. Finally, the receiver's own data stays primary: peer-containing minibatches are processed after the on-policy minibatches within the same update step.

The implementation runs on the verl framework with Ray, fully sharded data parallelism, and vLLM rollouts. Each model samples eight responses per prompt, and training data, learning rate, and number of steps are held fixed across methods, which is the right control for this kind of comparison. The paper reports the total GPU-hour cost of the full GRAFT run: both selected receiver checkpoints together require 23.2 GPU-hours, excluding the prior GRPO runs used to collect the peer logs. That is a concrete number to weigh against the 2.1 point average gain, and it is worth noting that the cost is dominated by the receiver's own rollouts, not the exchange itself, since the peer trajectories are already in memory or can be read from disk.

The three pairs are constructed from small open base models with different pretraining lineages: one pair pairs SmolLM3 3B with Qwen3 1.7B, a second uses the Qwen3 side as the updated policy against a different peer, and a third pair uses an OctoThinker 3B hybrid model as peer. The deliberate heterogeneity is the point: the complementarity fraction, and therefore the method's ceiling, is a property of the pair, and the results are reported per pair rather than averaged into a single number.

The evaluation covers three heterogeneous pairs of open-source base models and five mathematical reasoning benchmarks: MATH500, AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC 23, and Minerva, reporting pass at one, averaged over five evaluation runs with eight samples per prompt. The headline result: GRAFT improves both models in every pair over GRPO with the same per-model rollout budget, by 2.1 points on average across benchmarks and up to 4.5 points in model-level average score. In two of the three pairs, both models match or exceed GRPO trained with four times the rollout budget, meaning the exchange buys the equivalent of a four-fold rollout budget without paying for the extra rollouts. Against the two closest prior methods, HACPO, which accounts for cross-model mismatch but shares peer rollouts beyond receiver-failure prompts, and SGT, which targets receiver failures with a fixed-weight supervised loss, GRAFT wins by 4.0 and 1.5 points on average respectively. As a concrete number from the main table, on the SmolLM3-updated block, GRPO with eight samples averages 32.60, HACPO scores 31.51, GRAFT reaches 37.06, and GRPO with 32 samples reaches 35.71, so GRAFT at quarter the rollout cost beats the four-fold baseline on that block by 1.4 points.

The ablations and robustness checks are where I would look first as an implementer. The paper repeats both GRPO and GRAFT three times independently for all three pairs and reports means and sample standard deviations: GRAFT maintains higher mean aggregate performance than the single-model baseline in all six model blocks, so the effect is not a lucky seed. A stored-trajectories variant reuses peer trajectories from completed independent GRPO runs without simultaneous co-training, preserving 1.8 points on average of the 2.1 point gain, which is operationally important: you can run two RLVR jobs separately, bank the trajectories, and exchange them in a second pass. The paper also applies HACPO's and SGT's update rules to GRAFT's selected prompts, and both still underperform, which isolates the contribution as the combination of complementary prompt selection and compatibility-aware learning from full peer groups, not either piece alone.

The skeptical read. The models are small, 1.7 and 3 billion parameter base models, and the gains, while consistent, are 2 to 4 point ranges on pass at one. The complementarity that makes the method work depends on the two models having genuinely different pretraining histories; if you run two checkpoints of the same model family, the all-fail sets will overlap heavily and there is little to exchange. The 47.9 percent versus 18.7 percent asymmetry in the motivating figure also tells you the exchange is not symmetric: one model is a net donor on some prompts, and the paper's volume balancing is what keeps that from degrading into one-directional imitation. The compatibility gate threshold of 0.8 is reported as fixed for all pairs, and the appendix carries the full hyperparameters, but the sensitivity of the gate to model-pair similarity is the variable you will tune in your own deployment, and the paper does not give a schedule for it.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the problem framing: all-fail groups are a real, measurable source of wasted rollout budget in RLVR, and borrowing verified successes from a heterogeneous peer is a legitimate way to recover the signal. I would trust the stored-trajectories variant most, because it decouples the exchange from simultaneous co-training and is the easiest to adopt incrementally. Before adopting the co-training variant, I would re-verify the complementarity fraction on my own model pair, because that single number predicts the ceiling, and I would check the pass at one versus pass at eight gap to make sure the gains are not variance artifacts of small evaluation budgets. The engineering gotcha is bookkeeping: you are now maintaining two policies, two rollout caches, and cross-policy advantage computation in one training loop, and the minibatch ordering, on-policy first, peer second, is a load-bearing detail that changes the effective learning rate on peer data.

GRAFT turns a property of the open-model ecosystem, heterogeneous complementarity, into free rollout budget, with clean controls and honest ablations. Expect the gains to scale with how different your model pair is, and start with the stored-trajectories variant before committing to co-training.
