DRBench Pro: A Knowledge-Centric Benchmark for Professional Deep Research Agents, from Han Huang, Yuchen Yan, and Xingyu Wu, at Zhejiang University and Tencent. The claim: existing deep-research benchmarks judge the quality of a report along a few broad dimensions, but none measures which research directions a report actually covers and how deeply it explores them, so the authors built a benchmark that makes structured knowledge coverage the primary target and derives its scoring rubrics from the structure of the knowledge itself.

The task is deep research. A deep research agent takes a complex, open-ended question and iteratively searches, selects evidence, and synthesizes a long-form report that connects relevant facts, explains relationships, and organizes the findings into a coherent account. This is different from short-form question answering, because the agent has to decide which research directions even deserve investigation, and it has to do that without a human telling it what the answer is supposed to look like. That decision, which directions to pursue and how deep to go in each, is itself the thing that makes deep research hard, and it is also the thing that is hardest to evaluate once the report is written. The gap the authors target is the evaluation. Prior deep-research benchmarks do assess report quality, along dimensions like factual correctness, citation quality, and structure, but they do not have a dedicated, knowledge-centric view of coverage. Two reports can have the same overall accuracy and yet one is broad and shallow while the other is narrow and deep, and a flat quality score does not tell you which one you got. That is the distinction they want to measure.

The core idea is to make the reference for a task be a structured knowledge tree rather than a flat list of facts. They build local knowledge trees from Wikipedia, and they sample subgraphs of those trees to generate both the research query and the evidence-grounded rubrics. Each rubric corresponds to a relation in the tree, which means the structure of the knowledge being evaluated is preserved in the scoring, and it is also what makes the whole thing extensible: you can add a new domain by pulling its tree and sampling, without having to hand-write a rubric for every case. The agent receives only the research query, not the tree, so it cannot game the reference. Then coverage is measured in two directions. Breadth is how many of the major research directions, the top branches of the tree, the report touches. Depth is how far down related knowledge chains it goes, the relations nested under a direction. The overall coverage score is the geometric mean of breadth and depth, which is a deliberate choice: a report that is broad but shallow, or narrow but deep, gets penalized, because the geometric mean only rewards balance. That is exactly the trade-off a professional research report faces.

The scale is substantial. The benchmark has 144 cases across 24 domains and 44,102 rubrics. Tens of thousands of rubrics is a lot of structure, and it is what lets the breadth and depth measurements be meaningful rather than a guess over a handful of reference facts. The automated pipeline is the reusable piece here: knowledge tree construction from Wikipedia, subgraph sampling, query generation, and rubric generation are all done by an automated pipeline, which means the benchmark is extensible to new domains without hand-writing every case. That matters for a benchmark in this space, because deep research is domain-dependent and a hand-curated set of a few hundred questions would not generalize. Each of the 144 cases is a self-contained research task with its own reference tree and its own rubric set, so a single case already exercises a wide spread of knowledge directions, and the 24 domains are chosen to span the kinds of questions a professional deep-research agent would actually be asked.

The evaluation is scored by rubric judgments that the paper says agree closely with human annotations, and they report that agent rankings remain stable across the tested metric configurations, which is a useful robustness claim for a benchmark with this many rubrics. The stability across metric configurations matters more than it first appears: with tens of thousands of rubrics, the exact aggregation, how breadth and depth are combined, whether the geometric mean is used, can in principle reshuffle the rankings, and a benchmark whose ordering holds under that variation is one you can actually cite in a paper or a model card without worrying that a single arbitrary choice in the scoring function is what is driving the result. They evaluate 16 deep research agents, and the results show different strengths in breadth and depth rather than a single winner. The best-performing agent reaches 57.04 percent overall coverage, which is the number to hold onto: even the top system covers just over half of the reference structure. In the native-search LLM breakdown, the breadth and depth leaders are different models, which is the whole point of separating the two dimensions. One model leads on breadth while another leads on depth, and no model clearly dominates both, which is exactly the kind of profile a flat quality score would flatten into a single number and lose.

Where I would push back. First, the reference is Wikipedia-derived, which is a reasonable and reproducible choice but also a real limitation. Wikipedia's coverage of a topic is not a neutral ground truth for what a professional researcher should cover; it reflects what is documented, and for fast-moving or expert domains the tree may encode a stale or idiosyncratic notion of the major directions. The depth score in particular depends on the subgraph they sampled, and the geometric-mean aggregation inherits whatever bias is in those two numbers. A topic that Wikipedia treats as two separate branches but that an expert would see as one direction will get scored differently than the expert's intuition says it should, and there is no way to know that from the benchmark's own output. Second, the rubric judgments are automated, and the agreement-with-humans claim is what carries the whole validity of the score; I would want the agreement statistic, the number of human-verified rubric judgments, and the inter-rater or judge details before treating 57.04 percent as a hard ceiling. Third, 144 cases across 24 domains is about six cases per domain on average, so per-domain conclusions will have limited power, and the headline number is an aggregate that could be dominated by a few easier domains where the reference tree happens to be well-constructed.

What I would trust: the framing, which is correct that breadth and depth are a real, separable trade-off that flat quality scores collapse, and the method, which is to derive the rubrics from the reference structure so that coverage is measured against something with a known shape. The geometric mean of breadth and depth is a defensible aggregate and the automated, extensible pipeline is the part most likely to get reused by other groups. What I would re-verify: the human-agreement numbers behind the rubric judgments, the stability of rankings if you change the breadth-depth weighting instead of the geometric mean, and whether the Wikipedia-derived trees hold up in the specific domains you care about.

The bottom line: DRBench Pro is a well-motivated addition to the deep-research benchmark space, and its knowledge-tree approach to measuring coverage is the most interesting piece. The finding that the best agent covers only 57 percent of the reference, and that breadth and depth leaders differ, is a useful and honest signal that current deep-research systems are not yet reliable at comprehensive coverage, and it is the kind of result that a single aggregate score would never have surfaced. Treat the absolute numbers as provisional until the human-agreement evidence is stronger, and treat the breadth-depth separation as the part most likely to survive and get reused.
