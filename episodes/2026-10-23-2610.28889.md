The paper is Policy-Mask: Budget-Aware Adaptation of Diffusion Features to Discriminative Tasks, from Ivan Listopadov and Aibek Alanov at HSE University and the FusionBrain Lab, with Maksim Nakhodnov at Constructor University and co-affiliated with Moscow State University. This is a representation-extraction paper for a problem that keeps coming up in practice: you have a large frozen diffusion model, trained for generation, and you want its intermediate features to do a discriminative job, like segmentation or classification, without training a new encoder from scratch. The catch is that the useful signal is smeared across three axes at once. You have to pick a denoising timestep, because the feature at the start of the trajectory looks very different from the feature near the end; you have to pick a network block, because early and late layers encode different levels of abstraction; and you have to pick a subset of channels, because the feature maps are wide and most of the channels are not useful for your specific task. Existing methods either fix these by hand, or optimize one of them, usually the timestep, and then reuse the same choice across all inputs and all budgets. Policy-Mask replaces all three with a learned, budget-conditioned policy.

The setup is precise, which is part of the paper's value. The teacher is a frozen diffusion model, and the student is a small discriminative head. The policy takes the input image and a channel budget b, and outputs an action: a timestep t, a layer l, and a channel subset K of exactly b channels. The distillation loss, the downstream objective, is what trains the policy, and the teacher is never differentiated through. The action space is discrete and huge, the product of timesteps, layers, and the number of ways to choose b channels out of the full width, so the policy has to be trained with sampling rather than enumeration, and the paper uses exact-cardinality sampling to keep the channel count fixed at the budget. The key technical ingredient is a normalization fix. The channel score in the policy is a sum over the b selected channels, while the timestep score and the layer score are single terms, so without correction the channel head's gradient grows with b and dominates the other two heads during joint training. Dividing the channel score by b, the subset cardinality, converts the sum into a mean and puts all three heads on comparable scales. This looks like a small detail, but the paper shows it is load-bearing: the unnormalized joint policy underperforms even the timestep-only policy, meaning the joint optimization was actively broken before the fix.

The results are on three tasks. Segmentation on ADE20K, where the best fixed-extraction baseline in their setup lands around the low 30s in mean intersection-over-union, and Policy-Mask reaches 38.9 mIoU. Classification on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, with 75.4 percent top-1 on CIFAR-100 at a 128-channel budget, out of a 1280-channel width, so the student sees ten percent of the feature width. Semantic correspondence on SPair-71k, with 76.3 PCK at 0.1 under image normalization and 67.5 under bounding-box normalization. The matched-budget framing matters: these are comparisons against fixed-extraction and feature-filter baselines at the same channel budget, not against full-width extraction, so the claim is specifically that a learned, input- and budget-conditioned selection beats a hand-picked rule at equal storage cost.

The ablations are the interesting part. There is an action-axis ladder that isolates what each axis contributes. A timestep-only policy scores 31.78 mIoU. Extending to the timestep-and-layer grid raises it to 33.32, so layer selection adds about a point and a half. Adding a heuristic top-b channel subset by variance or Fisher score reaches 32.82, which is below the time-layer policy, telling you that heuristic channel selection is actively worse than none. The unnormalized joint policy lands at 31.68, below the timestep-only baseline, which is the failure the normalization fixes. With the channel-score normalization in place, the same joint policy reaches 37.7 plus or minus 0.7 over three seeds, against 36.8 plus or minus 0.6 without the 1-over-b factor. So the normalization contributes roughly a point, and the whole learned policy contributes about 6 points over the simplest viable baseline. On the budget axis, the best recorded score occurs at 256 retained channels, which the authors identify as a practical operating point: beyond that, the extra channels stop paying for their storage. They also adapt the policy to diffusion transformers, swapping the U-Net channels for the image-token hidden dimensions of PixArt-Sigma and SD3.5 MMDiT, which shows the formulation is architecture-agnostic, though the reported numbers there are thinner.

One design detail deserves emphasis because it is what separates this from a plain multi-teacher distillation. The policy is conditioned on the budget, not just the image. The same model, asked for 128 channels versus 256, produces different actions, and the training procedure samples across budgets so the learned policy interpolates along the storage-accuracy frontier rather than fixing one operating point. The feasible action set at budget b has a combinatorial size, the product of timesteps, layers, and the binomial coefficient choosing b channels out of the width, so the paper trains by sampling actions and applying the downstream distillation loss to the extracted features, with the teacher treated as a frozen feature provider. The exact-cardinality constraint is enforced in the sampling rather than with a penalty, which keeps the budget respected at inference without an extra projection step. This budget conditioning is the feature that makes the method a tool rather than a single tuned configuration: you can query the same policy for whatever channel budget your deployment allows, and the paper's budget-curve results, with the knee at 256 channels, tell you where to set it.

The distillation target is also worth noting, because it defines what the student actually learns. The student is trained to match the teacher's discriminative output through its own small head on the extracted features, so the policy is optimized end-to-end against the downstream objective rather than against a proxy like feature alignment. That means the policy can discover counterintuitive operating points, such as extracting from an early timestep at low budget where the semantic structure is still coarse but the channel selection carries the task signal. The training procedure is REINFORCE with a baseline: the task decoder evaluates the selected feature, its loss supplies a single scalar reward per sampled action, and the channel score is scaled by the reciprocal of the budget, which is the normalization in its final form. The held-out metrics, mIoU, top-1 accuracy, and PCK, remain pure evaluation metrics and never enter the policy's training signal, which the authors flag explicitly as a design choice to keep the reward honest.

A skeptical read. The contribution is real but narrow in one important sense: the gains over fixed extraction are modest in absolute terms, roughly 1 to 6 points depending on the task and the baseline, and the paper's strongest claim is really the matched-budget one, that learned selection dominates hand-tuned rules. I would want to see the comparison against full-width extraction with a correspondingly larger student, because if the full-width feature plus a bigger head beats the budgeted policy, the value proposition is purely a storage tradeoff, and the paper does not draw that line cleanly. The 31.68 below-baseline result for the unnormalized joint policy is a good honesty point, and it suggests the method is sensitive to training hyperparameters in ways the paper does not fully map; I would replicate the three-seed normalization ablation before building on it, because a point of mIoU is within seed variance on some datasets. The DiT adaptation is a sketch more than a study, with fewer settings reported, and I would not carry the ADE20K number over to the transformer-backbone regime without re-running it.

Bottom line: Policy-Mask is a small, well-specified contribution that turns the three-way feature-extraction choice into a learned, budget-conditioned policy, and the channel-score normalization is the detail that makes the joint training work. It is worth adopting if you are already committed to reusing a frozen diffusion backbone under a hard storage budget; for a from-scratch encoder, the gains are too small to justify the machinery.
