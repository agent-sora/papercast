Near-Zero Token Embeddings Silently Stall Training in Gated Sequence Models, by Xin Liu, Boyuan Sun, and Yuxin Wang, from Washington University in St. Louis and Nankai University, with the remaining authors also at Washington University in St. Louis, is a paper about a quiet failure mode in training modern gated sequence models, the family that includes Mamba, Gated DeltaNet, and xLSTM. The failure is this: some rows of the input embedding table end up with a norm so small they are effectively zero, and when a token with such a row appears in a batch, the gradients that flow back through it can be so large that the global gradient clip overflows to infinity and zeroes out the entire batch update. The model does not crash, it does not throw an error, it just silently skips updates on a meaningful fraction of training steps. The paper documents how widespread this is, why it happens, why transformers mostly escape it, and offers a two-line fix.

The prevalence survey is the opening result. They audit 291 open-weight language model repositories, 280 distinct embedding tables, and find that 52.6 percent of untied gated and hybrid checkpoints, which is 20 out of 38, contain reachable input embeddings with norms below one part per million, down in some cases to one part in 10 to the 36. By contrast, none of the weight-tied models have this problem, 0 out of 80 transformers and 0 out of 21 gated or hybrid models, and only 30.9 percent of untied transformers, 47 out of 152, have near-zero rows, and even those are mostly harmless. So the hazard is specific to the combination of an untied input embedding and a gated sequence architecture, and it is common enough that more than half of the open gated checkpoints they looked at carry it.

The mechanism has three parts, and the paper walks through each. First, how the rows get there. During pretraining, rare tokens and template tokens that never appear in a batch receive zero input gradient, and AdamW weight decay keeps shrinking their embedding vector toward zero on every step. With an untied embedding there is nothing to push it back, so the row decays until it is numerically negligible. Second, why that row is dangerous specifically in gated models. In a transformer, a near-zero input at the first position is tolerable, because subsequent positions are computed from their own embeddings and the model has a beginning-of-sequence token, so the near-zero row rarely gates anything important. In a gated sequence model the situation is different. The readout of the current token goes through factors, like the SiLU gate or a linear query, that are proportional to the input embedding. At a near-zero input those factors vanish, the current-token readout collapses, and the historical state readout shrinks with it. Meanwhile the residual norms across the stacked layers stay below the floor set by the residual layer normalization, and the pre-norm in each block multiplies backward gradients by up to 103 times per layer. The combination of a vanishing forward signal and an amplifying backward path means the gradient associated with one near-zero token row can exceed 1.84 times 10 to the 19. And that is the third part: once any single gradient entry exceeds that value, the FP32 global-norm clipping that PyTorch, the Hugging Face trainer, and FSDP version 2 all use by default overflows to infinity, and the clip rule zeroes every gradient in the batch. So one bad token row, present in the embedding table but only activated when that token shows up, discards the entire update.

They demonstrate the consequence on two concrete continual pretraining runs, which is where the controlled experiments live. They adapt the Falcon-H1-1.5B base model on FineWeb-2 Mongolian data and the Nemotron-3-Nano-4B model on FineWeb-2 Telugu data, each with FP32 master weights and BF16 autocast on H100 GPUs, across three random seeds, with a batch size of 32 packed sequences of length 512. On the Telugu run, the overflow discards batch updates on roughly 20 percent of steps, and the rows that trigger it stay frozen at their near-zero values for the rest of training, because the clipped batches are exactly the ones that would have updated them. They also verify the overflow behavior directly in single-step probes: PyTorch's native clipping, the Hugging Face trainer, and FSDP version 2 all produce an exact zero gradient update without raising any exception when the global norm overflows, so the failure is completely silent. That is the silent stall, the model keeps running, the loss still decreases on the other 80 percent of steps, and nothing in the logs flags that a fifth of the updates never happened.

The fixes are small, which is part of the paper's value. The first is GroupClip, which decouples the input-embedding gradient from the global clip. Instead of folding the embedding gradient into the same FP32 norm as the rest of the model, it computes the embedding's own norm in 64-bit or rescaled arithmetic, so the enormous but legitimate embedding gradient no longer overflows the shared accumulator. It is about three lines of code. In their runs it eliminates the lost updates and, because the triggered rows now actually receive gradients, it heals the near-zero rows in a single step. On the Telugu adaptation, after 1,000 steps, the model's accuracy on the Belebele reading benchmark is 30.67 percent under the 64-bit GroupClip variant, against 29.78 percent zero-shot, so the fix recovers real learning rather than just preventing a failure. The second remedy is GateFloor, which adds a small constant, between 0.01 and 0.1, inside the SiLU gate, so the gate stays positive even at a zero input. That alone cuts the near-zero gradient spikes by more than 28 orders of magnitude, up to models of 370 million parameters, with perplexity comparable to the unmodified baseline. The two fixes address the problem from opposite directions, and they are complementary rather than competing: GroupClip changes the training arithmetic so the existing near-zero rows can be updated, while GateFloor changes the architecture so new near-zero rows are far less able to generate the overflow in the first place. A production training stack could plausibly adopt both, with GateFloor as a cheap architectural guard and GroupClip as a safety net on the optimizer.

The skeptical read. The survey is a static audit of checkpoints, so it measures prevalence, not impact. A checkpoint can carry 20 near-zero rows and never activate them, if the tokens are truly absent from the downstream data, in which case the hazard is latent rather than active. The paper's own audit finds that 92 percent of the 979 sampled near-zero tokens still have healthy output-head norms, which is consistent with the rows being inert unless the input side activates them, so the real-world hit rate depends on how often those specific rare tokens appear in a given training corpus. The 20 percent step-discard number is from one continual pretraining setup on one model, and the fraction will vary with token frequency, sequence packing, and clip threshold. The GroupClip fix changes the training arithmetic, and any change to gradient clipping is worth re-checking against a longer run, because a clip that no longer zeroes batches also no longer provides that crude safety valve on genuinely exploding steps. The GateFloor constant is a small but permanent architecture change, and choosing the 0.01 to 0.1 range is itself a hyperparameter the paper validates only at modest scale. None of that undercuts the core contribution, which is that a failure mode this common and this quiet, invisible to logs and to loss curves, had not been characterized before, and that the fix is cheap enough to be adopted by default.

Bottom line: this is a diagnostic paper that turns an invisible training failure, silent batch drops from one near-zero embedding row overflowing the global gradient clip, into a measured, fixable defect, with a three-line clip change and a one-constant gate change that both work at reported scale. If you train or fine-tune any untied Mamba-family model, audit your embedding norms before you trust your step count.
