## StateM: Harness Scaling Without New Weights

First, StateM. Long-horizon agents fail even when the underlying model can solve every step: they lose track of mutable state, forget lessons from earlier runs, skip known procedures, stop early. The bet here is harness scaling — improve the execution system around the agent without touching its weights. StateM is an agent-native runtime organizing execution around durable states, phase-local context, checked transitions, recoverable runbooks, and versioned procedural practices that both agents and users can inspect.

The numbers: on Terminal-Bench Two Point One it raises GPT Five Point Five xhigh to ninety-two percent against an eighty-three percent reference; with GPT Five Point Six Sol xhigh it reaches ninety-five point three percent raw accuracy across four hundred forty-five trials, succeeding on all eighty-nine tasks at least once. The same runbook transfers unchanged across models — and the economics are wild: under fifteen dollars of API usage versus five hundred seventy-five for the GPT reference run. Runbooks built from postmortems become executable preconditions. That's the pattern worth copying: postmortem findings that persist as enforced state, not as wiki pages nobody reads.

## DumpsterCluster: LLaMA-70B on Sixty-Dollar GPUs

Second, DumpsterCluster — my favourite title of the week. As AI datacenters retire functional GPUs, huge quantities of capable accelerators hit secondary markets. The authors physically built a one-hundred-twenty-eight-GPU cluster from entirely second-hand components — V100s at around sixty dollars each — and ran it for a full year. Total cost: about twenty-two thousand dollars, versus six hundred thousand for an eight-GPU B200 system.

With pipeline-parallel optimizations, their cluster achieves competitive LLaMA-70B throughput — production viability demonstrated. But the honest findings matter as much as the demo: older GPUs burn far more energy per token, so total cost of ownership wins only where electricity is cheap, and under grid-average carbon intensity, second-hand systems emit roughly four times more carbon per token on small models — over forty times on seventy-billion-parameter models. GPU afterlife is real but not automatically green: pair old silicon with clean, cheap power or don't bother.

## ClawGym II: Black-Box RL Through Opaque Harnesses

Third, ClawGym Two. Training agents through complex harnesses is attractive — the harness is where deployment happens — but the harness is also a black box that breaks standard policy-gradient assumptions. Their framework sandboxes task environments for massive concurrent rollouts, places a serving proxy at the model boundary to capture calls, reconstructs multi-turn trajectories into prefix trees, and adapts both critic-based PPO and critic-free GRPO to optimize over the recovered tree structure while maintaining training-inference consistency. There's even mix-harness training: one model jointly optimized by heterogeneous harnesses.

With Qwen Three Thirty-A3B, black-box RL adds ten to fifteen Pass-at-one points through OpenClaw and Claude Code respectively, stable over two to four hundred optimization steps, with consistent gains transferring to harder suites like JobBench and OfficeQA. Together with yesterday's harnessed-RL papers, a clear consensus is forming: train where you deploy, treat the harness as part of the environment, and recover the policy's view of its own trajectory from the proxy.

## SA-MRPO: Spend Gradient Where It's Not Saturated

Fourth paper, SA-MRPO — Saturation Aware Advantage Reweighting for Multi-Reward Policy Optimization. Group-relative RL has become the default for training reasoners, but multi-objective setups usually scalarize rewards into one fixed weighted sum. Two problems follow: rollouts with different reward profiles can receive identical advantages, and training keeps allocating gradient budget to objectives that are already solved instead of ones with headroom left.

SA-MRPO standardizes each objective independently and adaptively discounts its contribution by batch-level saturation estimates — dynamically reallocating effort toward under-optimized objectives, sometimes reversing an update's sign outright. On maths benchmarks with multi-reward combinations it beats GDPO in twelve of fifteen comparisons, gains up to nine percent on AMC23, and lifts coding pass rates while keeping already-satisfied objectives near their levels. If your reward vector has more than one entry, fixed weights are quietly wasting your compute budget.

## How Do Agents Fail on AutoResearch?

Fifth, "How Do Agents Fail on AutoResearch?" — one hundred tasks grounded in published frontier science, eight harness-model combinations, eight hundred fully annotated trajectories, distilled into a taxonomy of forty-five empirically-grounded failure patterns spanning ideation, retrieval, execution, analysis, writing, and review.

The convergence is striking: across every combination — including the strongest models — failures centre on one overarching deficit: current agents lack a metacognitive loop. They cannot reliably check what they produced against what they found, revise when it doesn't hold up, or question whether their path was sound. And because the same patterns recur everywhere, the deficit sits at the model level, not in any particular scaffold. If you build agentic pipelines, this taxonomy is a ready-made checklist for instrumentation — and the metacognitive-loop framing tells you exactly which capability to test before trusting longer horizons.

## R-Cubed-Bench: One Budget, Many Problems

Last today, R-Cubed-Bench. Real deployments give an agent a shared budget, not unlimited retries per problem — yet most benchmarks evaluate independent per-task budgets. This benchmark scores six-problem suites under shared budgets across mathematics, competitive programming, and abstract reasoning, then calibrates against each model's own single-problem curves to build an empirical oracle.

The result: across seventy-two main-table cells for six models, the oracle matches or beats the actual contest mean in every cell — strictly higher in seventy-one of them. Under pressure, simple equal-allocation replay often exceeds letting the model decide. Trajectory diagnostics show limited strategy updating and pressure-dependent collapse patterns, and no scheduling policy dominates across domains. Demonstrated competence, it turns out, does not survive contact with a budget. Anyone serving agents metered by tokens should read this as a warning label.
