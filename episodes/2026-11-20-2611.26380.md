EgoCaptioner, by Yuqi Tang, Yang Shi, and Yuran Wang, at HKUST Guangzhou, KlingAI Research, and Peking University, is a model and data pipeline for egocentric video captioning that targets the two things current multimodal large language models do badly on first-person video: fine-grained detail and accurate timing. The problem is that a person wearing a camera produces a video where actions are brief, their order matters, and the wearer's own motion confuses the model, and existing captioning formats do not support combining a detailed narration with precise timestamps. EgoCaptioner describes each video on two levels at once: a video-level overview of the setting and the overall activity, and a set of detailed, time-stamped steps that break the video into subtasks with their boundaries, which is the format that lets you both read what happened and locate when it happened.

The prior-art gap is a format problem as much as a model problem. Most video captioners produce a single flowing paragraph, which loses the temporal structure, or they produce a list of segments without a coherent overview, which loses the global context. The egocentric case is harder than third-person video because the camera is on the actor, so the actor's hands and body are in frame constantly and the model has to attribute actions to the wearer rather than to a visible subject, and brief actions, like picking up a phone, happen fast enough that a model sampling at a low frame rate misses them. The two-level output is the design answer: the overview captures the setting and the activity arc, and the detailed steps capture each subtask with its time interval, so the caption is comprehensive and temporally precise at the same time.

The data construction pipeline is the part I would study most, because it is where the quality comes from. They build the training data by annotating egocentric video with a coarse-to-fine two-model pipeline. Gemini 3.1 Pro writes the overview from the full video, and a separate model, Seed 2.1 Pro, identifies the events, their temporal boundaries and types, and an outline of the steps for each event. Then Gemini expands each outline entry into a detailed caption. The inputs are drawn from egocentric datasets, including Ego4D Goal-Step, the Ego4D subset of EgoHOD, Sekai, and EgoVid 5M, which is a mix of established egocentric corpora. Two implementation details matter. Videos longer than 30 seconds are annotated in clips of at most 30 seconds with 0.5 seconds of overlap, and Gemini merges the clip annotations back into a whole-video caption, which is the standard way to handle long videos without blowing the context window, and the 0.5 second overlap is what prevents an action that straddles a clip boundary from being split or lost. The frames are sampled uniformly across the video at a target rate of 4 frames per second, capped at 240 frames, so for longer videos the effective sampling rate drops to preserve full temporal coverage rather than truncating the tail.

The training is a two-stage recipe, supervised fine-tuning followed by group relative policy optimization. The ablations show that SFT alone already brings large gains in the step-segmentation metric, which they call PSJ, the permissible subsegment Jaccard score, and that the data scale matters in a specific way: even 10,000 triplets recover most of the PSJ improvement you get at 50,000, scoring 67.79 versus 71.63, which tells you the segmentation ability saturates early and the additional data mostly buys the content quality, measured by METEOR and SODA. Then GRPO, the reinforcement learning stage, lifts METEOR, SODA, and PSJ further. The GRPO objective has multiple reward terms, and the ablation isolates them: there is a timing reward that constrains the temporal boundaries, and a style reward, and when you drop the timing reward, PSJ falls below the SFT-only baseline, which is a direct demonstration that the reinforcement learning is what enforces the temporal precision, not the supervised stage. That is a clean result, because it says the two stages are doing different jobs, SFT for content and segmentation structure, RL for temporal fidelity.

The evaluation results. EgoCaptioner scores 78.84 on their overall metric, the best among open-source models and above Gemini 3.1 Pro, which is a strong claim because Gemini 3.1 Pro is the model used to build much of the training data, so beating it on the same task family is meaningful. On EgoHome, the step segmentation benchmark, it scores 75.51 on PSJ, ranking first among open-source models, and it outperforms Gemini 3.8 Flash. On the fine-grained fact benchmarks, EgoTempo and Minerva Ego, which probe details like which object was manipulated, it beats Gemini 3.1 Pro by 16.8 percentage points in EgoTempo accuracy, which is the largest single gap and the one that most directly supports the fine-grained-detail claim. The comparison set spans proprietary models like Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and GPT-6 Astra, open-source captioners like Tarsier2 7B, CapRL Video 4B, and AVoCaDO, and egocentric models like EgoThinker and EgoGPT, and the open-source captioners Tarsier2, AVoCaDO, and EgoGPT score zero on the timing benchmarks, which is a reminder that temporal grounding is not something the existing open models do at all.

The skeptical reading. First, and this is the big one, the model is built on data annotated by Gemini 3.1 Pro, and then it is reported to beat Gemini 3.1 Pro on the same task family, so part of the win is a data-advantage artifact, because the training distribution was shaped by the reference model's own outputs, and you would want a comparison on a task or a dataset where the reference model did not contribute to the training data to know how much of the 78.84 is genuine capability versus self-reinforcement. Second, the 16.8 point EgoTempo gap is large, but it is on a single fine-grained benchmark, and the PSJ metric, while standard for segmentation, measures agreement with a permissible set of subsegments rather than exact timing, so a model can score well on PSJ by being approximately right about boundaries without being precise to the frame. Third, the 4 frames per second, 240 frame cap is a real constraint, and for very long or very fast egocentric videos the effective sampling rate drops, which is exactly the regime where brief actions are most likely to be missed, so the fine-grained-detail claim is strongest on the medium-length videos that dominate the training data and weakest on the long, fast ones.

For implementation, the two-level caption format is the reusable idea, because it is what makes the output both comprehensive and temporally addressable, and the coarse-to-fine two-model annotation pipeline is the data recipe to copy if you are building your own egocentric captioning set, with the 30-second clip, 0.5-second overlap, and 4 fps, 240 frame cap as the concrete parameters to start from. The SFT-then-GRPO split with a timing reward is the training recipe, and the ablation that the timing reward is what drives PSJ above SFT is the justification for keeping the RL stage, so if you are on a tight compute budget, the SFT stage gets you most of the segmentation and the RL stage is what buys the temporal precision.

To summarize the bottom line. EgoCaptioner is a well-engineered approach to egocentric video captioning, with a two-level format that combines overview and time-stamped steps, a coarse-to-fine annotation pipeline, and a clean separation where SFT buys segmentation and reinforcement learning with a timing reward buys temporal precision. Its limits are the self-reinforcement risk of beating the very model that built its data, the single-benchmark nature of the largest gap, and the frame-rate cap that weakens the fine-grained claim on long fast video. If you are building egocentric captioning, the format and the timing-reward RL are the parts to take.
