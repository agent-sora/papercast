Scaling Mathematical Proof Supervision from Structured Textbooks, by Youqing Fang, Zhouqi Hua, and Yinhao Tang, from the University of Science and Technology of China, Fudan University, and Shanghai AI Laboratory, with collaborators at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, is a data paper about what happens when you stop flattening mathematics textbooks into isolated problem-and-solution pairs and instead train on the books the way they are actually organized.

The claim is straightforward. Most mathematical supervision for language models arrives as uniform problem-to-solution pairs, whether the seed is a competition problem, a grade-school word problem, or a synthetic augmentation. The organization of the source disappears. A textbook is not a bag of solved problems; it has a chapter hierarchy, a proposition-and-proof structure, and prerequisite relations that reflect the actual dependency graph of the subject. The authors argue that the value of supervision is determined not just by the number of examples but by how the examples are constructed and ordered, and they build a corpus and a set of controlled experiments to test whether textbook structure carries real signal.

The corpus is called TOME-Math, which stands for Textbook-Oriented Mathematical Evidence. It is built from 2,118 mathematics textbooks, selected against university course syllabi and reviewed by domain experts, and it spans 29 subject categories. The pipeline converts each PDF into structured markdown with page images, reconstructs a hierarchical table of contents, and runs a state machine over the body text to recover chapter boundaries with retries on ambiguous pages. Exercises are carved out into a separate reservoir and deliberately kept out of the proof-supervision path.

There are two streams of supervision. The Proof QA stream has 205,861 records: theorem-proof, lemma-proof, and definition-proof units extracted from the core concepts of each chapter, with the earlier propositions a proof depends on retrieved and attached, so each record is source-grounded and self-contained. The Gap QA stream has 174,431 records. For each proof, a larger model is asked to find gaps, meaning implicit or missing reasoning steps, and when a substantive gap is found it is rewritten as a self-contained proposition with a completed proof. That gap stream is the interesting part: it adds explicit supervision for the intermediate reasoning that a textbook author leaves unstated. The whole corpus is 380,292 records. The yield on the gap stream is low: 174,431 retained out of 918,235 generated candidates, about 19 percent, which tells you the gap filter is doing real work. Quality control runs a source-conditioned judge over five axes, completeness, accuracy, formatting, self-containment, and atomicity, and invented proof steps are treated as hard failures, not stylistic defects.

For evaluation they use Qwen3 30B A3B Base as the backbone for everything, which is the right call for a controlled data study. They build TOME-LONGFORM, 300 proof-heavy problems drawn from 64 held-out books, with every one of those 15,661 associated training records removed from the training split to enforce book-level separation, and they score with a reference-guided step verifier, 16 solutions per problem, each reviewed eight times and accepted only if no review finds a substantive error. They also run TOME-OBJECTIVE and four public benchmarks: MiniF2F, ProofNet, MATH-500, and AIME 2026.

The headline number is the six-benchmark unweighted mean. The base model sits at 29.17 percent. After fine-tuning on the TOME-Math training split, which is 364,631 examples, the mean rises to 45.83 percent, a gain of about 16.7 percentage points. That is a large number. The comparison baselines are three public instruction corpora, OpenMath1 at 1.82 million examples, MetaMath at 395 thousand, and MathInstruct at 262 thousand, all independently fine-tuned from the same checkpoint for one epoch. TOME-Math beats all of them on five of the six benchmarks and lands second only on AIME 2026. The size comparison is the one to sit with: OpenMath1 is roughly 4.8 times larger than TOME-Math and it loses on all six benchmarks. So the gain is not explained by raw example count.

But the abstract framing that corpus size does not explain the ranking is honest about its limits, and the paper says so explicitly. The three public corpora differ from TOME-Math in several respects at once, so this single comparison does not isolate textbook structure as the causal factor. That is what the controlled studies in section 5 are for.

The first controlled study is training-free, structure-guided retrieval. The frozen base model is given a fixed context-token budget under five policies: closed book, random pages from the same book, the local chapter and its predecessors, TOME proposition-and-proof records selected by chapter position and recovered dependencies, and the same plus the gap records. At a matched budget, pass-at-one climbs from 13.25 percent closed book to 17.38 percent with the full proposition-plus-gap policy, a gain of 4.13 points. The gap records add a further 1.84 points over propositions alone. Random same-book context is a wash and sometimes hurts, which is the cleanest evidence in the paper that mere access to nearby material is not the mechanism; the selection by structure is.

The second study is curriculum. Across six subjects and three per-subject token budgets of 750 thousand, 1.8 million, and 2.4 million tokens, they reorder the same frozen set of question-answer pairs under four schedules: shuffled, forward with gap-first, reverse with gap-first, and forward mixed. Forward-gap is the strongest schedule in all 18 subject-scale cells. Its macro-average margin over shuffle widens with scale, from 4.34 points at the smallest budget to 7.72 at the largest. The controlled comparisons attribute most of that to chapter direction: forward beats reverse by 5.65, 7.41, and 8.50 points across the three scales. The gap-first ordering, holding direction fixed, adds a smaller consistent 1.53 to 2.37 points. So if you only take one lesson from this paper, it is that training in textbook order, definitions before theorems before proofs, beats both shuffling and reverse order, and that effect grows with data volume.

The third study is source breadth and transfer. At a fixed token budget, spreading the same number of training tokens across 16 books instead of 8 books buys a gain of roughly 1.6 to 4.8 points depending on subject. And the cross-subject transfer coefficients are mostly near or above 1, meaning a subject-specific checkpoint transfers to other subjects at near-full strength, with a couple of exceptions below 0.6.

Now the skeptical part. This is a data and training-order paper, and the strongest claim, that structure causes the gain, is supported by ablations that hold the example set fixed, which is good, but the full-corpus headline of 16.7 points is a comparison against three external corpora that are not matched on anything except the backbone and the epoch count. Those external baselines use different source distributions, different solution formats, and different difficulty mixes, so the aggregate win is real but it is not a clean causal estimate. The retrieval and curriculum studies are the ones that would survive scrutiny, because they change one thing at a time under a matched budget. A second caveat is the verifier: the entire long-form evaluation rests on an LLM step verifier running eight reviews, and the paper reports acceptance rates rather than a human-validated subset of the same problems, so the absolute scores on TOME-LONGFORM inherit whatever bias the verifier has. The 300-problem held-out set is also small enough that a few-point swing is plausible run-to-run. And the gap records are themselves generated by a frontier model, so the gap stream is a distillation of that model's notion of what a proof should contain; if you are trying to train a model to be genuinely more rigorous than its teacher, you have to assume the gap completion is at least as sound as the teacher.

Engineering-wise, the pipeline is a stack of separate models doing OCR, table-of-contents recovery, extraction, gap detection, and judging, and the paper points to appendices for the full module logic and model allocation, so reproducing it exactly will require those. The corpus is promised for release, which is the part that will actually get used.

Bottom line: the controlled retrieval and curriculum experiments make a credible case that textbook ordering and explicit intermediate proof obligations are real, reproducible sources of mathematical supervision, and the forward-curriculum effect in particular is the kind of cheap lever worth trying on any math fine-tune. The full-corpus headline is large but confounded by uncontrolled baseline differences, so I would weight the matched-budget ablations far more heavily than the 16.7-point aggregate when deciding whether this changes anything.
