RISE: Adaptive Imagination for World Action Models, by Hongbo Lu, Liang Yao, Chenghao He, together with Hao Han, Fan Liu, Wenlong Liao, Tao He, and Pai Peng at COWARobot Co. Ltd, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and Hohai University. The paper appeared on the Hugging Face daily papers list for August 25, 2026 with twenty-three upvotes, and ships a project page, a code repository, and a dataset release. The claim is narrow and implementable: World Action Models waste computation by granting every driving scene the same imagination budget, and a small learned scheduler that decides, step by step, whether to keep imagining the future or stop and plan, buys better planning at lower rollout cost.

Context first. End-to-end driving is moving away from direct trajectory regression toward policies that reason about the future before acting. World Action Models predict world evolution and judge actions by their consequences rather than by the current observation alone. At inference there are three established arrangements of that computation. Shared-backbone designs run prediction and planning from one representation, an imagine-and-plan scheme. Cascaded designs complete an entire future rollout before handing it to the planner, imagine-then-plan. A third family removes test-time imagination and plans directly from the observation. The paper's observation is that all three fix the schedule globally: nothing reassesses, after each predicted step, whether the partial future built so far already supports a good plan. The missing quantity gets a name, Future Planning Gain: the change in planning score obtainable from each valid continuation relative to stopping with the current prefix. Because every predicted latent updates the available evidence, that quantity changes during rollout, so the authors argue it should be re-estimated after each step and used as a sequential stopping signal, not folded into a fixed depth chosen in advance. The decision is deliberately local: roll when expected improvement justifies its cost, otherwise stop, and the effective horizon emerges from repeating that choice.

The base model is a conventional encoder, predictor, planner stack, which matters because the contribution is meant to be portable. The encoder is a frozen V-JEPA 2 ViT-L vision transformer encoding front-camera footage: four frames resized to 256 by 512 pixels, sixteen by sixteen patches, non-overlapping two-frame tubelets, producing two observed latent steps of 512 spatial tokens with feature dimension 1024. The predictor is a twelve-layer transformer, hidden width 384, twelve heads, conditioned on relative ego-pose changes between adjacent frames; it autoregressively appends future latent steps, up to four on NAVSIM and three on nuScenes, each covering two raw video frames, with no route command or auxiliary state input. The planner is a twelve-layer diffusion transformer of the same width. It consumes the observed tokens plus the selected future prefix, distinguished by learned source embeddings, alongside the ego-motion history and current kinematics, then generates six candidate trajectories through a variance-preserving diffusion process sampled in twenty DPM-Solver++ steps. Each candidate holds eight poses at half-second intervals represented as x, y, and the cosine and sine of heading.

The new component is the scheduler, two small heads reading the latent stream. At depth h the Latent Evaluator takes the observed latent and the imagined prefix and emits two vectors: a Risk Profile, one risk estimate per prefix depth reached so far, and a Future Planning Gain profile, one expected score delta per possible continuation depth, each measured against stopping now. The Rollout Gate receives pooled latents, both profiles, the normalized depth, cumulative computation cost, the marginal cost of one more step, and a scalar lambda encoding how strongly compute is penalized. Its output sign is the decision: roll once more if positive, stop otherwise. On stop, the chosen prefix undergoes a light risk refinement whenever its depth exceeds zero, implemented as norm-constrained gradient descent on the summed predicted risk for two steps at step size 0.05 with update norm capped at 0.25, followed by a single pass through the planner. Training runs in three stages. Stage one trains the predictor with teacher-encoded targets on real and counterfactual clips, then trains an initial planner exposed to random depths spanning zero to the maximum, so it learns variable-prefix planning from the start. Stage two manufactures supervision by enumeration: the initial planner proposes candidates at every prefix depth, a fixed geometry-based scorer supplies risk targets fit with a Huber loss, verified factual-counterfactual pairs add a margin ranking loss active after the annotated incident onset, and a localization term calibrates risk timing. Once risks are learned, every prefix is refined, a second planner trains at all depths, and the planning-gain targets come from actually scoring that planner when stopped at each depth, yielding realized per-depth score deltas. Stage three freezes everything and fits the gate as a cost-aware stopping policy: for each sample, enumerate all depths, compute the best cost-adjusted gain as the maximum over continuations of score delta minus lambda times cost delta, label continuation positive only when that maximum exceeds zero, and train with binary cross entropy. Implementation is PyTorch with bfloat16 precision on eight A100 GPUs at per-GPU batch size four; stage learning rates are 0.00002, 0.00005, and 0.001, and lambda is swept from zero to 0.05 with 0.005 as the default.

Risk supervision is the second contribution, because factual logs record only the future that occurred. The team built CounterDrive, a counterfactual dataset over selected NAVSIM and nuScenes scenes. For each source key frame, the prompt combines a fixed instruction, a scene description, and an incident description naming the event location and involved object, while constraining camera viewpoint, road geometry, background, and initial traffic configuration so the generated clip stays anchored to its source. Wan 2.7 produces a ten-second clip at 1080p resolution, sampled at two hertz into twenty frames. OpenVO recovers frame-wise ego poses, actions follow from adjacent poses, and human annotators verify motion consistency, mark the first incident frame, flag generation distortions, and categorize each clip as normal, caused by non-ego behavior, or caused by the ego vehicle, recording recommended avoidance actions for the ego-caused cases. Clips with severe distortion or unreliable motion are dropped. The yield after filtering: 2,432 training and 511 test clips from nuScenes, 5,013 training and 1,000 test from NAVSIM, each paired with its factual source within the selected subset only.

Now the numbers. On NAVSIM version one RISE reaches 91.5 PDMS, ahead of the strongest prior system, DriveFuture at 90.7, with sharp component gains: 98.3 efficiency and 98.6 time-to-collision against 84.8 and 95.7 for EponaV2, previously the best overall at 90.4. On NAVSIM version two it posts 90.8 EPDMS, 0.9 points clear of the field, ranking first or tied first on seven of nine components. On nuScenes it records the lowest average L2 error, 0.31 meters, and the lowest average collision rate, 0.10. Ablations credit both pieces: CounterDrive alone lifts EPDMS from 88.9 to 89.8, the scheduler alone reaches 90.4, and together they hit 90.8 alongside 91.5 PDMS. Against cheaper stopping heuristics the cost accounting is explicit: random stopping averages 2.03 rollouts at 264 milliseconds for 89.5 EPDMS, a latent-convergence criterion spends 2.98 rollouts and 308.5 milliseconds for 89.7, and the scheduler lands between at 2.40 rollouts and 287.4 milliseconds for 90.8. The necessity study is the most persuasive result: scoring every sample at every fixed depth shows 1,248 scenes peaking at zero rollout while 4,036 peak at depth three and 2,180 at depth four; forcing the zero-depth group to depth four degrades it from 89.9 to 88.4, while extending the depth-four group from zero to four raises it from 88.5 to 91.1. Two further checks support generality. Placing the scheduler into DAWN, a different world-action interactive model, without modifying its predictor or planner moves PDMS from 89.1 to 90.3, with efficiency up 2.7 and time-to-collision up 2.3. And on a held-out counterfactual test set, the model trained without CounterDrive ranks hazard against safe futures barely above chance, area under the curve between 0.49 and 0.52 with accident recognition at 0.51, while adding CounterDrive moves those to 0.93 through 0.96 and 0.96.

Skeptical notes before anyone adopts this. The risk targets derive from the same geometry-based scorer the pipeline uses for candidate selection, so the evaluator learns to predict one fixed rule-based metric, and the entire adaptive apparatus optimizes agreement with that metric rather than an external notion of safety; if deployment scoring differs, the gate's decisions inherit the mismatch. The efficiency figure on NAVSIM version one, 98.3 against a field clustered in the low eighties, is the kind of number I would reproduce independently before citing. Counterfactual clips come from a video generation model, so supervision quality is bounded by generator fidelity despite human filtering, and the authors concede coverage is partial: pairs exist only within the selected subset, not one-to-one across the NAVSIM training set. Reported latency covers rollout, so end-to-end wall-clock including refinement and the diffusion planner still needs measurement on target hardware. The limitations section is candid that evaluation covers autonomous driving only, leaving other domains untested. Bottom line: the central finding, that the optimal rollout depth varies enough across scenes that any fixed budget is wrong somewhere, is demonstrated cleanly and packaged as a cheap plug-in. If you operate world-model planners under latency constraints, reimplementing the gate against your own scoring function is a contained experiment with a credible expected payoff.
