Adaptive Reward Routing. The first three authors are Songlin Yang, Xiaotong Zhao, and Jiacheng Zhang, working between MMLab at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Tencent Video, and the University of Hong Kong.

The problem is multi-reward reinforcement learning post-training for joint audio-video diffusion models. You are fine-tuning a model like LTX-2, which generates audio and video from text prompts through two separate streams connected by bidirectional cross-attention, and you want it to satisfy several objectives at once: video quality, audio quality, text-to-audio alignment, and audio-video synchronization. The standard approach is to attach a reward model to each objective and optimize them jointly. But two things change during training that fixed recipes cannot track. First, where the updates should land. The layers and tokens through which the audio and video streams talk to each other shift as the model fine-tunes, so a routing map computed at initialization goes stale. Second, how the rewards should be balanced. The objectives disagree on the same sample, and the right balance moves as optimization proceeds. GDPO normalizes each reward independently but keeps fixed aggregation weights, so it cannot react to conflict. MARBLE adapts weights from gradient geometry, but its coefficients reflect gradient compatibility rather than what the user actually cares about, and the paper shows a concrete failure mode: a geometry-only weight for the synchronization reward drifts toward zero, which silently discards an essential objective.

The evidence that the problem is real comes from two probes run on the base LTX-2 model and an OmniNFT-trained checkpoint. In the forward pass, they ablate the key and value outputs of the cross-attention layers in ranges and measure the desynchronization penalty, which rises when you remove layers that matter for synchronization, and the base and trained models show different critical layers. In the backward pass, they measure the L2 norm of the cross-modal key and value gradients per layer, and the peaks move between base and trained. In other words, the cross-modal function and the gradient flow both relocate during fine-tuning, which is exactly why static routing fails. The third piece of evidence is the conflict map itself. They normalize each reward's per-prompt preference and look at where two rewards disagree, meaning their normalized preferences have opposite signs. The off-diagonal entries are large, so the rewards genuinely conflict on the same sample, and the fourth is the gradient-based weight drift: when you derive reward weights purely from gradient geometry, the synchronization weight tracks near zero for much of training, which is the signature of an essential objective being suppressed by a geometry-only rule.

The fix has two components. The first is Cross-Modal Influence-Guided Routing, which decides where the update should land. Instead of disabling the audio-to-video or video-to-audio paths and measuring the change, which would cost extra forward passes, they use a quantity the model already computes: the pre-gate response of each cross-attention path, collected over the intermediate-to-late denoising steps and detached before backprop. At the token level, each target token's response is averaged across the relevant layers and timesteps, normalized with a percentile-clipped min-max, and turned into a positive loss weight, so cross-modally influential tokens pull more of the loss. Audio responses are normalized globally, video responses per frame, to stop frame-level magnitude differences from dominating. At the layer level, the same response averaged over tokens becomes a soft detachment coefficient. The routed key and value tensors are a convex blend of a stop-gradient copy and the live tensor, so the forward value is unchanged but the backward gradient through that layer is scaled down for weakly coupled layers and preserved for influential ones. The two directions are routed independently.

The second component is Preference-Preserving Modality-Aware Reweighting, which decides how the rewards combine. Each reward is probed only through the branch it supervises, with token routing disabled during the probe so the measured gradient geometry is not biased by the current token weights. A per-branch MARBLE solve produces a conflict-aware coefficient, which is smoothed over successive estimates. After a warm-up round, that coefficient is applied as a residual correction on top of the user's predefined prior weight rather than replacing it. The prior therefore sets a nonzero floor, so a weak but essential objective cannot be starved just because its early gradient is noisy, while the residual term adapts to the current conflict. The whole thing runs on top of DiffusionNFT, a forward-process reinforcement objective that builds implicit positive and negative policies and uses group-relative advantages, so there is no learned value function, only whether a sample beat its peers for the same prompt.

For the experiments they use two backbones, LTX-2 at 19 billion parameters and LTX-2.3 at 22 billion, post-trained on 19,487 audio-video prompts from a VGGSound-derived corpus, optimizing five reward signals drawn from VideoAlign, HPSv3, AudioBox Aesthetics, CLAP, and a DeSync-based synchronization term. The baselines cover the axes the paper cares about: the pretrained backbone with no post-training, fixed reward coordination via GDPO, geometry-based reweighting via MARBLE, and OmniNFT, which adds modality-wise credit assignment but keeps its layer routing fixed from the base model. All numbers are means and standard deviations over three seeds. On JavisBench with LTX-2, the base model scores 0.604 on the desynchronization penalty, and lower is better here. GDPO actually makes it worse, 0.671, and MARBLE lands at 0.618, only marginally better than the base. OmniNFT gets to 0.390, and this is the strongest prior baseline. Adaptive Reward Routing reaches 0.341, with a JavisScore of 0.206 against 0.195 for OmniNFT, and leads on most of the consistency metrics as well. The pattern repeats on LTX-2.3.

The ablation isolates the two axes. Token weighting alone drops the penalty from 0.604 to 0.482, adding layer scale brings it to 0.366, and the full routing stack reaches 0.343. The reweighting side, built on MARBLE, moves from 0.492 with branch-aware balancing to 0.369 once warm-up is added. The complete model combines the two. They also validate the response proxy directly: disabling each of the 48 cross-attention blocks one at a time and comparing the ranking to the proxy gives Spearman correlations of 0.98 and 0.97, and the proxy recomputed from the current model stays above 0.96 throughout fine-tuning while the version frozen at initialization falls to as low as 0.38. Blocking the top-scoring ten percent of tokens changes the final prediction 1.62 times more than a random group for the audio-to-video direction, confirming the proxy identifies the tokens that actually matter.

Skeptically, the gains over the best prior baseline, OmniNFT, are real but modest, a desynchronization penalty of 0.341 against 0.390, and the routing machinery is the part doing most of the work, which means the reweighting contribution is comparatively small and the warm-up is doing a lot of the heavy lifting. The method is also tied to architectures where you can identify modality tokens and isolate directional cross-attention responses, so it does not transfer to models with inseparable modality representations, which the authors acknowledge. If you are post-training a dual-stream audio-video diffusion model and your rewards are genuinely in conflict, this is a reasonable thing to try, but I would re-verify the reward-model calibration first, because every number here inherits the biases of VideoAlign, HPSv3, and the DeSync proxy, and the absolute quality scores are low enough that a single seed's noise could reorder a few of the consistency columns.

The bottom line: Adaptive Reward Routing treats multi-reward diffusion post-training as a dynamic credit-assignment problem and, by re-localizing updates and re-balancing rewards as the model evolves, beats fixed-weight and fixed-routing baselines on both backbones, with the routing component carrying most of the gain. It is a sensible, well-probed contribution for joint audio-video generation, but the margin over the strongest prior method is narrow and the results are bounded by the reward models it optimizes.
