The paper is Recovering Reliable Supervision via Critique-Guided On-Policy Distillation, from Kaixiang Zhao, Tianrun Yu, and Shangzhe Li, from Brigham Young University and the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. It is a fix for a specific, named failure in on-policy distillation, and the method they call CriticOPD uses a teacher's error-recognition ability to restore the supervision signal exactly where standard on-policy distillation loses it.

The setup is on-policy distillation, or OPD. The student model generates its own rollouts, and the teacher provides token-level supervision at each prefix the student reaches. The appeal is that this removes the occupancy mismatch of offline distillation, where the student trains on teacher-written prefixes and then at inference has to continue from prefixes it wrote itself. But OPD has an implicit assumption that the paper names explicitly: the teacher must be recoverable, meaning the teacher can reach a correct answer from any prefix the student writes. The paper shows this assumption fails on incorrect rollouts. When the student has already made an error, the teacher, prompted from that same broken prefix, tends to follow the error rather than correct it. The key finding, and the one worth remembering, is that the teacher recognizes the error and then follows it anyway. It can see the mistake, but conditioning on the student's continuation pulls it along. So the teacher's next-token distribution, which OPD treats as ground truth, becomes an unreliable corrective target precisely at the states where correction is most needed.

Prior work responds to this in two ways, and the paper is fair about both. The first reduces supervision: it truncates the later part of long responses, or stops rollouts and supervision early, so the teacher is only asked to supervise prefixes it is confident about. The second replaces the sequence: it lets the teacher take over a span of the generation, or regenerates from an earlier point the teacher judges reliable. The shared problem with both is that they trade one failure for another. Reducing supervision leaves the later states, the ones the student actually reaches at inference, completely untrained. Replacing the sequence trains the student on sequences the teacher wrote and the student will rarely generate on its own, which reintroduces the occupancy mismatch that OPD was supposed to fix. So existing methods mostly avoid unreliable teacher supervision rather than improving its corrective value.

CriticOPD takes the third route: make the supervision reliable instead of removing it. The pipeline has four steps, and the first two are diagnostic. The teacher's critique prompt is a meticulous-math-teacher instruction: trace the student's work forward, number the steps, and find the steps that contain genuine errors, replying that no error was found if every step is correct. The critique is grounded by the reference answer, which tells the critic what the solution is supposed to reach, and each listed error quotes the offending step, cut to 200 characters, with a short note on the mistake. A separate judge then validates the critique, labeling each claimed error as a new error of its own, a propagated error from an earlier step, or not a real error at all, and 73 to 81 percent of the listed errors survive that check. The student then rewrites its solution, starting from the last error the critique identifies, keeping the earlier correct prefix and regenerating the rest. Finally, and this is the subtle part, the teacher scores the rewritten suffix while the critique is present in its context as privileged information, but the student is trained on that suffix without the critique. So the teacher evaluates the student's correction in a state where it can see what went wrong, and the student has to learn to produce the corrected continuation on its own, without the hint. The teacher's scoring gives a dense, token-level signal on a continuation the student itself generated, which keeps the occupancy mismatch small, while the critique restores the teacher's ability to give a meaningful target.

The results are on two Qwen3 students and six mathematical reasoning benchmarks, spanning competition problems to standard math suites. The benchmarks are AIME 2024, AIME 2025, the AMC, MATH500, an Olympiad-style set, and Minerva math, and the students are Qwen3 at two sizes in non-thinking mode, distilled from a single Qwen3 teacher, with an OLMo-3 student from an OLMo-3 teacher reported as an additional check in the appendix. On the 1.7B student, the per-benchmark average is 54.57 for CriticOPD against 50.18 for plain OPD, with the gap opening widest on the harder competition problems: AIME 2024 at 40.52 against 34.69, AIME 2025 at 29.06 against 22.71, and the AMC at 70.18 against 63.33, while on the easier MATH500 the difference is small, 89.40 to 86.70. That pattern, big gains where the problems are hard and small gains where they are easy, is exactly what you would expect if the method is really fixing error correction rather than inflating overall confidence, because easy problems need less correction. The ablations confirm the mechanism. Removing the critique and keeping only the reference answer drops the average to 48.67, below plain OPD. Replacing the specific critique with a generic your-solution-contains-errors message drops it to 53.11. Having the teacher write the suffix instead of the student drops it to 53.06. Each of those ablations isolates one piece, and each piece is load-bearing.

A cost note that goes the other way than you might expect. Each critiqued rollout costs one extra teacher generation and one extra student generation, so per-step it is more expensive than OPD. But CriticOPD reaches its selected training step earlier and uses 0.42 times the total training compute of OPD to hit its target, because the more reliable supervision converges faster. The teacher-as-critic is right on the order of 73 to 81 percent of the time under their judge, so the critique is a real but noisy signal.

Now the skeptical pass. The whole method leans on reference answers to decide which rollouts deserve a critique, which confines it to tasks with verifiable answers, and the authors say so plainly. For open-ended work you would need a reward model or a self-judging critic, which is left to future work, and that is a big qualifier for anyone thinking of applying this beyond math and code-with-tests. The judge used to validate the critique is a model judge, and model judges disagree with humans, so the 73 to 81 percent critic accuracy is itself an estimate with its own error. The student-rewrites-its-own-suffix design is the right call for occupancy matching, but it means the quality of the correction depends on the student's own ability to recover from an error given a hint it will not have at inference, which is a slightly circular dependency the ablations only partially resolve. I would re-run the AIME numbers across seeds, since competition math is a small-sample regime where a few problems move the score a lot, and I would check the compute claim of 0.42 times OPD end to end, because a total-compute comparison that depends on where you draw the stopping line is easy to present in the flattering direction.

Bottom line: CriticOPD diagnoses a real and specific failure, the teacher following student errors it can see, and fixes it with a critique-rewrite-score loop that keeps supervision on-policy and dense. The gains concentrate on hard problems where the failure matters most, and the ablations show every component is doing work. The dependence on verifiable answers is the boundary condition to remember, and the compute accounting is the number to re-check before you trust the efficiency story. For a lab running distillation on math or verifiable code, the rewrite loop is a drop-in improvement over plain on-policy distillation, and the 0.42 compute figure, if it holds, makes it the cheapest way to get a stronger student out of the same teacher.
