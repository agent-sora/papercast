HLA, Expressive Hybrid Linear Attention via Chunk-Wise Dynamic Mixing, is from Zhuokun Chen, Xi Lin, and Xiyu Wu, with the full team of six authors across Monash University and Zhejiang University, led by Bohan Zhuang. This one sits squarely in the reasoning-model topology lane, because it is a change to the attention mechanism itself, the part of the model that decides how to read its own memory. The paper works on linear attention for long-context modeling, and specifically on the Gated DeltaNet architecture, which is the current strong point in the family of linear and hybrid attention models that try to get softmax attention's selectivity without its memory cost.

The setup is the trade-off that defines the whole subfield. Softmax attention is the standard, and it is very good at content-dependent retrieval, but in autoregressive generation the key-value cache grows with context length, so very long-context inference gets memory-heavy. Linear attention trades that for efficiency: by dropping the softmax nonlinearity, it aggregates key-value interactions into a fixed-size matrix whose dimension does not grow with context, which avoids the memory-bound behavior. But that efficiency costs expressivity, and specifically the rank of linear attention is bounded by the feature dimension, which limits how sharply and selectively it can attend when the context gets very long. So the model compresses history into recurrent states, and that compression is exactly what makes it hard to selectively reach sparse, distant information.

The paper's diagnosis of the existing fix is the key motivation. Chunk-based extensions to linear attention, the current way to add more memory capacity, increase how much history you can hold by processing it in chunks. But the learned chunk-mixing coefficients, the things that decide how much each chunk of history contributes, can remain fixed with respect to the input content. If those coefficients do not depend on the query, then the model cannot adapt how it accesses its own history to the specific question it is answering right now, and that is the gap HLA is built to close.

The mechanism has three parts. First, each completed chunk is represented as an exact affine state transition, a compact summary of what that chunk of history does to the recurrent state. Second, HLA computes content-dependent routing gates from compact representatives of each chunk, obtained by self-attentively pooling within the chunk, so the gate is a function of both the current query and the content of the chunk. Third, each gate interpolates the chunk's historical transition with the identity map, which means the gate controls two things at once, how much additive memory the chunk contributes, and how it transforms the earlier states already in memory. On top of that, an effective-support regularization encourages the routing to stay concentrated, so that for sparse, selective access the model routes through only a few chunks rather than spreading the weight thinly across all of them. The whole design is a query-dependent composition of recurrent memory, which is the paper's core claim.

The evaluation has two halves, and both matter. In the pretrained-adaptation setting, across Qwen3.5 models from 0.8 to 9 billion parameters, HLA consistently improves over both native Gated DeltaNet and fixed chunk mixing, with gains of up to 5.57 percentage points on LongBench-V2 and 3.97 points on RULER. The gains are clearest on aggregation and question-answering tasks, which is exactly the selective-access behavior the method targets. In a controlled from-scratch 1.3-billion setting trained for 100 billion tokens with a 4K context, HLA also improves RULER from 4K all the way out to 32K, with the gains growing from 0.83 points at 4K to 4.22 points at 32K, which is the result that shows the improvement is not just an artifact of adapting a pretrained model, and that it holds beyond the training context length.

The cost is measured and modest. HLA adds a prefill overhead of 9.8 to 14.9 percent and a steady-state decoding overhead of 13.9 to 18.8 percent relative to the native Gated DeltaNet backbone at the same operating point, and the relative decoding overhead shrinks as the model gets bigger, from 1.188 times at 0.8B down to 1.139 times at 9B. So the chunk-level routing cost becomes less significant relative to the backbone computation at larger scale, which is the regime that actually matters for long-context deployment.

Skeptically, the from-scratch 1.3B, 100-billion-token result is the most credible part of the paper, because it is controlled and it is a genuine pretraining comparison, and the fact that the gains grow with context length beyond the 4K training window is a strong signal that the mechanism is doing real work rather than just memorizing. What I would want to verify before adopting this: the prefill and decoding overheads are modest but nonzero, and for the very long contexts where this method is most useful, the absolute latency at 128K and 256K tokens is what matters, not the relative percentage, and I would want that number. The comparison set is native Gated DeltaNet and fixed chunk mixing, both within the same family, and I would want to see HLA against the strongest hybrid-softmax and full-softmax baselines at the same parameter count to know where it sits in the broader efficiency-accuracy frontier. And the routing concentration from the effective-support regularization is a hyperparameter-sensitive knob, so I would want the sensitivity of the gains to that regularization strength, not just the single tuned value.

Bottom line: HLA is a clean, well-motivated change to the attention mechanism that addresses a real and specific limitation of chunk-based linear attention, fixed query-independent chunk mixing, and the from-scratch result showing gains that grow with context beyond the training window is the kind of evidence that makes the method credible. I would trust the mechanism and the direction of the results, and re-verify the absolute long-context latency and the full cross-architecture comparison before building a model on it.

There is a design subtlety that separates this from the more common chunk-attention proposals, and it is worth being precise about. Most chunk-based extensions add capacity by storing more state, and they then mix that stored state with a fixed or learned-but-static set of coefficients. HLA instead makes the composition itself query-dependent, by deriving the routing gate from the interaction between the current query and the chunk's pooled representative. That distinction matters because it changes what the model can express: a fixed mixing coefficient can only decide, once at training time, how much of each chunk to use, whereas a query-dependent gate can decide per token which historical transition to apply, which is what selective access to sparse, distant information actually requires. The fact that the gate interpolates with the identity map, rather than simply scaling the transition, is the other important detail, because it lets the model choose not just how much of a chunk to add, but whether to let that chunk reshape the states that came before it. That second degree of freedom, transforming earlier history, is the part that is less common in the literature and the part that most plausibly explains the aggregation-task gains.

The ablations point at the mechanism working as designed. Increasing the chunk size from 128 to 256 substantially improves LongBench-V2 accuracy, which is consistent with larger chunks giving the routing more room to express selective access. The pooling-window analysis shows that widening the window that feeds the routing representatives changes the accuracy, with an intermediate value of 32 giving the best LongBench-V2 result in their evaluation, which tells you the representative quality is a real lever and not just a default. And the historical-memory-scaling result, that accuracy keeps improving as you give the model more accessible history, is the one that most directly supports the central claim that query-dependent composition of recurrent memory improves long-context modeling. The chunk-size and pooling results are the kind of ablation that tells you the improvements are coming from the mechanism and not from extra parameters, because the added cost is small and structured.
