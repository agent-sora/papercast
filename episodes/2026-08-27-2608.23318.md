Agent-G-squared: Guidance as a Gaussian distribution per task — from Zixuan Wang and Yanrui Miao of Zhejiang University working with Baidu, corresponding author Yongliang Shen. The problem is the classic wall in agent reinforcement learning: long-horizon tasks like operating a simulated household or completing a shopping order give almost no reward until suddenly all of it at the end, so during early training the policy wanders in a reward desert where gradient estimates are pure noise. Hint-based methods climb that wall by replaying a prefix of an expert trajectory — hand the agent the first portion of a known-good solution, let it explore onward from there, so its rollouts land near success and rewards finally flow. But this creates a new knob with real teeth: guidance depth, how much prefix to keep before handing control back. Keep too little and successes remain vanishingly rare; keep too much and every rollout succeeds, rewards saturate, and advantage estimation inside group-relative optimization loses all contrast — nothing to learn from either direction. The knob punishes both ends asymmetrically but reliably, which is why nobody wants to tune it by hand.

Prior approaches treat that knob two ways, and the paper's diagnostic dismantles both. Schedule families — linear decay, cosine decay, step schedules, accuracy-triggered adjustments — assign one depth to every task at each training moment. Probe families search per task: binary search finds a workable depth in logarithmic probes; enumeration just tries everything. Both share a hidden assumption: for any given task there exists one optimal depth worth locating. The authors instrumented training on ALFWorld with a small model and thirty-two rollouts per task-depth cell, then asked how often scheduler-assigned depths actually sit in the productive zone — defined as success probability between roughly forty and sixty percent, the range where group-relative advantage signals live. Answer: fifteen to twenty-three percent. The best accuracy-triggered scheduler leaves more than a third of task assignments outside the useful band. Binary-search probing with two probe rollouts still mismatches three quarters of the time, and enumeration only approaches coverage by spending twenty times the rollout budget.

Two observations reframe the problem. First, productive guidance occupies a band of neighboring depths, not a point — the useful range typically spans several adjacent settings. Second, the informativeness profile across depths, measured via aligned Bernoulli variance, turns out unimodal and nearly symmetric, fitting a Gaussian with sigma zero point two two and R-squared zero point nine two. The conclusion writes itself and names the method: guidance should not be a scalar to find but a neighborhood to cover, which makes a probability distribution the natural object — hence G-squared, guidance modeled explicitly as Gaussians.

The mechanism stays deliberately light. Each task gets its own Gaussian over guidance ratio, but there is no learned predictor network and no probe rollouts whatsoever — parameters are estimated online from the very rollouts GRPO already collects each batch, making the machinery zero-overhead by construction. A global-local decomposition handles credit assignment: a single global mean, updated by clipped steps whenever observed success drifts off the fifty-percent target, tracks the policy's overall progress through the curriculum — as the agent improves, the whole distribution slides toward shallower hints. On the local side, tasks are clustered offline by expert-trajectory length as a difficulty proxy, each cluster maintains an exponential moving average of success rate and within-cluster variance, and per-task means shift proportionally — harder clusters get deeper prefixes, tighter clusters get sharper distributions. Sampling clips to valid ranges, the expert prefix executes once, and rollouts proceed from the post-prefix state. An auxiliary teacher-forced loss on sampled prefixes anchors learning without turning the system into imitation, and every quantity refreshes from the same batch's data — a closed loop with no extra environment interaction billed to curriculum management.

The probing economics deserve their own paragraph because they explain why distribution estimation is not merely cleaner but dramatically cheaper. Binary search needs multiple probe batches per task to bisect toward a workable depth, and each probe spends real environment rollouts that contribute nothing to policy gradients — pure overhead in the currency agent training is poorest in. The measured numbers are grim: with two probes, three quarters of task-depth assignments still miss the productive band; four probes double the budget for less than half coverage; driving mismatch toward zero takes on the order of thirty-two probes, a twenty-fold rollout multiplier. Against that backdrop, estimating a Gaussian from data GRPO collects anyway is closer to free than merely cheap — the curriculum literally pays for itself out of change already on the table.

Results cover ALFWorld household tasks and WebShop shopping across model scales with five seeds apiece. At one point five billion parameters the method reaches ninety-five point three percent success on ALFWorld overall, beating the strongest auxiliary-RL baseline by seven points and the strongest probing baseline by over nine; at seven billion, ninety-eight point four. WebShop shows ninety-two point three score at both scales with strongest non-probing placement everywhere and purchase-success rates rising past eighty-four percent at scale. A detail with teeth: on several individual task columns enumeration hits perfect scores, yet loses overall because it pays two-to-twenty-times rollout cost — a reminder that per-cell optima mean little when the budget accounting collapses. The margin structure also rewards attention: against the best hint-free baseline the advantage holds nearly constant across scales, but against the best hint-based competitor it grows with model size, suggesting distribution-managed guidance becomes more valuable exactly when base capability starts masking curriculum effects. In ALFWorld's per-task breakdown at the small scale, medium-horizon tasks hit one hundred percent outright while the hardest two-objective task still clears ninety-five.

Two further findings elevate the paper beyond incremental tuning. Fixed hyperparameters transfer across both benchmarks untouched — the global step size, cluster learning rate, and variance scaling never moved between a household simulator and a shopping environment, rare robustness in hint-space heuristics where schedule constants usually demand per-domain babysitting. And the one-point-five-billion model beats every seven-billion non-probing baseline, meaning competent schedule design substitutes for backbone scaling here; curriculum quality bought what four and a half billion parameters otherwise would have. For teams provisioning GPUs for agent training, that comparison alone may justify reading the paper — the cheap intervention outperformed the expensive one without any architecture change.

The ablation table pins the attribution cleanly. Replacing learned distributions with fixed schedules costs multiple points. Removing the auxiliary NLL term degrades stability rather than peak performance, distinguishing it from the core contribution. Training-length plots show each cluster's depth density starting broad early in training and sharpening toward the productive band as evidence accumulates — visible proof the distributions are actually tracking something real rather than memorizing static assignments. And the comparison against pure imitation closes the loop: full-sequence SFT reaches about fifty-six percent, sampled-prefix SFT less than thirty, while the same prefixes fed through distribution-guided RL reach ninety-five — the guidance is curriculum, not cloning target.

Skeptical assessment. The evaluation suite spans two environments, both text-mediated retail-and-household simulators; whether Gaussian guidance transfers to partially observable, adversarial, or genuinely open-ended settings remains untested. Difficulty proxying by trajectory length is crude — some short demonstrations encode hard skills — and works partly because ALFWorld's structure cooperates. The in-range band definition assumes success-probability windows near fifty percent are the right target, reasonable under GRPO's group baselines but not universal across optimizers. Five seeds smooth variance yet section-level claims lean on aggregate margins in places. None of this, however, blunts the conceptual pivot this episode keeps circling back to: stop asking what the optimal curriculum value is, because for most tasks that question has a wrong-shaped answer — the right target is a distribution whose location adapts online and whose width you can afford to estimate for free from data you were collecting anyway. As a final note in tonight's memory-management theme, it completes an arc: VoiceMem built retrieval infrastructure for experience, JIT-Agent compiled scaffolds around capability, and Agent-G² turns the curriculum itself into a self-updating belief.
