Behind Video Generation: Observations and Insights on Multimodal Understanding from Kling, from the Kling Team with Dong Chen, Jialu Chen, and Xinlong Chen on the author line, is a systems report out of the Kuaishou video generation group, the team behind the Kling video models. This is not a paper about a single new architecture. It is the group's attempt to lay out the full understanding stack that sits behind a controllable video generator, and to argue that the quality of the generated video is mostly a projection of how well the rest of the pipeline understands the world. The central claim is that generation quality is the projection of multimodal understanding through the entire data, conditioning, alignment, and evaluation chain, and the paper walks that chain module by module, from captioning to reward modeling to automatic evaluation.

The load-bearing component is something they call Kling-MLLM, a shared multimodal foundation model that unifies image, video, audio, synchronized audio-video, and interleaved inputs into one model, which is then specialized for each downstream module in the chain. They release it in three sizes: a 27-billion dense model, a 35-billion-parameter model with 3-billion active parameters, and a 397-billion-parameter model with 17 billion active, all with backbones initialized from checkpoints in the Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.8 model families, and the report describes a four-stage training curriculum that moves from low-level perception to full interleaved multimodal understanding. One practical detail they flag: they retain a 20 percent replay of the first stage in later stages, because the low-level perception acquired there tends to degrade otherwise, and they train the vision tower at a 1 to 10 learning rate ratio relative to the language side. The framing of the whole thing is that a diffusion transformer is trained on far fewer samples than a language model, so every bit of semantic understanding has to be squeezed out of the conditioning signal. That is why they treat captioning as a semantic contract rather than a nicety. The caption is the contract between what the user asked for and what the generator can actually render, and the goal is to maximize description density in it.

The data construction side is where the engineering gets concrete. They organize the training corpus along difficulty slices: duration, shot count, entity count, event count, motion speed, optical character recognition density, and audio presence. They describe a principle they call perceptual evidence preservation, meaning preprocessing steps like downsampling, black frame removal, and audio gating must not delete the evidence that the captions later have to describe. Their captions are structured to preserve shot timing, audiovisual alignment, and visible text, including mirror-reversed lettering in scenes, and they explicitly require that audio-related caption fields only activate once an audio track has actually been inspected, so the model learns not to hallucinate sound that is not there. That is a small-sounding detail that goes a long way toward making the caption distribution honest.

The first module is captioning, extended into what they call reference-grounded captioning, which makes the binding between a named reference object and its visual appearance explicit in the text. The point of the reference grounding is that a user can ask to change the hair color of the person on the left while keeping the background geometry locked, and the caption has to carry that binding explicitly so the generator does not swap the two people. The second is prompt enhancement, where a basic prompt enhancer is derived from captions of compelling videos and then refined with text-level and generator-in-the-loop reinforcement learning, using GRPO to optimize a reward that combines three scores. They are explicit that a single prompt enhancement model has limited capacity and can miss the vividness of the compelling videos it was derived from, which is why the generator-in-the-loop step matters: the reward is measured by what the generator actually produces, not by how well the text reads. They add an agentic variant that refines the story and retrieves compelling examples before generation. The third module is the text encoder, and their finding here is the least obvious in the paper: a reliable text encoder comes from perceptual training, not from stronger language ability. The intuition is that the encoder does not need to be a good general-purpose language model, it needs to produce representations that the generator's conditioning layer can actually use, and they show that encoder candidates can be screened from text alone, before any end-to-end training, which is a real cost saver if you are trying to iterate on encoders.

Reward modeling gets its own module. They pair a general reward model, which is meant to give explainable, localized critiques of a candidate video, with a decision reward model, which is a faster multidimensional scorer for ranking candidates. They treat perceptual bandwidth as a first-class design parameter, meaning they explicitly budget how much visual detail the reward model is allowed to look at, on the reasoning that an overly fine-grained critic either overfits to artifacts or becomes too slow to be useful in a loop. The decision model scores each of several dimensions separately rather than through a single overall preference, and the scalar score is a probability-weighted expectation of the rating, which keeps the reward smooth enough to train against.

The final module is task-specific automatic evaluation, and they spend real effort on protocols that expose scoring shortcuts, because their argument throughout is that a benchmark that rewards the model for gaming a metric is worse than no benchmark. They cite the CHAIR result from captioning as the canonical example, where higher sentence-level scores do not imply fewer hallucinations, and they build their evaluation protocols to trace a failure back to its upstream source in the chain, so that a bad score tells you which module to fix rather than just that the output is bad.

On numbers, the report is a mixed bag, and that is worth being honest about. The 397-billion-parameter, 17-billion-active Kling-MLLM-397B-A17B is the strongest of their family on most of the video understanding benchmarks they report, leading five columns on a set of benchmarks including DSI-Bench, Event-Bench, MotionBench, Camera Motion VQA, and the coverage side of VidCapBench, while a smaller 35-billion active model wins VSI-Bench. On audio and speech benchmarks, they report that their models land within a fraction of a point of the best external results, and on the LibriSpeech-based audio understanding scores the external Doubao model actually scores better. For caption quality they report average scores on a 1 to 5 scale for faithfulness, reasonableness, and vividness, and they emphasize conjunctive quality gating, where a candidate caption has to clear thresholds on accuracy, coverage, and a third dimension simultaneously rather than on average.

The skeptical read is that this is a report about a product, and the evidence is mostly internal. The benchmarks are their own, the baselines are other vendors' released models rather than a controlled comparison, and the claim that generation quality is the projection of understanding is a framing device that is hard to falsify from the numbers presented. What is genuinely useful is the decomposition itself. If you are building a controllable video pipeline, the paper tells you exactly which levers exist: caption density, reference grounding, prompt enhancement with RL, text encoder selection, and reward model design, and it gives you an opinion on which of those levers matter most. The text encoder finding, that perceptual training beats raw language ability for this use, is the kind of result that is not obvious and that you could test yourself.

Bottom line: this is a competent and unusually transparent systems report from a team with real product stakes, and the module-by-module decomposition of the understanding chain is the part worth keeping. But the headline claim is a narrative, not a theorem, and the internal benchmarks mean you should treat the specific numbers as directional rather than as ground truth.
