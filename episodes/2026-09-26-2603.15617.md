HorizonMath, by Erik Wang, Sumeet Motwani, James Roggeveen, and a team
that includes Eliot Hodges, Dulhan Jayalath, Charles London, Kalyan
Ramakrishnan, Flaviu Cipcigan, Philip Torr, and Alessandro Abate, from a
set of institutions that spans Oxford, Harvard, Princeton, and the
Ellison Institute of Technology, which is a detail worth holding onto,
because a benchmark for mathematical discovery is exactly the kind of
object that benefits from being built by people who work in the area
rather than by a single lab with a product to ship.

The question the paper is trying to answer is the one the field has been
arguing about, whether large language models can do novel mathematical
research, not just solve known problems. The paper's framing is that the
models are clearly capable of sophisticated mathematical and scientific
reasoning, that part is not in dispute, but whether they can perform novel
research is still widely debated and underexplored, and the reason it is
underexplored is a measurement problem, and the paper identifies the
measurement problem precisely. The existing benchmarks fall into two
camps, and neither of them measures discovery. One camp is the
competition mathematics, the problems that have a known answer and a known
difficulty, and doing well there shows that the model can solve problems,
it does not show that the model can find new ones. The other camp is the
research-level benchmarks, and those have the discovery property, but they
rely on formal proof verification or on manual review to check the
answer, and the paper's point is that both of those are expensive to
scale, a formal proof requires the result to be in a form a theorem
prover can check, and manual review requires a human expert for every
candidate answer, and neither scales to a benchmark that is meant to be
run on every new model release.

The paper's contribution is a benchmark that sits in the gap between those
two camps, and the design property that makes it work is a specific
constraint on the class of problems, and it is worth stating it exactly.
The benchmark targets problems where discovery is hard, requiring
meaningful mathematical insight, but verification is computationally
efficient and simple. That is the whole design, and it is the difference
between this benchmark and the formal-proof benchmarks, because the
problems are not required to be formalizable, they are required to be
checkable, and checkable in a way that a program can do without a human
expert. The benchmark is built from more than one hundred predominantly unsolved
problems spanning eight domains in computational and applied mathematics, and the
eight domains are the part that gives it breadth, the problems are not all
from one area, they are spread across the applied and computational
mathematics that is the kind of mathematics where a computational
approach is natural, and the predominantly unsolved property is the part
that gives it the discovery character, because the model is being asked
about problems that do not have a published answer, so a correct answer is
a contribution, not a recall. The open-source evaluation framework is the
other half of the contribution, because the verification is automated, and
the framework is released, so a reader can run the benchmark on a new
model without waiting for the authors to do it, and that is what makes it
a progress-measuring tool rather than a one-off evaluation.

The result the paper reports is specific, and it is the part that makes
the benchmark credible, because it is an instance of the thing the
benchmark is for. Using the platform, the authors found two problems for
which GPT five point four Pro proposes solutions that improve on the best-
known published results, and the paper is careful about the status of
those, it describes them as potential novel contributions pending expert
review, which is the honest framing, the automated verification says the
proposed solution is better than the published one, but the claim that it
is a correct and genuine improvement is a mathematical claim that still
needs a human expert to confirm, and the paper does not overstate it, it
reports the two as candidates for novelty rather than as confirmed new
results. That distinction is the right one, and it is the part a reader
should hold onto, because the benchmark's automatic verification handles
the check, is the proposed solution better than the baseline, and it does
not handle the proof, is the proposed solution actually correct and
actually new, and those are two different claims, and the paper keeps them
separate.

Now the skeptical read. The first question is the predominantly unsolved
property, because predominantly unsolved is not all unsolved, and the
honest version of the question is how many of the over one hundred
problems are genuinely open, in the sense that no one has published a
solution, versus how many are solved in the literature but not in the
standard references the model would have been trained on, because the
latter are a recall problem with a research veneer, and a reader should
check the problem set, because the discovery character of the benchmark
depends on the problems being genuinely open, and if a significant fraction
are known results that the model is just retrieving, the benchmark is
measuring recall, not discovery. The second question is the verification
scope, because the automated verification checks that a proposed solution
is better than the published baseline, and the question is what that
check actually computes, because better can mean a tighter bound, a
faster algorithm, a cleaner construction, and the automated check has to
be able to distinguish those, and a reader should read the evaluation
framework to understand what class of improvement it can detect, because a
check that only detects a numeric improvement on a specific metric is
measuring a narrower thing than the paper's framing of discovery
suggests. The third question is the two-problem result, which is the
benchmark's own proof of concept, and the honest read is that two problems
is a small number, and it is enough to show that the platform can surface
candidates for novelty, which is the point, but it is not enough to
establish a rate, a reader cannot tell from two problems how often the
models produce genuine novelties, and the value of the benchmark is in the
trend over many model releases, and the paper should be read as the first
data point of that trend, not as the measurement. And the practical
question is the open challenge, the paper releases the benchmark as a
growing community resource, and the question for a reader who wants to use
it is whether the problem set and the verification framework are
maintained, because a benchmark that is a snapshot is less useful than one
that is a living resource, and the community-resource framing is the
stronger claim, and a reader should check that the repository is actively
maintained, that new problems are being added, and that the verification
is stable across versions, because the progress-measuring property of the
benchmark only holds if the yardstick does not move between measurements.

Bottom line: this is a well-motivated benchmark that targets a real gap in
how the field measures AI mathematical ability, the gap between solving
known problems and making novel contributions, with a specific design
constraint, problems that are hard to discover but cheap to verify, over
one hundred predominantly unsolved problems across eight domains, an open-
source automated verification framework, and a proof-of-concept result
where a frontier model proposed improvements on two problems pending expert
review. The open question is how many of the problems are genuinely open
versus known-but-unretrieved, what class of improvement the automated
check can actually detect, and whether the benchmark is maintained as a
living resource rather than a snapshot.
