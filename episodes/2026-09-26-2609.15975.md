Disentangling Representation Evolution in Transformers through Directional
Decomposition, by Shwai He, Haichao Zhang, and Shen Yan, from the University
of Maryland College Park, Northeastern University, and ByteDance.

This is a paper about the geometry of what a transformer layer actually does
to a token, and it is one of those interpretability pieces that tries to turn
a hand-wavy intuition into a quantity you can measure, perturb, and use. The
starting point is the residual stream: every layer takes a hidden state and
adds a learned update to it, so the representation only ever changes by an
additive step on top of what it already was. The authors ask what direction
that step points in, relative to the representation it is being added to. Any
vector can be split into a component parallel to the current state, which just
rescales it, and a component perpendicular to it, which rotates or redirects
it. That two-way split, the parallel part and the perpendicular part, is the
entire instrument the rest of the paper hangs on.

The first finding is descriptive and it is the one I would weight most. Across
a set of pretrained models from the Qwen3, Llama-3, and Gemma-3 families, the
learned updates are not mostly perpendicular. They contain a substantial
parallel component, meaning a large share of each layer's contribution is
essentially a rescaling of what the token already was, rather than a new
direction. That matters because the residual connection already gives you a
free, zero-parameter identity path, so a purely parallel update looks, at
first glance, like the model is spending capacity to do something the residual
stream could do for free. The question the paper pushes into is whether that
parallel mass is harmless, useful, or something in between, and the answer turns
out to depend strongly on which space you are looking in.

That is the second contribution, and it is where the paper gets specific. They
apply the same decomposition in two different places. One is the residual
level, where you decompose the attention or MLP update relative to the hidden
state going in. The other is inside attention itself, where you decompose the
value aggregation relative to the current token's own value. The reason the
distinction matters is a subtlety they call out clearly: inside attention, the
token's message to itself, the diagonal entry, is intrinsically parallel to its
own value. So if you naively strip out the parallel component of the full
aggregate, you do not just remove the cross-token signal you were aiming at,
you also extinguish the token's own identity carrier, the direct self message.
That naive parallel removal causes severe degradation. The fix is an exclude-
self version, where you decompose only the non-self aggregate and leave the
diagonal self message untouched. That one design choice is what makes the
value-space intervention clean, and it is the thing I would want to see in the
code first. Concretely, the intervention is a two-knob component scaling: you
pick a scalar for the parallel component and a separate scalar for the
perpendicular component, with the no-operation being both set to one, and the
pure parallel removal being parallel set to zero while perpendicular stays at
one. That parameterization is what lets them sweep a whole family of edits from
mild rescaling all the way to full suppression, which is how the robustness
claim in the next paragraph is actually measured rather than asserted.

The intervention results are the payoff of that design. When you scale just the
parallel component of the exclude-self value-space aggregate, model behavior
stays remarkably close to baseline over a wide scaling range, whereas the
residual-space and perpendicular versions are noticeably less stable. In other
words, the model tolerates being rescaled along its own direction in value space
far better than it tolerates being redirected, and it tolerates it in value space
better than in the residual stream. The asymmetry is strong and it is space-
dependent, which is the cleanest claim in the paper. Parallel updates, at least
the ones inside the cross-token value aggregation, are broadly robust, and
perpendicular steering is the fragile part.

They then reuse the same decomposition for two downstream diagnostics, which is
what gives the paper its breadth. The first is compression. When you quantize or
prune a model, the update each layer applies gets distorted, and you can split
that distortion into a parallel error and a perpendicular error. Across 4-bit
AWQ quantization and Wanda pruning at the unstructured, 4:8, and 2:4 sparsity
patterns, the better-performing compressed models consistently show lower
perpendicular error, while the parallel error is far less discriminative. So the
geometry tells you something a plain accuracy number does not: the direction-
changing part of the update is what compression most damages, and that is the
part to protect.

The last result is the most ambitious and the one I would treat as the most
provisional. They run from-scratch pretraining on OpenWebText, at scales from
296M up to 2.7B parameters, and during training they suppress the parallel
component of the attention aggregate, comparing the residual-space and value-
space variants against an unmodified baseline. The value-space variant
consistently gives lower validation-loss trajectories, and both variants improve
downstream performance averages, with the value-space version producing the
largest gains. The logic is that if the parallel component is mostly redundant
with the residual identity path, then actively suppressing it during training
frees the layer to put its capacity into the perpendicular, direction-changing
work that actually changes the representation. That is a plausible mechanism,
but it is also a from-scratch training claim, so it is the piece most worth
reproducing before you would build anything on it.

Now the skeptical read. The descriptive finding, that updates carry a large
parallel component, is the safe and valuable part, and it is well supported
across three model families. The intervention robustness claim is also strong,
but it is a stability claim under a particular, well-motivated perturbation,
not a statement that the parallel component is useless. In fact the compression
result cuts the other way in an interesting way: the model does not need the
parallel direction to be accurate, but it does need the perpendicular direction
to survive compression, which suggests the two components play different roles
and that collapsing the parallel part during inference is cheap while collapsing
the perpendicular part is expensive. The from-scratch suppression result is the
one I would interrogate hardest. The gains are measured on validation loss and a
downstream average at specific scales, and the whole premise is that the
redundancy with the residual path is real and large enough to suppress, which is
exactly the assumption the descriptive finding motivates but does not by itself
prove at training time. There is also the exclude-self subtlety doing a lot of
load-bearing work: if your parallel removal accidentally touches the diagonal
self message, the result flips from robust to degraded, so the method is only as
good as that one bookkeeping detail, and a naive reimplementation is likely to
get it wrong.

Bottom line: this is a clean geometric contribution with a real, measurable
quantity, a space-dependent robustness result that survives three model
families, a compression diagnosis that separates methods by perpendicular
error, and a from-scratch training intervention whose gains I would want to see
reproduced before trusting. The open question is whether the value-space
parallel suppression keeps its edge at scale, and whether the exclude-self
bookkeeping stays clean enough that the robustness the paper reports is what you
actually get in a real deployment. And the deeper takeaway, the one that would
generalize beyond transformers, is that the direction a layer points, not just
how much it changes the magnitude, is a first-class object you can probe, and
that the answer changes depending on which internal space you probe it in.
