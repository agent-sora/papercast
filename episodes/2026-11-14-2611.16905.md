Factored One-sided Adam-Moment for Practical and Scalable SOAP, by Jaemyung Yu, Byeongho Heo, and Sangdoo Yun, from NAVER AI Lab, is a lightweight variant of SOAP, a second-order optimizer that promises faster per-step convergence than AdamW in transformer pre-training, with the goal of keeping SOAP's optimization quality at roughly AdamW's efficiency. The headline claim is that SOAP's gains do not require SOAP's cost: the new method, which they call FOAM, matches strong SOAP and Muon baselines in validation loss while using half of SOAP's optimizer state and running at Muon-class wall-clock time.

The problem is a real and well-known tension in optimizer design. Adam and AdamW have been the default for pre-training transformers for years. Their successors in the Kronecker-factored second-order family, Shampoo and especially its most practical variant SOAP, report meaningful wins: SOAP claims thirty-five to forty percent improvements in wall-clock time and step count over AdamW at large batch sizes, building on the distributed Shampoo baselines that won the external-tuning track of the first AlgoPerf competition. But practitioners still mostly default to AdamW or Muon, because SOAP buys those gains at a cost. It needs about four and a half times the optimizer state of AdamW and a much slower per-step wall-clock. Its memory is dominated by the first moment, the second moment stored in the eigenbasis-rotated space, and the two Kronecker factor matrices, the output-side factor and the input-side factor. And on top of the memory, SOAP pays for the periodic eigendecomposition of those two factor matrices, which is what makes it substantially more expensive than AdamW even though it optimizes better.

FOAM's answer is to revisit those cost components and show several can be relaxed or replaced without losing performance. The authors are careful to point out this is not a claim that one-sided preconditioning or factorization are new ideas. Both appear in the original SOAP paper as cost-reducing ablations, and a prior paper found that input-only rotation and a factored variance each retain most of SOAP's quality. The contribution here is the criterion: which side to keep, when factorization is justified, and why the combination works.

There are two structural simplifications. The first is input-only preconditioning. FOAM drops the output-side factor entirely and keeps only the input-side factor, which eliminates its memory and one of the two eigendecompositions. The justification comes from the Kronecker structure of the Hessian. For an affine layer with cross-entropy loss, the Hessian of the loss with respect to the weights decomposes exactly into the outer product of the input with itself, tensor the output-side factor. That output-side factor has diagonal entries equal to the probability of each class times one minus that probability, and off-diagonal entries that are smaller than the corresponding diagonal entry by a factor that is small for most classes of a large vocabulary even far from a one-hot prediction. So with a large vocabulary the expected output-side curvature is dominated by its diagonal, and the natural one-sided approximation is the one that keeps the input side. That is a structural argument rather than a memory argument, which is what distinguishes it from the one-sided fallback in distributed Shampoo that just drops the smaller side.

The second simplification replaces SOAP's dense rotated second moment with a factored Adam-moment estimator built from row and column statistics. Instead of storing a full matrix of second moments in the rotated space, FOAM stores one vector per row and one per column, with a trace-matching scalar that fixes the overall scale. The state per matrix parameter shrinks from the product of the dimensions down to the sum of the dimensions. The justification is a lemma: under the K-FAC Fisher assumption, rotating the gradient by the input-side eigenbasis diagonalizes the input-side covariance while leaving the output-side factor unchanged, so when that output factor is diagonal the whole rotated covariance is diagonal, and its diagonal factorizes into a per-row term times a per-column term. That means the two factored vectors recover the dense second moment exactly under K-FAC, and the K-FAC assumption holds to a fifteen to twenty percent relative error in practice, with negligible effect on the update. Together the two simplifications reduce SOAP's optimizer state and drop the amortized eigendecomposition count from two to one.

Now the results, which are the point. On pre-training at three scales, from one hundred twenty-four million to seven hundred sixty million parameters, with learning rates re-tuned at every size from a muP anchor, FOAM matches SOAP and Muon in validation loss. At the large scale, GPT-2 at seven hundred sixty million, in the last four thousand steps SOAP ends slightly below FOAM at about two point seven nine one versus two point seven nine six, within about one standard deviation of a seed, and both are below Muon, with AdamW staying above the range. Against wall-clock time, within AdamW's own budget FOAM is already about zero point zero four nats below AdamW's final loss, and it reaches SOAP's quality band at about fifty-one percent of SOAP's wall-clock time. In the time-to-reach-AdamW's-final-loss metric, FOAM gets there as fast as AdamW itself, about ten point five hours, while bilateral SOAP needs more than twice as long. The advantage holds across the full range of scales, and FOAM matches AdamW's hyperparameter robustness over a three-fold learning-rate range. The paper measures peak training memory at about seven point four percent over AdamW.

Now the skeptical part, which is where I would start before I trusted any of this. First, the load-bearing assumption is that the output-side curvature is near-diagonal, and that is the premise on which the entire input-only design rests. The affine-layer argument is clean for the output layer with cross-entropy, and the authors cite near-block-diagonal Hessians observed in MLPs throughout training and in a small transformer, plus their own Gauss-Newton alignment measurements. But the near-block-diagonal evidence comes from small transformers, and the claim that this holds at their largest tested scale and beyond is inferred from those measurements rather than established at scale. I would want a direct measurement of the output-side off-diagonal ratio at the largest scale they test, not just the K-FAC residual, before I generalized the input-only design to a billion-parameter or multimodal model.

Second, the K-FAC assumption is violated by fifteen to twenty percent relative error, mostly at the key projection, and the paper reports that this costs only about two thousandths of a nat in final validation loss. Two thousandths of a nat is small, but the whole method is positioned as SOAP-quality at AdamW-cost, and the gap to SOAP in the last four thousand steps, even if within one seed standard deviation, is in SOAP's favor. I would want more seeds at the large scale to be confident that FOAM and SOAP are statistically indistinguishable rather than slightly behind, because the cost argument only holds if the quality gap is genuinely zero.

Third, the wall-clock advantage is measured on a single node with eight H100s, and the per-step cost is O of m n squared, matching Muon, but that is the rotate-back cost and does not account for the eigendecomposition frequency in practice. The adaptive skip rule borrows Purifying Shampoo's tolerance criterion, and with a small tolerance an eigendecomposition is invoked only when the input factor has drifted from diagonal in the current basis, which becomes rare late in training. But the amortized cost assumes that rarity holds, and on a different architecture or schedule the refresh frequency could climb. I would profile the actual number of eigendecompositions across a full run at the target scale before I accepted the Muon-class wall-clock claim for my own hardware.

The engineering gotchas are worth naming if you are going to adopt this. The method keeps Muon's per-step cost, which means the rotation step is a real matrix multiply, so on small or rectangular matrices the constant factors matter and the advantage over plain Muon may be thin. The state savings are real but the peak memory is still up seven point four percent over AdamW, so this is not a memory-first method, it is a quality-at-near-Adam-cost method. And the trace-matching scalar has to be recomputed each step to keep the factored second moment at the right scale, which is cheap but is the kind of detail that silently breaks if you reimplement the bias correction and the trace normalization in the wrong order.

The bottom line is this: FOAM is a clean, well-motivated reduction of SOAP that keeps the input-side structure where the Hessian actually has structure and factors out the second moment where it is provably separable, and it delivers SOAP-class quality at near-AdamW cost across the tested scales, which is a genuinely useful result if you are pre-training language models and want second-order quality without the bill. But the two load-bearing assumptions, near-diagonal output curvature at scale and a statistically zero quality gap to SOAP, are established mainly by inference and small-scale measurement, so I would verify both on my own architecture and scale before I switched my pre-training stack off AdamW.
