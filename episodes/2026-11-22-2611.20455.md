Embeddings versus Grep, by Siqiao Xue, Ziyin Zhang, and Zihan Liao, with the work done at Ant Group, is a measurement paper that asks a question a lot of teams have quietly assumed the answer to: what is the dense advantage in code search actually worth? Code-search benchmarks consistently show dense embedding encoders well ahead of BM25, the classic lexical search, by wide margins in the standard nDCG at 10 metric. Yet in practice, coding agents do quite well with plain grep and find. The paper's position is that existing benchmarks cannot tell you what the dense advantage measures, because they use curated queries, old code, and a handicapped BM25 baseline. To find out, the authors build Repo-Premium, a realistic repository-level code-search benchmark, and trace the dense advantage back to two concrete sources.

The setup is precise, and the precision is the point. The task is file ranking within a real repository: given a query, rank the files so that the file a change actually edited is near the top. The labels come straight from the merged change, so there is no curation of what is relevant. The core benchmark has 1,265 queries over ten repositories from SWE-bench Verified, and it varies two axes deliberately: where the query comes from, either a curated problem statement or raw GitHub pull-request text, and when the change was made, with windows in 2022, 2023, and on or after January 2026. The authors skip 2024 and 2025 on purpose, so that every window lies clearly before or after the encoders' training cutoffs, which lets them probe whether the dense advantage is partly data contamination. An extension set adds five more languages, nine held-out repositories, and a preregistered test pool of 91 repositories, with 539 lockbox queries that are scored as preregistered tests. The queries span Python, JavaScript, TypeScript, Go, Rust, and Java.

The paper measures the gap between dense retrieval and BM25 with a specific term it calls the dense premium, which is the nDCG at 10 lead of the dense retriever over BM25 under one consistent protocol. They evaluate thirteen dense encoders, plus SPLADE and ColBERTv2, and they run three versions of the lexical baseline: an untuned BM25 that reuses the dense retriever's chunking, a tuned BM25 that indexes whole files, and a tuned BM25 with LLM-written identifiers added to the query, which the paper calls generate-then-grep.

The three findings, which the paper labels F1 through F3, are clean and I can verify each in the text I have. F1: evaluation choices inflate the dense advantage. Indexing whole files for BM25 instead of the dense retriever's chunks cuts the curated-pool premium from a positive 0.121 to a positive 0.034, and counting edited test files as relevant, which the authors' first version did, doubles the premium on the post-cutoff pool. Against a fair whole-file BM25, the encoder still leads on the raw query pools, mostly through recall at 10, but on curated queries its confidence interval covers zero. F2: the residual premium sits on queries that lack the code's rare identifiers, and adding those identifiers helps BM25 far more than the dense encoder. From the same LLM-expanded query, the encoder gains a fraction of what BM25 gains, and stripping prose of identifiers changes nothing. With generate-then-grep, whole-file BM25 matches or beats dense retrieval in every Python pool, even on pull requests opened after the LLM was released, so the result is not the LLM memorizing the repository. F3: the two retrievers win on different queries, and nothing the authors tried predicts which. Classifiers, LLM judges, and a preregistered router all fail to route individual queries to the better retriever. A fixed fusion of the two beats either one alone, and it held on the 539 preregistered lockbox queries.

From these, the paper derives three guidelines. G1: measure dense gains against a fair lexical baseline, which means indexing whole files for BM25, stripping templated pull-request text, and stating before the comparison which edited files count as relevant. G2: keep dense retrieval but fuse it with BM25, because a fixed rank fusion beats dense alone on development data and on the lockbox, and no router beat the fusion. G3: when a query names no code, add the code's words before searching; a single LLM call that drafts the missing identifiers lets whole-file BM25 match an embedding index, and because that call recurs per query, it suits repositories that are searched rarely rather than hot paths.

The skeptical read is narrower here than for a methods paper, because this is a measurement paper and its job is to be fair. The main caveat is that the conclusion is scoped to file-level retrieval in the languages and repositories studied, and the "BM25 matches dense" result is strongest in Python; the other languages are in the extension set and I would want the per-language tables before generalizing generate-then-grep across all of them. The second caveat is the generate-then-grep intervention: it replaces the dense premium with an LLM call per query, which is cheap for a rarely-searched repository but not free for a hot path, and the paper is honest that the LLM may know the repository, so the intervention replaces the premium rather than fully explaining it. The third is that no method predicts per-query which retriever wins, which is a genuine null result and a limitation for anyone hoping to build a smart router; the practical answer the paper gives is to just fuse, which is the safe but less elegant outcome.

Still, the contribution is exactly what the field needed: a controlled, preregistered, contamination-aware measurement that separates a real, if small, dense advantage from the artifacts of benchmark construction. The finding that tuning the lexical baseline and expanding the query collapse most of the gap is a result that should change how teams justify building embedding indexes, and the three guidelines are directly actionable. The preregistered lockbox is the feature that makes this more than a re-analysis of old benchmarks.

The methodological apparatus deserves a moment, because the conclusions rest on it. The thirteen dense encoders are evaluated alongside SPLADE and ColBERTv2, so the lexical versus dense comparison is not one encoder against BM25 but a whole family of dense retrievers against the tuned lexical baseline. The query axes are the core of the design: curated problem statements, which is the style most retrieval benchmarks use, and raw pull-request text, which is what a coding agent actually has to search with, and the year windows, 2022, 2023, and on or after the start of 2026, are chosen so that every window sits clearly before or after the encoders' training cutoffs, which is how the paper probes whether the dense advantage is partly contamination rather than generalization. The paper also defines a specific predictor, an adaptation of the MaxIDF idea, that uses the highest inverse document frequency among tokens shared by the query and the first edited source file, and it is this predictor's failure, along with classifiers, LLM judges, and a preregistered router, that supports the finding that nothing tried predicts per-query which retriever will win. One more result is worth flagging: in an agent evaluation where the agents are allowed to call grep directly on 96 tasks, the paper finds that the LLM matters more than the search tool, which is the practical upshot of the whole measurement: in an agentic setting, the retriever choice is a smaller lever than the model doing the reasoning over what it retrieves.

My verdict: Embeddings versus Grep is a rigorous, well-controlled measurement paper whose core claim, that much of the dense advantage in code search is an artifact of evaluation choices and is largely recovered by a fair BM25 plus LLM query expansion, is well supported and practically useful. It will not please teams that have invested in embedding infrastructure, but it is the honest baseline they should have been measured against.
