A-RSI: Adaptive Task Refresh for Zero-Data Multimodal Self-Improvement, by Yu Liu, Youxing Li, and Yuedong Tan, with a large co-author list spanning Shanghai AI Laboratory, the University of Trento, the University of Twente, and Carnegie Mellon University, attacks one specific question in recursive self-improvement: not what new experience should a model generate next, but when has the experience it is currently training on stopped teaching anything? The system monitors its own training logs, swaps in a fresh pool of tasks when the learning signal declines, and reports that on an eleven-benchmark multimodal suite, Qwen3-VL-8B goes from 57.77 to 61.05 after eight continuation windows using only 800 fresh tasks, with no external training examples and no human-written answer labels anywhere in the loop.

The prior art is the zero-data self-improvement line: systems like Absolute Zero, R-Zero, and the multimodal extensions Vision-Zero and MM-Zero, where a model generates its own tasks, solves them, and trains on the results. The standard supervision in that line is self-consistency or majority voting: the model votes on the answer to its own generated question, and the majority becomes the label. That has an obvious failure mode, which is that the model's errors become the next round's ground truth. A-RSI separates two questions that the prior work conflates. The timing question, when to draw new experience, is answered from training dynamics. The correctness question, what is the right answer, is answered by construction rather than by vote, because the new tasks come from a library of executable programs.

The task pipeline is the part that makes zero-data honest here, and it is where this paper differs most from majority-vote self-training. Before training, the base model writes a fixed library of parameterized programs over seed task types in geometry, charts, tables, and counting. Each program first constructs an underlying configuration, then renders an image from it, composes a question, and computes the reference answer from the same stored values. The paper's running example is a chart program that samples two bar values, 18 and 11, draws the bars, asks how much larger one bar is than the other, and computes the answer as the difference, 7, from the stored values. The learner only ever sees the image and the question, never the latent values, and the answer is arithmetic on the configuration, not a model prediction. That removes the self-consistency failure mode by construction: the label is computed, not voted on. Candidates are checked, leakage-checked against the evaluation benchmarks, deduplicated against prior pools, and assembled into sealed replacement pools with fixed family quotas, so the composition of each refresh is controlled rather than left to whatever the model happens to generate. During training, drawing a replacement pool costs no model calls at all, which is what makes the refresh timing a pure data decision.

The training loop is group-based reinforcement learning, specifically GRPO, on the active pool, with rewards for answer correctness, format, and finishing within the output budget. After each accepted window of updates, a controller reads two quantities from the training logs: how many distinct prompts still produce within-group reward variation, which is the prompts that actually generate a nonzero relative-advantage signal, and the logged answer accuracy. When the specified condition on those two signals fires, the controller swaps the entire active pool for the next sealed replacement pool. The model weights are kept; the swap is pure data. The controller never looks at validation or test scores. The paper is careful to call this an operational indicator of declining signal, not a proof the pool has been mastered, which is the right epistemic framing for a log-based trigger.

The experimental design is the strongest part of the paper, because it matches budgets carefully. The main run starts from a checkpoint already initialized on the first pool, at 57.77 average over the eleven full benchmarks, and then compares refresh policies at equal training progress. A-RSI reaches 60.40 at continuation window 6 using 600 fresh tasks, while continuing to train on the original pool scores 58.35 at the same budget, a plus 2.05 point difference. At window 8, A-RSI scores 61.05 with 800 fresh tasks, against 60.64 for refreshing every window, which consumes 3200 fresh tasks, 59.89 for periodic frontier-selection refresh with 1200, and 59.59 for detector-triggered frontier selection with 800. The original-pool continuation arm does not just lose, it halts before producing a valid final checkpoint, failing its own training health gate. The same pattern holds on the second backbone, MiMo-VL-7B, which improves from 59.08 to 60.71.

There are two honest negative results worth calling out. First, the replacement selection rule does not beat random. Under the fixed task-family shares, difficulty-aware and competence-aware replacement strategies do not outperform random sampling of fresh tasks. The paper scopes this limitation explicitly: it is about these generators, this model, and these tested rules, not a general theorem. Second, the paper runs a response-level audit because a score can rise for two different reasons: more answers finishing within the output budget, or completed answers actually being corrected. They decompose the gain into completion versus correction and report the two separately, and they also re-check whether the apparent gains persist under a longer generation budget and format-corrected scoring. That audit infrastructure, including an outcome-independent audit of 1,000 consumed training tasks with controlled reward scoring across 18 response variants, is more work than most self-improvement papers do, and it is where a reviewer will spend their time.

Now the skeptical read. The headline gain is 3.3 points on an eleven-benchmark average over eight windows, and the comparison that matters most is A-RSI at 60.40 versus 60.64 for every-window refresh at nearly four times the fresh-task budget, plus 58.35 for no refresh at the matched budget. The method's value is real but modest, and it is mostly a data-efficiency result: the same budget spent continuously buys less. The trigger itself is a condition on logged reward variation and accuracy, and the paper does not establish that the trigger fires at the right moments more often than a simpler schedule; it shows the adaptive policy beats the fixed alternatives, which is a weaker claim than the title implies. The task library is a narrow slice of multimodal reasoning, geometry, charts, tables, counting, and the 11-benchmark suite is aligned with those families, so the transfer breadth is unproven. The program-computed answers are a genuine fix for the self-consistency label problem, but the programs were written by the base model, so the ceiling of the library is the base model's ability to write correct parameterized task generators, and the paper's own checks are specified as machine-verified properties of a subset of the reward and refresh rules, not formal proofs of every rendered question. If you want to adopt this, the pieces worth stealing are the programmatic task library with configuration-derived answers, the sealed-pool construction with leakage checks, and the completion-versus-correction audit. The log-triggered refresh is a reasonable controller, but I would validate its timing against a validation probe before trusting it in a long run.

My verdict is that this is the most carefully controlled paper in the zero-data self-improvement line I have read this cycle: matched budgets, a negative result on the selection rule, an audit of what the gain actually consists of, and honest scoping of the trigger's claim. The 3.3 point average gain is not a breakthrough, but the separation of when to refresh from what the answer is, plus the program-derived supervision, is a structural improvement over majority-vote self-training that I would want to see in any future run. The fact that the every-window arm, with four times the data, lands only slightly ahead of the original-pool arm while the adaptive policy beats both at a fraction of the cost is the result that actually justifies the refresh framing, and the sealed-pool leakage checks are the kind of hygiene the rest of this literature still lacks.
