Hy-Nomas: Towards Free-form Interactive World Generation, by Qiaoling Chen, Jiaxiang Cheng, and Xueqing Deng, of the Tencent Hy Visual Generation Team, with the full author list running to thirty eight contributors across the Tencent Hy teams. This is a team paper, not a named-author paper, and the affiliations collapse to a single lab, which you should keep in mind when weighing how independent the design decisions are from the rest of the Tencent Hy video stack.

The problem is interactive world generation, and the specific gap is that most video world models either take fixed camera commands or free text, but not both, and they struggle to preserve history across many interaction turns. Hy-Nomas is an eight-billion-parameter video world model, built on the HunyuanVideo 1.5 backbone, that supports free-form text instructions and continuous camera control simultaneously, across multiple turns. It generates variable-length segments, each conditioned on the current input and a cached history, and the central design decision is how to keep descriptive context alive across segments while restricting action instructions to the temporal window they are actually meant to affect. That separation is the whole point, because a naive model that lets every text instruction bleed into every future segment will keep acting on a command that was only meant for the last turn.

The architecture is where the paper earns its detail. The backbone is a standard autoregressive diffusion transformer with a video autoencoder that maps pixels to latents, a text encoder, and a KV cache that stores previously generated segments as context for the next one. On top of that they add a camera representation: keyboard inputs are converted into a binary key state, a tensor of shape eight, encoding the W, A, S, D keys and the four arrow keys, plus a per-frame continuous camera pose. The action-aware causal attention is the key mechanism. Descriptive context, the scene description and visual style, remains accessible to every segment in the cache, but action instructions are masked so they only influence the segment they target. That is a temporal window on the action tokens, and it is what lets a command like turn left apply to the next segment without corrupting the one after it. The camera and text controls are interleaved, and during training the camera conditions are dropped with fifty percent probability per segment so the model learns to generate coherently with or without explicit camera input.

The Prompt Enhancer is a separate vision-language model that sits in front of the generator. It takes the user's free-form request and the multimodal interaction history and produces structured conditions for the next segment: the structured caption, the camera trajectory, the subject states, the visual style. They evaluate this component directly, and it is where the paper is most concrete. Their Prompt Enhancer reaches a nearly one hundred percent success rate in adhering to the required structured output format, compared to about fifty percent for a Qwen model and seventy percent for a Kimi model at the same role. That is a large gap, and it matters because a generator that receives malformed or missing structured conditions will drift regardless of how good its backbone is. They also report that the Prompt Enhancer outperforms the reference Qwen model on prompt following and physical plausibility when used in the loop, and they ablate it against the distilled generator baseline.

The training is a multi-stage curriculum, and the stages are named and ordered. Stage one learns causal generation on a broad video corpus. Stage two introduces text interaction on curated, balanced videos, learning action timing. Stage three adds continuous camera control on videos with and without camera labels. Stage four refines visual quality and text fidelity. Stage five, segment forcing, trains the model to continue from its own generated history, which is what makes multi-turn coherence work. And the final stage is diffusion distillation, using a combination of the PC-M initialization, DDMD, and an adversarial refinement, to get the few-step generation that interactive use requires. The infrastructure is real: they shard the model with FSDP2, use a Muon-style optimizer with a distributed full-matrix orthogonalization, and they report that a particular memory optimization saves roughly one point six gigabytes per rank without a meaningful speedup, which is the kind of detail that tells you they actually ran this on a cluster rather than on a single node.

On the data side, they are explicit about the filtering. They drop videos where the inter-frame displacement of tracked visible points exceeds twenty percent of the maximum of height and width, and they drop videos where the median total displacement of tracked points is below thirty percent of that same bound. That second filter is the interesting one: it removes static or near-static clips that would teach the model to ignore camera motion, and it is a reasonable way to ensure the training distribution actually contains the motion the model is being asked to control. They also do hierarchical clustering, taxonomy labeling, and camera trajectory annotation over the corpus, and they build a structured captioning pipeline that produces subject IDs, static profiles, perspective and style, and timestamped subject actions per segment.

The evaluation is pairwise human evaluation, which is the honest way to compare world models but also the one most sensitive to evaluator bias. On prompt following, Hy-Nomas reaches a win rate of sixty five percent against HappyOyster-Directing and seventy five percent against LingBot-World version two. On physical plausibility the numbers are more modest, forty five percent and sixty eight point three percent, so the physical consistency is not as clean a win as the instruction following. On camera control they report win rates of seventy five percent and seventy three point three percent. There is one number I would flag as a warning: on a spatial-memory benchmark where the model has to return to a previously seen pose, they report a win rate of thirteen point three percent, a tie rate of fifty six point seven percent, and a loss rate of thirty percent, which means the spatial memory is roughly a coin flip with a downward bias. That is the weakest claim in the paper, and it is the one most likely to matter in real deployment, because a world model that forgets where it has been is a world model that cannot be navigated.

Here is the skeptical read. The win rates are the only evaluation, and pairwise human eval on a small number of scenarios is a low-power measurement. The spatial-memory result is a genuine negative, and the authors do not overclaim it, which I respect, but it means the free-form interactive claim is currently bounded by the model's inability to reliably return to and recognize previous states. The camera-control win rates are strong, but the training drops camera input fifty percent of the time, so the model is partially untrained on explicit control, and the gains may be partly an artifact of the baseline not being camera-aware in the same way. The Prompt Enhancer results are the most defensible part of the paper because they are measured on a concrete format-adherence task, but they also mean the system's quality depends heavily on a component that is itself a large language model with its own failure modes. The distillation stage is described at a high level, and the adversarial refinement is not ablated on its own, so you cannot tell how much of the final quality comes from the distillation versus the segment-forcing stage.

The bottom line: this is a serious systems paper from a team that has the video stack to actually run multi-turn interactive generation at scale, and the action-aware causal attention plus the structured Prompt Enhancer are the two ideas I would take away, because they are the parts that separate a multi-turn world model from a one-shot video generator. The caveat is that the evaluation is human win rates rather than objective metrics, and the spatial-memory result is a clear weak point that the free-form interaction claim will run into the moment a user asks the model to go back to where it was. If you are building interactive world generation and you need to keep instructions scoped to the turn they belong to, the temporal-window masking on action tokens is a concrete, cheap design decision worth copying, even if you never touch the rest of the pipeline.
