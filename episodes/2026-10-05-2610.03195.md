Title: Source Preference in the Wild: How LLM Agents Favor Items by Source, and How to Reduce It
Authors: Jonghyun Song, Haewon Park, Jeonghoon Shim
Labs: Graduate School of Data Science, Seoul National University
Arxiv: 2610.03195
Day: 2026-10-05
Upvotes: 14
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03195
---
Source Preference in the Wild is from Jonghyun Song, Haewon Park, and Jeonghoon Shim, with collaborators at the Graduate School of Data Science at Seoul National University. It is an empirical study of how much language-model agents favor items based on which source they come from, rather than on how well the items actually satisfy the request. The setting is end-to-end search, where an agent picks a product, a hotel, or a paper to present on a user's behalf.

The concern is concrete and consequential. Agents increasingly decide what a user sees. They choose which products to surface, which accommodations to recommend, and which papers to cite. If an agent has a hidden preference for items from certain sources, that preference narrows what users get and can quietly steer them away from better options just because those options come from a disfavored source. The paper's worry is a feedback loop. If these biased selections then flow back into training data, existing preferences get reinforced, concentrating visibility on favored sources and leaving equally good alternatives from other sources permanently overlooked. Prior work had hints of source bias, but this is the first study that isolates it cleanly in end-to-end search across many models and domains.

The experimental design is the interesting part, and it is careful. They run twelve agent models across three domains, shopping, hotels, and scholarly works. The key control is that they compare items from different sources that satisfy the same requirements and sit at the same position in the result list. By holding requirement-satisfaction and position constant, any difference in selection is attributable to the source itself. They rotate items through every position so that position bias is averaged out, and they use a source-blind judge to score requirement satisfaction so the ground truth is independent of the source. The dataset is 4,822 requests, split across the three domains, with the shopping domain contributing 1,500 and the scholarly domain 2,536.

The headline finding is that every model prefers some sources and avoids others in every domain, and the models largely agree on which sources they favor and which they avoid. More striking, the preference can outweigh the actual quality of the match. When a slightly worse item, one that satisfies one requirement fewer, comes from a preferred source and the better item comes from a disfavored source, the worse item is still selected about two-thirds of the time. Flip the sources and the better item is almost never chosen. So the source label is doing real work in the decision, enough to override a direct comparison of requirement satisfaction.
The second set of results isolates the mechanism, and it is the part that most clearly establishes that the source label itself, not anything correlated with it, drives the behavior. When the authors hide the source information, the preference weakens. When they relabel an item to claim it comes from a preferred source, its selection rate goes up. Those two manipulations together show that the agent is reading the source field and using it as a signal, not just reacting to the item's content or its position. The source is a genuine input to the decision.

The paper then traces two routes by which this preference forms. The first is training-driven. If the training data rewards selecting better items, and if better items happen to correlate with a particular source, the model can learn the source as a shortcut for requirement satisfaction. It is not reasoning about the item, it has learned that this source usually means good. The second route is missing information. When the item description is incomplete, the agent falls back on prior beliefs about the source to fill the gap, and those preconceptions become the tie-breaker. Both routes point to the same practical conclusion.

The mitigations follow directly from the two routes. If the preference comes from a learned shortcut, supplying the missing information that the model was trying to shortcut over reduces the reliance on the source. If it comes from a prior belief triggered by missing information, a prompt that explicitly counters those preconceptions reduces the bias. Both interventions, filling in the information and prompting against the prior, measurably reduce source preference, which is a useful result because it gives a practitioner two concrete levers rather than just a diagnosis.

The skeptical read. The study is methodologically strong, and the position-rotation plus source-blind-judge controls are exactly what you need to claim that the effect is the source and not something else. The concerns are generalization and mechanism completeness. Twelve models is a good cross-section, but it is still a snapshot of a particular generation of models, and the agreement across them on which sources are favored suggests the bias may be partly a property of the training data common to many models, which would make it persistent rather than model-specific. The two-thirds-of-the-time figure is striking but it is an average, and the variance across domains and models is not fully characterized in the main text, so the strength of the override can differ a lot by setting. The two routes to the preference are plausible and supported by the manipulation results, but they are not fully separated, and the paper does not establish that these two account for the entire effect. And the mitigations, while real, are partial, they reduce the preference rather than eliminate it, and their effect sizes and the cost of the prompting intervention are not deeply quantified.

Verdict: this is a rigorous, well-controlled demonstration that LLM search agents have a real, cross-model, cross-domain source preference that can override direct requirement satisfaction, with clean manipulations showing the source label itself is the driver. The main caveats are that the two-thirds override is an average with uncharacterized variance, that the two causal routes are not fully separated, and that the mitigations reduce rather than remove the bias, so treat it as a confirmed failure mode to audit for in any agent that selects on a user's behalf, with the information-supply and counter-prior prompts as the practical first-line fixes.
The measurement method deserves a second look, because it is what makes the two-thirds result defensible. The source-blind judge is the linchpin. The judge scores how well each item satisfies the request without ever seeing which source it came from, so the ground truth for requirement satisfaction is independent of the very bias being measured. If the judge itself had a source preference, the whole comparison would be contaminated, so the source-blindness is not a minor detail, it is the control that lets you attribute the selection difference to the agent's source preference rather than to a mismeasurement of quality. The position rotation is the other half of the control, because agents are known to favor early positions, and without rotating items through every slot you could not tell a source effect from a position effect. Getting both controls in is what elevates this from an observation to a causal claim.

The feedback-loop concern is the part with the longest horizon. The paper identifies it as a risk but does not close the loop, it shows that the preference exists and that it can override quality, and it argues that if biased selections enter training data the effect compounds. That is a reasonable concern and a hard one to test, because it requires observing a real training-data feedback cycle over time, which no single study can do. What the paper can do, and does, is show that the preference is strong enough to matter, which is the necessary precondition for the loop to be a real threat rather than a theoretical one.
The relabeling experiment is the cleanest single result in the paper, and it is worth isolating. When an item that objectively satisfies the request is given the label of a preferred source, its selection rate rises, even though nothing about the item's content changed. That is direct evidence that the source field is being read and used as a feature in the decision, not merely correlated with quality in a way the model happens to exploit. Combined with the hiding result, where removing the label weakens the preference, you have a manipulation pair that establishes both necessity and sufficiency of the source signal in the decision, which is a stronger evidentiary bar than a single correlation would provide.
