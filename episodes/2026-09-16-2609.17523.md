Paper six is ScienceBuddy: Recursive-in-Recursive Self-Improvement for Interactive Scientific Agents, from Shuhan Xue, Jianyuan Zhong, and Ziyuan Nan, with corresponding authors Yin, Wu, and Yang, a collaboration between PhAI Labs and academic groups including Fudan, Oxford, Stanford, and Princeton. The product page and code are public, and the paper reads as a system report for a deployed interactive research workspace, with case studies in place of aggregate benchmark tables.

The system is a multimodal scientific research workspace: documents, images, tables, and biological sequences, with 224 tools and 22 modules. The document handling and execution interfaces are domain-agnostic, but the current tools and data lake specialize in biomedicine: genomics, molecular and cancer biology, pharmacology, bioimaging, literature retrieval, and database queries. Researchers interact with it the way they would with a lab tool. They pose questions, upload figures, inspect intermediate results in a trajectory view, and refine requirements, and those recorded interactions become the raw material for improvement. The paper shows this concretely: a researcher uploads an immune-signaling diagram, the agent decomposes it into signaling branches, queries the data lake and PubMed, and produces a target-by-evidence table where missing records are explicitly marked as missing rather than invented. The interface exposes three views: a chat pane for the conversation, a trajectory timeline that separates user messages, system events, and tool calls, and a compute panel where any tool event can be expanded to show its input, output, and metadata, so a researcher can trace exactly which lookup a claim was built on. The frontier model and the agent harness are both pluggable, which is what makes the double recursion meaningful.

The core idea is the double recursion, and it is worth spelling out carefully because it is not just self-improvement with a marketing adjective. The inner recursion improves the harness while the model stays fixed. A user simulator and feedback interpreter generate bounded researcher-style feedback, a reflector role diagnoses which criterion went unmet, and an editor proposes one bounded procedural change at a time: revise an instruction, or add, remove, or revise one scoped skill. The candidate passes validation and a paired evaluation, and it is accepted only if it improves the score. The outer recursion does the opposite: it trains the model with reinforcement learning under the now-improved harness. Rubrics are composed per task from the original collaboration trajectory, each criterion carries a nonnegative importance weight, and the trajectory reward is the weighted mean of the criterion satisfaction scores, using executable checks wherever they exist and a fixed judge for criteria that require scientific interpretation. The policy update is GRPO with fresh rollout groups and a clipped surrogate. The coupling is the point. Harness changes alter the training experience the model sees, so the inner loop shapes what the outer loop learns, and a stronger model creates new failure modes and new opportunities for harness adaptation, so the outer loop feeds the next round of the inner. The workspace keeps serving researchers throughout; updates run in the background, and all harness versions and environments are retained and re-evaluated under the updated model before reuse.

The scheduling deserves a sentence because it is what makes the system safe to deploy. The online service runs on the current model and harness pair over a fixed collection interval and never blocks while updates happen. When the interval ends, the interactions collected during it become the evidence buffer, the background inner loop runs its budget of revision rounds, the outer loop trains the model, the selected harness is re-evaluated under the new model, and only then is the pair swapped in. Every rejected edit stays in the history as a decision record rather than being discarded, which means a diagnosis can revisit a previously rejected change if new evidence supports it. Development tasks, policy-training tasks, and the final held-out test set are explicitly separate, and the test set never informs editing or selection.

The two real researcher interactions are worth a quick pass because they show how the task rubrics get built. In one, a researcher asks for a study design on JAK1, immunotherapy outcomes, and the immune microenvironment in small-cell lung cancer, then narrows the scope to focus on JAK1 first, with mechanisms later. The agent's plan sequences the work accordingly: gene-specific association tests before mechanistic follow-up, Seurat and Scanpy for the single-cell analysis, UCell and AUCell for signature scoring, and CellChat for cell-communication analysis. In the second, a researcher asks for a presentation connecting the background and results of an ARL4C study, and specifies panel selection, per-slide conclusions, mechanism schematics, and speaker notes. The agent links candidate screening to cellular and molecular evidence, using depletion and knockout comparisons for attribution and rescue assays for interpretation. These interactions become the source of the task objectives and evaluation criteria that the rubric composer later turns into RL training tasks, which is the pipeline from human intent to reward signal, and it is the part of the system most dependent on the quality of the research it is pointed at.

The evidence, and this is where the paper gets specific. The task collection has 895 tasks across four scientific families: 96 literature-reading tasks, 511 database-query tasks, 108 experimental-protocol troubleshooting tasks, and 180 genome-wide association tasks, over 16 subtopics. The standalone harness case study is the cleaner experiment. The task model is a fixed 4-billion-parameter Qwen3.5 model, the harness starts empty, and the editor is GPT-6 Astra, a separate, much stronger model. Over 288 adaptation conversations, in batches of 12, the harness accumulates four instruction entries and nine scoped skills. On a held-out validation set that never guided the edits, the selected harness reaches 51.1 percent correct against 31.1 for the initial one, a 20-point gap from procedure changes alone, with zero weight changes. The model-learning case study holds the initial harness fixed and compares two checkpoints of the same 4-billion model, four attempts per problem, and coverage at pass at 4 rises from 48.3 to 67.8 percent. The full coupled-cycle result is reported as case-level improvement across the four research questions rather than as a single aggregate number.

My assessment: this is the closest thing on today's list to a deployed instance of the recursive self-improvement machinery the RSI survey describes, and the two papers read as a pair. The design has real hygiene in it. The user simulator is bounded to permitted replies and never sees the reference answer. The feedback interpreter is explicitly barred from the private verdict. The editor is restricted to instruction and skill text, with tools, budgets, rubrics, and evaluators fixed, and it is told not to encode task-specific answers. The held-out validation set never informs revision. Those boundaries are exactly the ones the RSI paper says a real improvement loop needs. But three caveats matter. First, the harness editor is GPT-6 Astra, a frontier model an order of magnitude larger than the task model it is improving, so the impressive 31 to 51 percent gain is partly purchased with external intelligence, and the paper does not isolate how much would survive with a same-scale editor. Second, the rubric reward and the scientific verifier are the same fixed evaluation surface that both recursion loops optimize against, so the RSI paper's warning about evaluator exploitation applies, and a 4-billion model optimizing a weighted rubric is precisely the setting where reward hacking should be checked for. Third, everything is biomedicine, so the claim about generalizing across scientific disciplines is asserted by the framework, not shown by the data. What I would trust: the architecture, the information-boundary design, and the harness-only result, which is the most reproducible number in the paper. What I would re-verify before believing any capability claim: a same-scale editor, and held-out evaluation of the coupled system with the verifier itself audited.

The bottom line: a credible systems blueprint for coupling harness evolution with model training in a live research workflow, with better experimental hygiene than the category usually shows. Its own evidence is case-level and single-domain, though, and the reliance on a frontier-scale editor means the recursion is more assisted than autonomous.
