RewardVerse: Rubric-Guided Policy Optimization for Video Reward Modeling. The first three authors are Zhenchen Tang, Yang Li, and Songlin Yang, from the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Tencent, and the University of Science and Technology in Hong Kong, with the corresponding author Jing Dong and project lead Songlin Yang.

Reinforcement learning is now how video generation models get aligned to human taste, and the reward model is the whole ballgame, because everything downstream optimizes against whatever number it emits. The problem the authors name is scalar drift. Most video reward models take a video and map it directly to a single scalar score, either by regression or by a reasoning chain that ends in a number. But video quality is subjective and multi-dimensional, and with no explicit criteria anchoring the score, the scoring scale collapses into a narrow band or shifts whenever the prompt or context changes. The reward, which is supposed to be a stable training signal, becomes unstable exactly when the RL loop needs it most. The fix borrows from how professional human annotators actually work: nobody scores a video cold. They first break the evaluation into explicit criteria, then score against those criteria. RewardVerse makes that intermediate step explicit and learnable.

The architecture inserts a dynamic rubric between the evaluation query and the scorer. Given a query, which pairs an evaluation dimension, say logic, with the actual prompt, a rubric generator first writes out explicit evaluation criteria, each with a theme, a weight, and a hint, for example check that the eyelines stay consistent across the cut. The scorer then takes the video plus that rubric and produces a per-theme score, and the final scalar is a weighted aggregation over the themes. The rubric is the semantic anchor: because the criteria are written out, the scoring has something stable to hold to, and the scale stops drifting. The scorer reads out soft logits rather than a hard integer, which keeps the per-theme scores in a continuous, comparable range. This one change, decoupling standard generation from objective execution, is what mitigates the drift, and it natively supports both pointwise scoring and pairwise preference, because both are just aggregations over the same theme scores.

The training is a two-stage algorithm they call Rubric-Guided Policy Optimization, RGPO. Stage one warms up the scorer using self-evolving seed rubrics, giving it a stable footing before the rubric generator is in the loop. Stage two jointly optimizes the rubric generator to produce query-adaptive criteria while continuously aligning the scorer with human ratings. The data efficiency is the selling point: they train on as little as thirty pairs per dimension, four hundred eighty pairs total, and still outperform the strongest non-oracle baseline on the seen split by roughly seven percentage points, closing much of the gap to an oracle rubric, which is the upper bound where the true human rubric is handed to the model for free.

The evaluation is on a sixteen-dimensional benchmark called EvalVerse plus external datasets. They report both pointwise correlation metrics, PLCC and SRCC, and pairwise accuracy, and the ablations are the cleanest part of the paper. Dropping the scorer warm-up or the joint optimization stage costs real correlation, and a flat rubric without the hierarchical per-theme tips underperforms the structured one, so the decomposition into themes with weights and tips is doing genuine work. They also test transfer: the drift mitigation learned in pointwise scoring carries into pairwise preference, even on out-of-distribution benchmarks and evaluation dimensions the model has not seen, which is the kind of generalization claim that separates a real structural fix from overfitting to one metric. They measure inference cost too: the extra rubric generation step adds only a small latency on a single accelerator, so the interpretability and stability come cheap.

The skeptical read. The rubric-as-intermediate-representation idea is the right one, and it is not new in spirit, chain-of-thought and structured rationales have been doing the same job in reasoning models, so the contribution is really showing that the same move fixes a real, named failure mode in a different modality and building the training loop to make it robust. The data efficiency is the strongest result, getting near-oracle performance from four hundred eighty pairs is a genuinely useful property for a reward model, because collecting human video ratings is expensive and the alternative, training on massive preference data, is exactly what most video RMs do and what causes the drift in the first place. What I would want to see is the downstream RL result, because the whole point of a stable reward is better-trained video models, and the paper stops at showing the reward itself is stable and accurate, not that a model trained against it beats one trained against a drifted reward. The EvalVerse benchmark is the authors' own, so the state-of-the-art claim is on their own yardstick, and the external-dataset numbers are what I would actually trust. If you are running RL for video generation and your reward model is drifting, the rubric intermediate is a low-risk thing to try. Verdict: a clean, data-efficient fix for scalar drift that generalizes across pointwise and pairwise evaluation, with the honest caveat that the headline state-of-the-art is on the authors' own benchmark and the end-to-end RL gain is the missing proof.

Worth unpacking is why the intermediate representation actually works, because a lot of structured-reasoning tricks collapse under inspection. The key property of the rubric is that it is generated before the video is scored, from the query alone, without video access. That means the criteria are not a rationalization after the model has already formed an impression; they are a commitment made in advance. An annotator who writes the rubric first cannot quietly adjust the standard to match what they see, and neither can the model. The rubric themes carry weights that sum over the evaluation, and the hints are concrete, procedural instructions, check the eyelines, evaluate whether the cut is motivated, which pushes the scorer toward verifying specific things rather than emitting a global vibe. The per-theme scores stay in a bounded, comparable range because each one is a soft-logit readout against a fixed theme scale, and the aggregation is a weighted sum, so the final score inherits the stability of the parts. The scalar drift they are fighting has two faces, the scale collapsing into a narrow band so every video scores about three point five, and the scale shifting across prompts so the same quality gets a four in one context and a three in another, and the rubric addresses both, because the themes and weights are stable anchors that the scoring must respect regardless of context.

The training algorithm's two stages each have a job. The scorer warm-up, trained against self-evolving seed rubrics, exists because a joint loop that starts from scratch has the classic cold-start problem: a bad rubric generator and a bad scorer train each other into a local minimum. Giving the scorer a head start on a stable rubric distribution means that when the generator starts producing query-adaptive criteria, the scorer can actually exploit them instead of treating them as noise. The joint stage then optimizes both, the generator toward criteria that are useful for the query, and the scorer toward ratings that track human labels, with the rubric as the shared medium. The hierarchical tips, the per-theme procedural hints, are ablated separately and they matter: a flat rubric with themes and weights but no tips loses correlation, which says the model needs to be told not just what to weigh but how to check it, the difference between a category and a procedure.

The downstream framing is the paper's own most important claim and its softest point. A stable, accurate reward model only matters if the model trained against it improves, and the paper's evidence for that link is indirect, the rubric mitigates drift, drift makes RL unreliable, therefore this is a better reward. That is a reasonable chain, but it is not the end-to-end experiment, train a video generator with a RewardVerse reward and a drifted reward under an identical compute budget and compare. The inference-time cost is reassuring, the rubric generation adds only a small latency on a single accelerator at the frame budget they test, so adopting the pipeline is not a throughput tax. And the interpretability is not just a nice side effect: because the rubric is explicit, you can audit why a reward went up or down, which is something a black-box scalar reward model cannot give you and something an RL pipeline really wants when the reward starts to misbehave. The missing experiment is the one that would make this a method paper instead of a component paper, and it is the natural next step.
