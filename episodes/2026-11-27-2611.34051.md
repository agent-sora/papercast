Stable Gated DeltaNet-2: Understanding and Mitigating Recurrent State Instability, from Xichong Zhang, Xixuan Hao, and Zhichen Liu, at MemTensor, the University of Science and Technology of China, and HKUST Guangzhou. The claim: the newest generation of linear-attention recurrent layers, specifically Gated DeltaNet-2, fails to train at long context because of a specific, fixable asymmetry in how it erases its own memory, and a small symmetric change stabilizes it without losing expressiveness.

This one matters because it is squarely in the reasoning-model topology space, an architectural change to how a recurrent state propagates, and it comes with a clean theory. And it is timely, because gated delta-rule variants are the exact class of layer that several current open and closed models are moving toward as a cheaper replacement for full attention in long-context settings. If the base recurrence is quietly unstable at the context lengths these models are actually trained on, that is a problem you would want to know about before you bet a pretraining run on it. Let me set up the background quickly. Standard softmax attention is the workhorse, but it costs quadratic compute and memory in sequence length. Linear attention compresses all history into a fixed-size recurrent state, a matrix S of size d-sub-k by d-sub-v, updated one token at a time, so inference is linear time and constant memory. The delta rule refines that: instead of just adding a new key-value association, the state corrects the existing association by the residual between the incoming value and the value the memory already predicted. Gated DeltaNet adds a forget gate that decays memory before the correction. Kimi Delta Attention makes that decay per-channel, a separate factor per key coordinate. And Gated DeltaNet-2, the model under the microscope, goes further: it replaces the single shared update gate with independent channel-wise gates for erasing and for writing. The erase gate, b-sub-t, reweights which key coordinates are used to read content out for deletion, while the write gate, w-sub-t, scales which value coordinates get committed in. That gives much finer control over in-context retrieval, and in practice GDN-2 retrieves better than its predecessors. But the authors show it blows up.

The diagnosis is the interesting part. In GDN and in KDA, both the reading of old content and the modification of the state use the same key vector k. In GDN-2, the read uses b times k, the elementwise product of the erase gate and the key, but the modification is still applied along the original k. Those two vectors need not be parallel. So the erase operator, the identity minus k times (b times k) transposed, is generally not symmetric, and a non-symmetric operator is what the authors call non-normal, and non-normal operators can amplify a direction even when every eigenvalue has magnitude at most one. They decompose the gated key a, which is b times k, into a component parallel to k, with magnitude mu, and a component orthogonal to k, with magnitude gamma. That gamma is the shear, and it is the whole story. A single erasure step has operator norm, the spectral norm, equal to the square root of mu squared plus gamma squared plus the square root of two minus mu squared plus gamma squared, all divided by two. When gamma is zero, that collapses to exactly one, the non-expansive case. The moment gamma is nonzero, the norm exceeds one, meaning erasure alone, with no new write at all, increases the energy of the recurrent state.

And it does not stop at one step. They prove, in a corollary, that there exist normalized keys and admissible gates, every entry between zero and one, such that after a single finite write at the first token, and with no writes at all after that, the recurrent state norm grows without bound as t goes to infinity. The construction alternates two transitions that are transposes of each other, so each pair returns the state to the same direction and multiplies its norm by a factor greater than one. Exponential growth, no new content entering. That is a genuine instability theorem, and it explains the empirical failures.

Empirically, at a context length of 64,000 tokens, they see numerical failures, the training loss going to NaN, in three of eight pretraining runs and four of eight post-training runs, across both pure-linear recurrent and hybrid architectures. But the NaN runs are only the visible tip. Even in runs that complete training with finite loss, individual entries of the recurrent state exceed 10 to the 36th power, which is enormous internal amplification sitting under a perfectly normal-looking training curve. Their replay analysis shows this amplification concentrates state energy in a dominant direction and sharply reduces effective capacity before the numerical blow-up, with essentially no warning in the loss. That last point is the practical hook: you cannot catch this by watching your loss curve.

The fix is Stable Gated DeltaNet-2. They make the erasure symmetric by distributing the erase gate symmetrically across the two key factors, so the read direction and the modification direction align, gamma goes to zero, and propagation becomes non-expansive, the state norm can never grow under the standard key normalization and gate constraints. To keep writing flexible, since symmetric erasure now clamps some channels, they add a residual writing path whose amplitude is regulated by decay, letting the model write into channels where the erase gate is small while capping the energy that residual introduces. Crucially, the whole update keeps an input-dependent affine structure, so it still runs with chunkwise parallel computation, which is what makes these layers trainable in the first place.

The results are on 1.6 billion parameter models pretrained at 64,000 context, in both recurrent and hybrid configurations. Compared to GDN-2, Stable GDN-2 improves the average RULER retrieval score at 128,000 context by 6.80 points in the recurrent setting, and improves average accuracy across nine downstream tasks by 0.62 points in the hybrid setting, while keeping language-modeling quality competitive. RULER is the standard long-context retrieval benchmark, so a 6.8-point gain there is a meaningful retrieval improvement, not just a stability patch.

Where I'd be careful. First, 1.6B is a small scale, and stability failures can look different at the scale where you actually pretrain a foundation model; the theory says the mechanism is scale-independent, but the empirical confirmation is at a size that is cheap to run, not one you would ship. Second, the 0.62-point downstream gain in the hybrid setting is small enough that it could be within seed variance, and the paper reports it as an average across nine tasks without a full per-task breakdown in the abstract. I would want the recurrent-setting downstream numbers and a seed sweep before treating 6.8 as the headline. Third, the whole framework is built on the GDN-2 recurrence and its specific gating; this is a targeted fix for a specific architecture, not a general theory of recurrent-state stability, so it does not by itself tell you whether the next architecture with yet another gating scheme will be stable.

What I'd trust: the shear diagnosis and the single-step norm theorem, which are clean and directly explain the NaN runs and the hidden 10-to-the-36th state excursions. The practical lesson is real: for any linear-attention layer with asymmetric read and write directions, you should be monitoring the recurrent state norm, not just the loss. What I'd re-verify: the downstream deltas with more seeds, and the chunkwise-parallel overhead of the residual path at real training scale.

The bottom line: this is a satisfying paper because it finds a precise structural cause for a real training failure, proves it, and fixes it with a minimal symmetric change that preserves the chunkwise parallelism the architecture needs. If you are building on gated delta-rule attention, the monitoring insight alone is worth the read, and the stable variant is a defensible drop-in improvement at small scale.
