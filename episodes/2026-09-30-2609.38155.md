Beyond the Timeline, Augmenting Long-Video Memory with Grounded Entity Biographies. The first three authors are Hui Ren, Lei Fan, and Henry Pao, working between the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and Amazon, with Alexander Schwing and Gang Hua also on the byline.

The motivating problem is identity over time in long recordings. Question answering on long video often requires connecting events that involve the same physical object or person across hours or days. A chronologically organized memory, or a memory built from text-derived entities, leaves physical identity unresolved. Two different mugs can both be described as a red mug, and observations of the same mug remain disconnected across events because nothing binds them to a single physical instance. The paper's running example is a question about whether the mug used for coffee ended up in the dishwasher: a descriptive memory retrieves both a striped red mug used for coffee and a solid red mug placed in the dishwasher, and the answer turns on which mug is which. Retrieving relevant events does not necessarily recover the biography of the particular entity a question concerns.

Grounded Entity Biographies, or GEB, is the proposed framework. The key idea is to group visually grounded observations of the same physical instance, across clips, into retrievable biographies while preserving the context of each individual moment. The memory construction has two edge families. One family is the observation-to-timeline edge, which keeps the chronological structure of events. The other family is the identity edge, which links observations that the system has grounded as referring to the same physical instance, so that the biography of an entity can be traversed independently of time. During question answering, the biography is retrieved alongside episodic evidence, and the answer model follows the entity through events using identity links that were established during memory construction, not during answering.

The construction pipeline works on captions and visual observations from the video. Entity grouping happens at memory-build time, and the system records for each observation both its moment context and its identity association. Biographies carry the accumulated descriptions, so the biography text itself is a first-class retrievable artifact, separate from the index that maps entities to observations. The paper is careful to separate the index, which is the structure, from the biography text, which is the content, and the ablations treat them as independent contributions.

A practical consequence of the design: because biographies are built at memory-construction time, the cost of identity resolution is paid once per recording, and answering-time retrieval is a lookup over the biography index plus the episodic evidence for the question's time window. The paper's coverage analysis in Table 3 shows the mechanism at work on the retrieval side: GEB's fraction of questions with overlap for every annotated evidence interval reaches 0.521, comparable to WorldMM's 0.526 but with a lower retrieved duration, 0.343 against 0.399 of clip duration, while MAGIC-Video reaches only 0.354 on the same coverage metric. In other words, GEB retrieves the same complete evidence with less of the video, which is the regime where a downstream answer model has the most to work with.

The evaluation covers four benchmarks, including day-long and week-long recordings. The multiple-choice results on EgoLifeQA, a week-long egocentric benchmark, give GEB 72.0 percent accuracy with a Qwen 3.5 35-billion-parameter controller and answer model, improving over the strongest competing memory framework, MAGIC-Video, by 4.4 percentage points. The gains break down by question family, with the largest in EventRecall at 7.9 points. On Ego-R1-Bench, the gain over MAGIC-Video is 6.6 points with the same controller and answer model. For open-ended answering, GEB reaches 36.83 percent on the Test@Week split, exceeding WorldMM by 5.41 points, and leads by 0.83 points on the Test@Day gameplay split. The reported bootstrap confidence intervals exclude zero for EgoLifeQA, Ego-R1-Bench, and Test@Week, but include zero for Test@Day, which the paper states plainly. On MultiHop-EgoQA, which additionally tests temporal grounding, GEB's answer score increases from WorldMM's 2.74 to 3.11, and the mean intersection-over-union improves by 1.6 points, beating every model the benchmark reports, including a system fine-tuned on the benchmark's own training set.

The ablation table is the part I would read first. Removing the association edges, which link an observation to the entity it belongs to, drops EgoLifeQA accuracy from 72.0 to 68.6, a loss of 3.4 points. Keeping the index but stripping the biography text, so the answer model sees the entity-to-observation structure but not the accumulated description, drops it to 68.0, a further 4.0 points. Keying identity by name rather than by the learned physical-instance grouping costs 2.8 points. Removing the biography text from the answer model's context but keeping it in the index costs 1.2 points, which is the smallest single removal, consistent with the biography being a redundant but helpful signal when the index is intact. Appending descriptions to captions rather than keeping them as separate biography entries costs 3.8 points, and removing the observation-to-timeline edges also costs 3.8. Removing the caption text entirely is the largest single removal at 10.2 points, which is a reminder that the raw visual grounding is the substrate the biography structure is built on. The pattern is clear: the biography text and the association structure each carry independent value, and the identity resolution, the grouping of observations into the same physical instance, is worth 2.8 points on its own.

The retrieval analysis is where the mechanism becomes visible. On EgoLifeQA, GEB increases evidence hits from 37.6 percent to 58.9 percent compared with the strongest competing memory framework, and the improvement holds across all question families. The biographies make an observation reachable through a different entity than the one the question superficially names, which is exactly the failure mode of timeline-only retrieval.

The ablations are the most useful part of the paper for anyone implementing this. Removing the relevance propagation along a biography costs three points. Removing the identity association entirely costs 3.4 points. Keeping the entity grouping but dropping the biography text, so the answer model sees only the index, costs 4.0 points. Keying identity by name rather than by visual grounding costs 2.8 points. Dropping the caption text costs 10.2 points, by far the largest single drop, which is a reminder that the memory system is only as good as the captioning underneath it. The paper also includes a controlled comparison: appending richer descriptions to the captions without the identity structure does not fully recover the gains, which isolates the identity-linking mechanism from mere verbosity.

Here is the skeptical read. The controller is a 35-billion-parameter open model, so the results are reproducible in principle, and the paper reports mean over three runs for the multiple-choice benchmarks, which is good hygiene. The Test@Day result, where the confidence interval includes zero, is the honest disclosure: the day-long gameplay gain is not statistically distinguishable from the baseline, and the week-long results are where the method earns its keep. The 10.2-point dependence on caption text is a real engineering constraint: if your captioner is weak, the biographies inherit the weakness, and the identity edges will link wrong observations together. The framework is also memory-construction-heavy, so the build cost scales with recording length, and the paper's evaluation of build cost versus retrieved duration shows GEB comparable to WorldMM with lower retrieved duration, which is the right direction but not a free lunch.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the decomposition: separating identity edges from timeline edges, and separating the index from the biography text, is a clean design that generalizes beyond video to any long-horizon agent memory with recurring entities. Before adopting, I would re-verify the EgoLifeQA number with the exact controller version, inspect the identity-edge precision on a small recording, because a biography that merges two distinct mugs is worse than no biography, and I would measure the memory-build wall time on a full day of egocentric footage. The gotcha to plan for is identity drift: the same object re-appearing after long gaps, and different objects sharing a description, are the two failure modes, and the ablations tell you which components catch which.

GEB is a clean, well-ablated contribution to long-video and long-horizon agent memory: identity as a first-class edge type, with the gains concentrated on week-scale recordings. The method is worth adopting for any agent that must track recurring entities across long contexts, with the caveat that caption quality sets the ceiling and day-scale gains are within noise on the current benchmarks.
