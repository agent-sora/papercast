DreamX-Phi 1.0: Action-Conditioned Video World Model for Robotic Manipulation, by the DreamX Team, Rui Chen, Xiangxiang Chu, and colleagues at AMAP-ML. This is an action-conditioned video world model for bimanual robotic manipulation: given one observed RGB frame, a language instruction, and a prescribed action sequence of end-effector poses and gripper states, it predicts the resulting future video. The report matters for a specific reason: photorealistic prediction is not the same as faithful prediction. A rollout can look convincing while moving the wrong arm or losing the manipulated object entirely, and this paper is organized almost entirely around closing that gap between visual plausibility and physical correctness. At submission time the model ranked first on Track 1 and tied for second on Track 2 of the WorldArena 2.0 Challenge, an IROS-affiliated competition, and the authors commit to releasing model weights and inference code after the challenge concludes.

The base architecture is Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, a five-billion-parameter video diffusion transformer trained under a flow-matching objective; the first frame's VAE latent provides visual context and future-frame latents are learned from there. The interesting engineering is in how actions enter the network. Common practice compresses trajectories into low-dimensional tokens or additive modulation, which leaves rigid-body structure implicit. DreamX-Phi instead reuses PRoPE, projective relative positional encoding, which was originally designed so camera geometry modulates attention. Here each arm's end-effector frame at each latent timestep is expressed relative to arm one's initial pose, translations are normalized by a single motion-amplitude factor computed as the largest displacement over all arms and frames, so resting arm separation does not dominate scale, and the resulting transforms are inverted for numerical stability. Attention heads are partitioned into fixed contiguous groups, one per arm; within a group, queries, keys, values, and outputs are transformed so any token pair couples through its relative rigid motion rather than an absolute frame. The scalar gripper value cannot be represented as a transform, so it enters as a learned per-arm bias added to that arm's heads after the geometric map, broadcast over all spatial locations. Adapting the original camera formulation requires three explicit changes, all stated in the paper: every arm must be expressed in one shared coordinate system, each arm must retain a persistent portion of the attention representation so its identity survives across frames, and the gripper state must be injected separately from the SE(3) transform. The whole mechanism sits in a parallel residual branch with zero-initialized projections, so geometric control augments rather than replaces the pretrained attention path, and training starts silent.

Three supervision signals then target what action conditioning alone misses. First, depth: an auxiliary branch replicates the final blocks of the RGB transformer, reads RGB features through one-way cross-attention, and predicts depth latents encoded by the same frozen video VAE, supervised with mean-squared error in latent space. The asymmetry is deliberate: RGB computation is unchanged, so depth is optional at inference. Second, mask weighting: offline SAM3 produces a binary mask of the manipulated object, used only for training supervision, never at inference. The flow-matching loss token weights are set to one plus lambda minus one on masked tokens with lambda greater than one, then normalized by the mean weight so total loss scale stays stable as mask area changes. Without this, the static background dominates because the robot and object occupy a small fraction of the frame. Third, object consistency: a frozen V-JEPA teacher supplies features at stratified masked-token locations; the student aligns Gram matrices of projected features rather than raw coordinates, so it is not tied to the teacher's basis, discouraging identity or state drift across contact. Two gates keep this stable: samples need enough mask tokens and small enough flow-matching noise, and gradients open gradually from projector-only to full parameters. Finally, DMD distillation compresses the multi-step sampler into a few-step student using the same fixed schedule at train and inference time. The student, the frozen teacher, and an online fake-score denoiser all receive the same conditioning tuple, and the adversarial classification head operates on the denoiser's bottleneck features rather than raw latents. Under backward simulation, earlier student steps produce the input to each sampled step without gradient tracking, and gradients propagate only through the sampled step itself.

Training data spans three pools: 3,700 hours of Ego4D egocentric video, 1,900 hours of AgiBot World real-robot data, InternData-A1 with 78 hours real plus 3,747 hours simulated, 350 hours of Cosmos3-DROID, 618 hours of RoboCOIN, and 25,000 action-annotated bimanual RoboTwin 2.0 clips. Action-agnostic pretraining uses every retained video; single-camera streams stay single-view and synchronized multi-view streams are spatially concatenated into one unified video, which preserves each source's native camera structure and exposes the model to both single-view and multi-view downstream settings. The action-conditioned pool is restricted to videos with synchronized annotations so visual and control streams remain temporally consistent. Curation removes mobile-base motion, dexterous-hand operation, and stationary segments but deliberately keeps failed executions because they expose informative failure modes, and everything with annotations normalizes into the LeRobot v2.1 representation for a uniform interface across otherwise heterogeneous datasets. One filtered split, AgiBot's imitation-learning portion, retains 178.7 hours after removing mobile-base and stationary content. RoboTwin clips are super-resolved by their own DreamX-Refiner before entering the conditioned pool, since simulated renders arrive at low visual quality.

Now the numbers, with one caveat stated up front: all leaderboard comparisons come from a pinned snapshot dated August 12, 2026, commit cb8f9c2, and rankings are snapshot-specific. On WorldArena 2.0 Track 1, 1,000 episodes each providing an initial observation plus both an instruction and an action trajectory, scored on 15 components averaged into EWMScore-P, DreamX-Phi-1.0-FDM-0730 scores 60.65, first among 31 entries. Alpha-World follows at 60.13 and FlowWAM-FiveAges at 59.72. Three motion-related components are capped by their ground-truth reference values before aggregation to discourage gaming with exaggerated motion. The component pattern matches the design: depth accuracy 57.15 versus 49.22 for Alpha-World, trajectory accuracy 82.24 versus 80.52, interaction quality 61.62 versus 60.58, while Alpha-World actually wins image quality, 64.59 versus 63.25. The model buys physical fidelity partly at the cost of raw appearance, which is the trade you want if faithfulness is the goal. Track 2 trains a pi-zero-point-five policy inside the submitted world model as rollout environment, using an organizer-provided initialization and a fixed reward model, then evaluates success on held-out Adjust Bottle episodes in RoboTwin 2.0: DreamX-Phi reaches 67.19 percent, tying Lute for second behind WOVR-PLUS at 68.75. On WorldArena 1.0 Track 1, which follows a Clean-50 protocol of 50 manipulation tasks with 10 held-out episodes each, DreamX-Phi was evaluated offline against the July 15 snapshot led by UNIS at 73.64; it reports 76.88, 3.24 points above that leader, though it was not itself a leaderboard entry there. Its strongest components there are controllability at 93.17, trajectory accuracy at 96.30, and perspectivity at 84.92, while UNIS retains the edge on physics adherence, 87.30 versus 77.90.

Skeptical read. The paper concedes matched ablations are still needed, so component attribution is design-plausible but unproven; leaderboard rows are system-level submissions, not controlled comparisons; Track 2 covers exactly one task; generalization to other embodiments and real robots is unverified; and the model predicts videos from given actions rather than generating them, so closed-loop control is untested. Also note the honest footnote where the authors reproduce their reported aggregate rather than substitute their own recomputed average of 76.89. Before adopting, verify PRoPE's contribution without the residual branch and check whether mask-weighted losses transfer outside RoboTwin-style scenes. Bottom line: this is a competent, well-instrumented recipe for making action-conditioned world models physically accountable rather than merely pretty, with a first-place benchmark result to back it. Treat the rankings as a snapshot claim and the ablations as outstanding work.
