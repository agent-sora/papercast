Late Entry in Looped Language Models, by Ziqi Jin, Jiaxi Li, and Weiwen Xu, at Nanyang Technological University with the Singapore University of Technology and Design and the Chinese University of Hong Kong, is a decoding paper for looped language models, the family of models that deepen reasoning by iterating a shared stack of layers T times per token instead of stacking more parameters. The problem it tackles is a direct consequence of that design: because each token costs T passes, recurrence depth turns directly into sequential decoding latency, and the standard speedup, early exit, has a hidden flaw. Early exit stops a token's computation after a few passes, but it does so before the key-value cache for that token is complete, so every later token attends to a truncated, shallow-depth cache of the earlier ones. The paper's proposal is the opposite strategy, late entry: a new token skips its first passes and enters directly into the final K passes, operating on a cache that is already at full depth. The headline: on Ouro-2.6B with T equal to 4, late entry with K equal to 2 reaches a 5.2 times speedup over greedy exact decoding while adding 26 percent on GSM8K over block decoding at K equal to 4, and on Huginn-3.5B the speedups scale with the recurrence depth, reaching 8.4 times greedy-exact and up to 12.5 times in lossy mode at T equal to 64.

The core question the paper asks is whether a deep cached context can substitute for the token-local passes that late entry skips. The naive answer is no, and the paper shows why. If you take an off-the-shelf looped model and just let new tokens enter at pass K, the agreement with full-depth decoding collapses, dropping to 0.552 on Ouro-1.4B and 0.751 on Ouro-2.6B, far below what the task needs. Inspecting the internal activations reveals the cause: the embeddings emitted by the entry boundary have a median L2 norm of 0.76, while the passes the token is jumping into expect activations with norms in the 37 to 51 range, a norm mismatch of roughly two orders of magnitude. The fix is a lightweight fine-tune of the entry boundary to align the hidden-state norms, and with that fix, agreement recovers to 0.816 and 0.919, recovering 60 to 70 percent of the gap, and the deep cached context then carries the rest. The paper measures this across tens of thousands of token positions, 78,693 on Ouro-1.4B and 70,101 on Ouro-2.6B, which is the kind of position-level analysis that makes the mechanism claim credible rather than a single-benchmark anecdote.

The engineering half is where the wall-clock speedups actually come from, and it is more subtle than just skipping passes. To get late entry into production latency without stalling the pipeline to build deep caches, the design runs candidate blocks through only the final K passes on the critical path, while previously committed tokens complete their remaining passes in parallel with subsequent steps at zero extra serial calls. Then it verifies those candidates with a Wake verification step that projects the finished T-pass wake states inside the existing output matrix multiply, governed by a logit-margin tolerance tau. That single tolerance unifies three regimes: tau equal to 0 gives greedy-exact T-pass decoding, matching K equals T block decoding within floating-point reduction order; tau greater than 0 gives margin-gated verification, where you accept candidates whose logits are safely ahead; and tau equal to infinity gives non-rollback lossy late entry, where you accept whatever the shallow pass produced. The whole thing fits in one static CUDA graph, which matters because a dynamic control flow would erode the launch efficiency that makes small-model decoding fast in the first place.

The scaling result is the interesting one for people tracking the looped-model line. As the recurrence depth grows from 8 to 64 on Huginn-3.5B, the rollback rate for K equals T over 4 drops by a factor of 21.5, which means deep recurrence makes late entry cheaper to verify, not more expensive. The greedy-exact speedup climbs to 6.1 times at T equal to 32 and 8.4 times at T equal to 64, the last with a 153 percent gain over K equals 64 block decoding, and the lossy mode reaches 10.6 to 12.5 times. The paper also models throughput across batch sizes from 1 to 32 with an 8.3 percent mean absolute percentage error, which is the systems-level claim that the speedups survive realistic serving conditions rather than only appearing at batch one.

The skeptical reading. First, this is a decoding method for a specific model family, the Ouro and Huginn looped models, and the norm-mismatch fine-tune is per-model, so adopting late entry for a new looped architecture means re-running the entry-boundary calibration, which is cheap but is a step, and a calibration that drifts as the base model is updated would need to be redone on every refresh. Second, the lossy mode, tau equal to infinity, is where the biggest speedups live, and lossy means the output is not guaranteed to match exact decoding, so the 12.5 times number is a quality-speed tradeoff, not a free lunch, and the GSM8K and MBPP deltas in lossy mode are the numbers to check before trusting it on your task. Third, the static CUDA graph assumption is hardware-specific, and the graph is built for a fixed T and K, so a serving stack that changes the recurrence budget at runtime would need to recompile, which is a real operational cost that a multi-model inference service would feel. Fourth, the comparison against early exit is the natural one, and it is decisive, but the paper frames late entry as the better of two shallow strategies, and the absolute quality of late entry relative to full-depth decoding in the margin-gated regime is what ultimately bounds its usefulness on hard reasoning tasks.

For implementation: if you are serving a looped language model and your latency is dominated by the T passes per token, the recipe is (1) fine-tune the entry boundary to align hidden-state norms so late entry agrees with full depth, (2) verify candidates through only the final K passes on the critical path with committed tokens completing in parallel, and (3) set the logit-margin tolerance tau to taste, zero for exactness, infinity for maximum speed. The evaluation stack is agreement with full-depth decoding measured per token position, task accuracy on GSM8K and MBPP at matched speed, and throughput across batch sizes. The cheapest validation is the position-agreement measurement on your own looped model, because if your norm mismatch is as large as the paper's, the fine-tune is mandatory and the speedups are real. The one design decision worth making early is whether you need the exact or lossy regime, because the static graph is built for a fixed T and K and a serving stack that wants to switch between exact and lossy at runtime will pay the recompile cost, so pick your latency-quality operating point per model rather than trying to serve both from one compiled graph.

To summarize the bottom line. Late Entry is a clever, well-analyzed inversion of early exit for looped language models, grounded in a position-level diagnosis of the norm mismatch that breaks naive late entry and a systems design that turns the fix into up to 12.5 times speedups that scale with recurrence depth. Its limits are model-family specificity, a per-model calibration step, and the fact that the top speedups are lossy. If you are on the looped-model path for cheap deep reasoning, this is the paper that changes what is affordable at inference time, and the rollback-scaling result is the one to watch as recurrence depths keep growing.
