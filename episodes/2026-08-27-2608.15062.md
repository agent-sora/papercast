Gated Recurrent Transformers: Expressive Depth through Recurrent Modulation, by Amr Hegazy of the German University in Cairo, Amr Alanwar of the Technical University of Munich, and Mostafa Elhoushi of Cerebras Systems. The paper starts from a tension every large-model engineer knows: depth and parameters are rigidly coupled in a standard transformer, because every layer carries its own weights, so the memory bill grows exactly as fast as the expressivity. Weight tying breaks that coupling, but naive tying forces an identical transformation onto what are radically different representational stages, and the representation after the first recurrence is nothing like the one after the eighth, so a static shared layer collapses the very functional diversity that makes depth valuable. The paper grounds this in a framing worth repeating: a finite set of states applied iteratively is enough to compute anything computable, so the power of a reasoning system may lie in the depth of iteration rather than the breadth of parameters, and recurrent depth is the hidden-state analogue of test-time chain-of-thought, thinking longer within the residual stream instead of spending tokens. The paper's answer to the collapse problem is borrowed from an old idea: treat the hidden state across recurrences the way a GRU treats its state across time, and let a learned gate decide, element by element, how much of the shared block's output to absorb at each pass. The same weights become fixed in parameters but dynamic in behavior.

The architecture follows the now-standard prelude-shared-coda layout: a few fixed blocks encode the input once, a small shared core is iterated many times, and a few fixed blocks project to output. The machinery lives between recurrences. The current hidden state, concatenated with the fixed prelude output, which acts as a constant anchor to the original input, is projected, passed through the shared blocks, and then blended back through an elementwise gate computed by a small two-layer network conditioned on both the state and the prelude view, with noise injected into both the state and the gate during training to keep the system from collapsing into brittle patterns. The gate bias is initialized to plus four, so at the start of training the gate sits near zero point nine eight and the residual stream passes through the recurrence almost untouched; the model then learns which elements to overwrite as training proceeds, which is the classic recurrent-network initialization trick, the forget-gate bias, transplanted from time to depth. During training, the recurrence count itself is sampled uniformly, which trains every exit point simultaneously and doubles as stochastic-depth regularization.

Two evaluation regimes, and the paper is strongest in the first. Under isoFLOPS, matching the dense model's compute per forward pass while storing far fewer parameters, a three-unique-layer recurrent model at one point eight four gigaflops per forward pass stores about thirty-five million parameters against the twelve-layer GPT-2 small baseline's one hundred twenty-four million, and it matches the dense baseline's loss, three point one four against three point one five, with three seeds showing the recurrent model's worst seed beating the baseline's best. At medium and large scale the dense baseline still leads at the standard nine-point-eight-billion-token budget, by zero point zero five and zero point zero six nats, though the paper shows both gaps closing as the token budget doubles at medium scale. Against the four recurrent competitors the picture is a clean sweep: it leads Mixture-of-Recursions and heavy-tail depth sampling in all nine scale-by-budget cells, and the strongest competitor, Relaxed Recursive Transformers, which buys diversity with per-recurrence LoRA adapters, reaches parity only at small scale. The paper's diagnosis of that gap is the interesting part: LoRA deltas are fixed at training time and applied identically to every input, so the diversity they provide is static, while the gate conditions on the state it is about to update, so successive states stay separable. Downstream at large scale, the isoFLOPS variant matches the dense baseline's nine-benchmark average, forty-two point zero eight versus forty-two point zero five, using only thirty-seven percent of the parameters. Under isoPARAMS, matched parameters with more compute per pass, deeper recurrence helps everywhere, improving validation loss at medium and large scale, and the isoPARAMS variant beats the dense baseline on eight of nine benchmarks, average forty-four point one five against forty-two point zero five, at the cost of up to five times the inference FLOPs, which is the standard trade in this literature.

The practical results are the early exit and the memory accounting. Because every recurrence depth is trained, one checkpoint becomes a continuous compute-quality dial: cut the recurrences in half and the model retains about ninety-two percent of its accuracy without any auxiliary exit losses, and at matched inference compute the recurrent early exit beats a dense model truncated at the equivalent layer. Qualitatively, the paper shows next-token distributions sharpening across recurrences: knowledge-heavy prompts stabilize in one or two steps, arithmetic and reasoning prompts show progressive error correction across more steps, and open-ended prompts keep refining, suggesting the model allocates its own thinking depth by task. On deployment, the honest numbers: measured latency under torch.compile is ten percent higher than the dense baseline at large scale, eleven percent at medium, with about half the gap attributable to kernel-launch overhead of the extra elementwise operations rather than arithmetic, in exchange for sixty-two percent fewer parameters and fifty-nine percent less peak memory. The recurrent KV cache is the hidden cost, since naive decoding wants one cache per recurrence step, but averaging the key and value across steps cuts end-to-end decoding memory to zero point three nine times dense while actually improving HellaSwag slightly, apparently acting as a regularizer.

The skeptical read. First, scale: everything is GPT-2-class, seven hundred seventy-four million parameters at the top and under ten billion training tokens, roughly two orders of magnitude below frontier runs, and the known history here is sobering, since the original scaling-law paper found recurrent models lose at matched compute; this paper's matching is more careful, but the result still needs a scaling ladder to be believed. Second, the isoFLOPS comparison matches forward-pass FLOPs, while the recurrent model pays an extra projection and gate per pass, ten-d-squared per step, which is included in the FLOPs accounting but complicates the claim that compute is truly equal. Third, the headline sixty-two percent parameter saving buys matching, not beating, the dense baseline at standard budget, and the win appears only when the budget doubles, so the fair summary is memory efficiency at equal quality, with quality gains confined to the matched-parameter regime. Fourth, gate and state noise magnitudes, temperature, and the bias initialization sweep are tuned on the same evaluation being reported, and the bias sweep's spread, under two hundredths of a nat, is small enough that the initialization story is less load-bearing than the introduction suggests. Fifth, the KV-cache averaging result, better accuracy with less memory, is suspicious enough that it wants its own ablation before anyone ships it. Sixth, the evaluation is zero-shot and one-shot on nine standard harness benchmarks, with no long-context or in-context-learning probes where recurrent depth might differentiate most, and the ablations, while covering gate-bias, noise, and depth-sampling schedules, stay within one architecture family on one dataset, so generality is asserted rather than measured. What I would trust: the core mechanism, gating across depth with a prelude anchor is a clean, well-motivated fix to the collapse problem, and the emergent early exit, which falls out of depth sampling for free. What I would re-verify: the scaling ladder to billions of parameters, wall-clock at serving batch sizes, and whether the gate's per-element specialization is interpretable or just noise-shaped regularization. Bottom line: the paper argues that the recurrent-depth literature's real obstacle is not weight sharing itself but the absence of input-conditioned modulation, and shows that a GRU-style gate over depth closes the gap to dense models at a third of the parameters while making inference depth a runtime dial. Looping is becoming a family of concrete engineering options, gate it, route it, relax it, or scale it, and this is the clearest statement yet of the gating branch.
