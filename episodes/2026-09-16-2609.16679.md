Paper three is AI for Games in the Foundation Model Era, from Meng Luo, Yanlin Li, and Hao Li, a survey from the National University of Singapore with Ran Zhang at Nanyang Technological University and contact authors at NUS. This is a 120-page synthesis of how foundation models are being used across the entire game lifecycle, and it is organized in a way that the authors claim distinguishes it from earlier game AI surveys.

The organizing idea is six roles, defined by the immediate use of the AI output. Role one, AI that plays and acts: agents that select actions under game rules, from classical search and reinforcement learning up to generalist models, with control hierarchies, test-time adaptation, and opponents and non-player characters. Role two, AI that models players and games: learned world models, planning models, interactive simulators, and behavioral inference about players. Role three, AI that designs games: assets, levels, worlds, rules and mechanics, and narrative. Role four, AI that builds and maintains games: code, scenes, engine projects, tool-using development agents, and debugging and repair. Role five, AI that generates and adapts at runtime: live content, dynamic rules, and cross-session memory. And role six, AI that tests and evaluates games: automated playtesting, mechanic verification, model-based judges, and the question of whether simulated players are representative of human ones.

The opening of the survey anchors the breadth in a concrete example. Recent uses of a model the authors call GPT-6 Astra place the same pretrained model in different roles across the lifecycle: ARC-AGI-3 evaluates it in unfamiliar interactive environments where it has to discover how the game works and then act, and a company called Playco reports using the same model inside an engine-connected development tool to create playable game prototypes. One model, several roles, different evidence for each. That framing is the survey's thesis, and the rest of the paper fills in each role in detail.

For each role the survey asks a consistent set of questions: what structure does the game or workflow supply, what does the AI actually learn or generate, which capabilities transfer across settings, and what evidence supports the claims. That last question is where the survey is most useful. The authors are explicit that evaluation is most standardized and most grounded in execution for bounded game playing and a few learned environments, and much less established everywhere else. Persistent state in learned worlds, repeated software revision, validated player modeling, sustained runtime adaptation, and representative automated testing are all flagged as weakly evidenced. Their concrete examples make the points: GameNGen learns an interactive simulator of a platformer from gameplay trajectories, and learned environments can supply imagined experience for policy learning, in the Dreamer tradition. DreamGarden turns a natural-language design intention into a hierarchical plan that designers inspect while specialist modules generate assets and code. Play2Code connects a coding agent to a browser-based playtester so the system can watch its own generated game run. And IF:CARGO has players express rules in natural language, which a language model translates into constrained commands that the engine validates.

The playing-and-acting section is the deepest of the six, because it has the longest history. It traces the line from Shannon's chess program through reinforcement learning and self-play to the current generalist agents, and then splits the recent work into player-specific and generalist agents, learning and control hierarchies, test-time adaptation and memory, and the social roles of opponents, teammates, non-player characters, and companions. The modeling-players-and-games section is where the world-model literature lands: planning models, training environments, and interactive simulators, followed by the harder problems of representation, uncertainty, and persistent state, then player models and behavioral inference, and finally the real-time and long-horizon-consistency problems of learned action interfaces. The design sections cover assets and levels, rules and mechanics, and narrative and co-creative design, with a consistent note that designer intent, diversity, and controllability are the open problems. The build-and-maintain sections follow the software-engineering story into games: code, scenes, and engine projects, tool-using development agents, execution and debugging, and the under-studied problem of revision, maintenance, and handoff between generated and human-authored code.

The cross-role connections are the part that justifies calling this a synthesis rather than six separate reviews. Trajectories from playing can train world models. Learned environments can provide training experience for agents. Design specifications can drive executable implementations. And feedback from play or testing can guide revision. But the authors are careful that these connections do not make the capabilities transferable for free. Control schemes, rules, engine interfaces, state representations, and player contexts often remain setting-specific, so a capability demonstrated in one game or engine has to be re-validated in its target setting before you can claim it carries over. That is the central challenge the survey isolates: enabling outputs and capabilities to be reused or transferred across roles while re-establishing the evidence for their effectiveness in the specific structures, interfaces, and player contexts where they are actually used.

There is a benchmark and evidence section that maps each role's evaluation targets and metrics, and a discussion of the limits of interpretation. The honest takeaway is that the field is broad but uneven. The roles where execution-grounded evaluation exists, bounded game playing and a handful of learned simulators, have real evidence. The roles that generate, adapt, build, and test across open engines and real players have far less. The survey's value is that it names that unevenness role by role instead of letting the impressive demos stand in for general capability.

The testing-and-evaluation role deserves its own mention because it is where the survey is most critical of the field. Automated playtesting with simulated players exposes different aspects of the same game depending on play style, which is genuinely useful, but the survey presses on two follow-ups that most playtesting papers skip. First, model-based judges: a foundation model that scores game content is itself a system whose outputs need validation, and the survey treats model-based judgment as a distinct target with its own reliability questions rather than assuming the judge is trustworthy. Second, player representativeness: simulated players are only as good as the behavioral model behind them, and the survey is explicit that validated player modeling is one of the least established capabilities across all six roles. The benchmarks section then maps, role by role, which evaluation targets exist, which metrics are standard, and where the interpretation breaks down. The honest summary is that execution-grounded evaluation is concentrated in a few settings, and most of the lifecycle is evaluated by judgment rather than by a held-out test.

My assessment: if you work on game AI or on foundation models applied to interactive domains, this is the map to keep. The six-role taxonomy is clean and the consistent question set, structure supplied versus learned, transferability, and evidence, is a better organizing principle than the usual capability-based buckets. I would use it to audit a specific system's claims: which role does it occupy, what structure is it leaning on, and is the evidence execution-grounded or demo-based? Where I would push back is that the survey is necessarily a snapshot of a fast-moving area, and the transferability caveats, while correct, are also a little unfalsifiable, because the burden always lands on re-validation in the target setting. The open-challenges section is the right list, but it is also a reminder that the hard part, cross-role transfer with re-established evidence, is mostly unsolved.

The bottom line: a thorough, well-organized survey that maps foundation-model use across the game lifecycle and is unusually honest about where the evidence is and where it is not. It does not advance any capability itself; it tells you where the field's claims are and where they are thin, and the cross-role transfer question it leaves open is the one most likely to decide which of the six roles becomes load-bearing as foundation models get better.
