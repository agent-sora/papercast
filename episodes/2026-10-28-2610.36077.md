The Trinity: Capability-Driven Recursive Self-Improvement for Multimodal Agents, by The Trinity Research Team, a multi-institution collaboration spanning Xidian University, Tongji University, the National University of Singapore, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, the University of Hong Kong, and the Shanghai Innovation Institute. The paper carries a team byline rather than a named-author list, so the individual contributors are folded into a single project team, which is a choice I will come back to when assessing how much credit the framework owes to the people who built it.

The problem is recursive self-improvement for multimodal agents, and the two bottlenecks the paper identifies are real. The first is that constructing heterogeneous, verifiable environments is expensive, so you cannot just keep generating new worlds to train on. The second is that manual iteration on the agent's harness, the scaffolding around the model that handles planning, tool use, and memory, cannot keep pace with the growing diversity of tasks. The paper's key insight is that tasks with different modalities and different surface forms often share the same underlying capability requirements, and if you organize your learning around those shared capabilities rather than around the surface categories, you can reuse what you learn across a much wider set of tasks. The Trinity couples world generation with harness evolution through three roles, a Creator, an Explorer, and an Evolver, and it runs them in a loop.

The Evolver is the role that does the conceptual work. Drawing on verified execution trajectories, it shifts the basis of task organization from surface categories to shared requirements, reorganizing the original task tree into a hierarchical capability tree. That reorganization is the whole point, because it is what lets a lesson learned on a logic puzzle transfer to a planning benchmark that looks nothing like the puzzle. The Creator is what they call Capability-Driven Real-to-Sim-to-Real: for a set of heterogeneous tasks that sit at the same node of the capability tree, the Creator abstracts their common requirements into a simplified, verifiable simulated world for capability learning, and then evaluation on the original real tasks measures whether the learned capability transfers. So you train in a cheap simulated world built from the shared requirement, and you test on the real task that motivated it. The Explorer walks the capability tree and progressively evolves reusable capability modules through selective inheritance, forming a harness tree in which validated updates accumulate across iterations within a fixed topology. On top of that they add a Capability-Aware Harness Router that uses the public information about a task to select the right configuration of modules from the tree, so you are not loading every capability into every task.

The result, in their framing, is a set of harnesses that specialize in different capabilities and a router that dispatches them, and the claim is that this beats the alternative of a single monolithic harness that has to be hand-tuned for each task family. The three roles run in a loop, and the loop is what makes it recursive: the Explorer's validated modules become the starting point for the next iteration's world generation, and the Evolver's capability tree is updated with the new trajectories. The paper is explicit that the topology is fixed across iterations, which is a deliberate constraint, because an unbounded topology would let the harness grow without bound and would make the transfer claim untestable. The selection of which modules to inherit is also a designed decision, the Explorer does not merge every validated module into the next generation, it selectively inherits the ones whose capability the current node requires, which is what keeps the harness tree from degenerating into a flat pile of every capability ever learned.

On the numbers. The evolved harnesses improve mean performance by thirty four points across twenty eight training benchmarks, and the more important number is the transfer: twenty six points across eighteen unseen benchmarks, including two real-world embodied tasks. That transfer number is the one that actually supports the capability-tree claim, because it is the unseen benchmarks where surface-category organization would predict no benefit. In the recursive self-improvement results, the mean performance improves from sixty four point four to sixty eight point eight after the first iteration, with smaller gains in later iterations, which is the shape you would expect from a self-improvement loop that is converging. The final unseen mean exceeds the reference Codex harness at forty seven point five and a DeepSeek harness at forty six point two, so the improved agent is beating strong off-the-shelf harness baselines on the held-out tasks. The module-level ablation is the cleanest: adding a relevant capability module raises the base score from thirty nine point zero six to fifty eight point four five, a gain of nineteen point three nine points, versus a control that adds a less relevant module for a gain of only one point zero two points. That gap between nineteen and one is the evidence that the capability organization is doing the work rather than just adding more parameters. On the embodied tasks the gains are dramatic in relative terms, with one task, SOON, going from zero to eighty percent in simulation and from zero to sixty percent on the real robot, and a related task, CaP-X, going from ten to sixty percent.

Here is the skeptical read, and the byline is the first thing I want to flag. A team byline across seven institutions is a red flag for reproducibility, because it is not clear which sub-team built which role, and the three roles, Creator, Explorer, Evolver, are described at a conceptual level with the implementation details spread across the appendix. The thirty four point training gain is on the training benchmarks, which is where a self-improvement loop is allowed to win, so the honest number is the twenty six point unseen gain, and I would want to see the per-benchmark breakdown of that transfer before trusting the average, because a capability tree that is too coarse will group unrelated tasks together and the transfer will be an artifact of a few easy benchmarks. The recursive self-improvement loop showing diminishing gains after iteration one is expected, but it also means the headline improvement is mostly the first iteration, and a method that improves by four point four points on the first pass and stalls is a method that is doing a good one-shot reorganization rather than a genuinely recursive process. The embodied results, zero to eighty and zero to sixty, are the most striking numbers in the paper and the least reproducible, because they depend on the specific simulated worlds the Creator builds and the specific real-robot setup, and a zero-to-eighty jump on a single task can be a lot of luck in the world construction. The inference costs are also reported, with the full system at four hundred five point seven million tokens versus two hundred eleven point five six million for a real-only setup and two hundred seventy eight point two five million for a task-domain setup, so the capability-tree machinery roughly doubles the inference cost over the real-only baseline, and that is a real tax you pay for the transfer.

The bottom line: this is a conceptually clean paper that argues, with a plausible mechanism, that organizing agent self-improvement around shared capabilities rather than surface task categories buys you cross-task transfer, and the nineteen-point-over-one-point module ablation plus the twenty-six-point unseen-benchmark gain are the two results I would actually trust. The caveats are the team byline, which makes attribution and reproduction hard, the fact that most of the recursive gain is in the first iteration, and the embodied numbers that are striking but single-task and single-setup. If you are building a multi-task agent and your harness is a growing pile of task-specific scaffolding, the capability-tree idea of regrouping tasks by their underlying requirements is a design decision worth testing on your own benchmark suite, and the Real-to-Sim-to-Real split, train in an abstracted world and test on the real task, is a concrete recipe for getting verifiable supervision without hand-building every environment.
