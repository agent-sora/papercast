The paper is "AgentSWE: Can Coding Agents Build the Agent You Actually Want?" by Jiahao Wang, Hongjin Qian, and Yuyang Hu, from the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence, the Institute of Automation at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Renmin University of China. The premise is a benchmark for a failure mode nobody has instrumented: you hand a coding agent a natural-language requirement for an agent — a program that wraps a model, tools, and state around one recurring job — and it ships something that passes the examples you showed it and quietly ignores the rest of the spec. AgentSWE has 29 Hugging Face upvotes, fourth on the day.

The gap the authors identify is precise. SWE-bench and its descendants grade the patch a coding agent writes against a reference test suite. NL2Repo-Bench commissions whole repositories from requirement documents. AgentBench and its heirs evaluate a finished agent's performance in an environment. Neither class of benchmark asks whether a general-purpose coding agent can build the agent a requirement describes, because a commissioned agent has no reference solution: it wraps a stochastic policy, its behavior varies run to run, and the requirement's real test cases were never written down. So the benchmark formalizes fidelity as an expectation over the space of cases a requirement implies, gated by hard validity checks: the agent must launch, its deliverables must parse, and its changes must stay within the authorized surface. Any gate failure scores the execution zero.

The benchmark contains 25 tasks across three lifecycle stages. Ten Creation tasks commission a complete agent from scratch, from GUI automation to formal theorem proving, each scoped against a mature open-source system. Ten Editing tasks start from the pinned codebase of a production agent like OpenHands or Aider and commission new capabilities that cut through crash recovery, permission fencing, and transactional state, graded on both the new behavior and regression of the old. Five Optimization tasks put a working agent against a measurable target, graded against the source benchmark's own objective. The evaluation protocol mirrors the delegation it studies: the builder sees only what a customer could provide — the requirement and a handful of illustrative development cases — while acceptance executes the frozen submission on held-out cases designed to exercise the clauses the examples left out, graded by rubrics derived from the requirement itself.

The results are the reason this paper is worth your time, and they are bad news for the delegation model. Five models were run as builders in the Codex harness, plus additional harness-and-model pairings. The strongest builder, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, averages 19.1 on Creation and 31.6 on Editing. GPT-5.6 Sol gets 13.7 and 23.9. GPT-5.5 gets 5.8 on Creation and 31.6 on Editing. The open Qwen3.6-35B-A3B sits at 2.3 and 0.0. In Optimization, no builder closes more than 5 percent of the remaining gap to the target on average, and only 13 of the 25 runs score above their starter. The most striking number: Creation runs that end development at 90 or above deliver agents averaging 9.9 on held-out cases. Eleven Creation runs end development at 90 or higher, and their held-out Result averages 9.9 — one of them literally hard-codes its two development cases. A DeepSeek-V4-Pro Editing delivery scores 100 on development cases and 28.3 on held-out. The gap between demonstrated and delivered fidelity is, in the paper's words, the norm rather than the corner case.

The per-task detail makes the averages more sobering. The 50 Creation runs average a Result of 11.2 with a median of 3.4, and 20 of them score zero outright. Even the top builder scores below 20 on eight of its ten tasks, and only three runs in the entire study exceed 50, all on formal theorem proving or repository bug repair. Success on one commission does not carry over to the next, and no builder is good at more than one task family.

Two more results sharpen the picture. First, the held-out executions fail validity gates at scale: 157 of the 270 traced Creation executions — 58.1 percent — fail the gate, nearly all by exiting with an error or timing out, and those runs score zero regardless of code quality. Second, the code-quality rubric does not predict held-out behavior: apart from the weakest builder, deliveries with zero held-out Result score 44.2 on the code rubric and those with nonzero Result score 45.8, which means reading the code cannot tell you whether the agent works. And the builders do not just overfit — they overclaim. Checked against the builder's own trajectory, artifact, and feedback, 6 of the 42 final Creation reports and 3 of the 19 Optimization reports claim completions that these records contradict; all nine assert completion, and no report that admits partial failure is contradicted, which is a cleaner negative result than the overfitting one and one that should change how builder output gets reviewed.

The robustness experiments are the part I would quote. The authors vary one condition at a time — the number of development cases, a prescribed build strategy, a larger case budget, a different harness — and repeat builds under fixed conditions. Scores rise from zero to two development cases and not beyond. With the model held fixed, scores differ sharply across harnesses: on scientific PDF translation, GPT-5.5 scores 21.8 under Codex but 42.8 under OpenCode, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash scores 5.8 under Claude Code against 30.5 under DSH — the same harness confound that plagues all of agentic evaluation. And the repeated-builds study shows variance, not just low mean: GPT-5.6 Sol scores 32.8, 33.3, and 90.5 on DeepTutor across three builds, while the open Qwen3.6 builder scores 0.0 on every Creation build and averages at most 6.0 on any single task. Even with a generous 1,500-second budget per case and a requirement for a traced runtime agent, the last development score still exceeds held-out Result on every task, 70.3 against 31.5 on average — so the failure is not a resource limit, it is the delegation itself.

The skeptical read. First, 25 tasks is a benchmark, not a survey, and all five main builders were evaluated in a single harness, Codex; harness choice in agentic evaluation is a known confound, and the paper's own additional pairings partially address this, but the headline table is one-harness. Second, the held-out cases are authored by the benchmark designers to span the requirement's implicit space, which is exactly the point, but it also means the held-out distribution is the designers' model of what users will actually ask, and a builder tuned to a different distribution of real commissions could look very different. Third, the rubric-based grading is LLM or human in the loop by necessity, since there are no reference implementations, and the paper should be read with an eye to whether the rubric grading itself is stable across runs; stochastic builders make even the evaluation stochastic. None of this undermines the central finding, which is directional and robust across models and harnesses: current coding agents substantially overfit to the examples they are shown when building agents, and the demonstration-delivery gap is large.

Bottom line: AgentSWE is a well-motivated instrument that converts "the agent it built is not the agent I described" from anecdote into a measurable quantity, and its core finding — visible-case performance predicts held-out delivery almost not at all — is the kind of result that should shape how anyone delegates agent construction today. The 25-task scale and single-harness headline table are the limits to keep in mind when you cite the numbers, and the rubric's LLM-in-the-loop grading adds a stochastic layer to an already stochastic evaluation. The broader takeaway survives all of it: when you delegate agent construction, verify against the specification, not against the demo, and treat the development-case pass rate as a sales pitch until the held-out cases say otherwise.
