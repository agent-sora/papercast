On the Generalization Limitation of On-Policy Distillation for Post-RL Consolidation, from the University of Chicago, Duke University, the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Northwestern University, and ByteDance. The first three authors are Yichen Wang, Yufa Zhou, and Siqi Zhu. The result is a diagnosis of a blind spot in a stage of the post-training pipeline that a lot of frontier labs are now running, and the diagnosis is uncomfortable. It says that the near-perfect in-distribution distillation scores that make multi-teacher on-policy distillation look like a free consolidation win are hiding a real out-of-distribution loss.

To set the terms. On-policy distillation, which the paper abbreviates OPD, trains a student model on trajectories the student itself generates, using the teacher's next-token distribution as dense token-level supervision. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation, MOPD, extends that to a mixture, assigning different teachers to different domains so you can fold several separately trained specialist models into one. The motivation for MOPD is consolidation. You train a math specialist, a coding specialist, an agent specialist, a long-context specialist, each from a common base, and then you want one model that has all of them. Model merging does this in parameter space, mixed reinforcement learning does it by jointly optimizing one model on a shuffled mix of tasks with their rewards, and MOPD does it by distilling from the specialists. This paper argues that all three of these consolidation stages inherit the same failure, but it is cleanest to see in distillation, so that is where the evidence is.

The claim, stated plainly. In-distribution distillation, where you evaluate the student on the exact task distributions the teachers were supervised on, reaches about ninety-six to one hundred and three percent of the teacher's score. That is the number every prior paper celebrates. Out of distribution, though, the student sits two point four five to sixteen point two five percent below the base model it was initialized from. So the consolidation step that is supposed to preserve the base model's general capability while adding the teachers' improvements is actually eroding the base model's out-of-distribution performance. Near-perfect distillation, in other words, and measurable forgetting of exactly the capability you most need beyond the supervised tasks.

The experiments use Qwen3-8B as the student base. There are two teacher configurations. Self-RL specialist teachers, trained independently from the same Qwen3-8B base on separate task distributions with group relative policy optimization, and then distilled into a student from that same base. That reflects the specialize-then-consolidate pipeline. And external generalist teachers, where a larger generalist, specifically Qwen3-32B, is distilled into the 8B student. That configuration matters because it rules out one easy explanation. If the student forgets out-of-distribution, you could argue that is because the specialist teachers themselves forget, and the student is just inheriting that. The external generalist setup closes that off, because Qwen3-32B outperforms the 8B base on the out-of-distribution evaluations. The teacher has the capability available to transfer. It is not there.

The domains are mathematical reasoning, coding, agent and tool use, and long context. For extrapolative generalization they train on things like GSM8K, MATH, and DAPO for math, Nemotron competitive coding data for code, a mix of BFCL, xLAM, and ToolACE data for agents, and DocQA for long context. Then they probe extrapolation by shifting to harder benchmarks in the same domain, the AIME 24 and 25 problems being the headline case. For compositional generalization they train on separate component capabilities and test whether the model can use them together, specifically long-context math, long-context coding, and agentic coding on SWE-bench Verified.

The numbers, for the self-RL specialist setup. Single-teacher OPD reaches about ninety-seven point one percent of the teacher's in-distribution score on average across the three math training datasets, which is essentially perfect. Its out-of-distribution score, though, is ten point seven eight percent, eight point five six percentage points, below the base. And the specialist teachers themselves forget out of distribution, so the student is at least forgetting less than the teacher. OPD reduces the average out-of-distribution forgetting by about twenty-five percent relative to the self-RL teachers. The mean loss from the base falls from eleven point four three to eight point five six percentage points. But it is still below the base. In the multi-teacher mixtures, MOPD's average out-of-distribution score stays fourteen point zero seven percent, eleven point one eight points, below the base across all the pairwise and three-teacher mixtures. It does reduce forgetting relative to the mixed-RL baseline by twenty-nine percent, but again, below the base.

The external generalist result is the cleanest counterexample to the teacher-forgetting explanation. Distilling Qwen3-32B into Qwen3-8B, OPD reaches ninety-nine point three four percent of the teacher's in-distribution score, less than a single point below it. On the out-of-distribution evaluations it is still three point seven three percent, two point nine six points, below the base. A strictly stronger teacher, with the out-of-distribution capability demonstrably present in its own weights, does not fix the transfer. The bottleneck is not teacher strength.

The compositional results make a different but related point. Component improvements do not compose. Across the three composition settings, MOPD improves the in-distribution component scores, by as much as twenty-two point four six percent over the base in the best case, but on the composed task it is still eighteen point one nine to twenty-four point three four percent below the best teacher. The code-plus-long-context case is the sharpest. MOPD improves all three component benchmarks, and yet LongBench-v2 Code, the composed long-context coding task, stays flat at the base's forty-four point zero, while both teachers are above it at fifty and fifty-eight. So the student learned coding and learned long context separately and measurably better, and still cannot do long-context coding. Improvements on separately supervised capabilities do not transfer to the task that requires them jointly.

Why. The paper examines two candidate bottlenecks and finds both matter. Teacher capability, whether the out-of-distribution behavior is even present in the teacher to begin with, and data coverage, how narrow the distillation distribution is. Relaxing teacher capability alone, giving the strong Qwen3-32B teacher, does not eliminate the gap. Relaxing both, keeping Qwen3-32B as the teacher but broadening the distillation data to ninety percent GSM8K plus ten percent OpenR1, does. In that configuration the student exceeds the base on all five math benchmarks, matches the teacher's AIME 24 and 25 pass@8 at eighty-six point six seven and eighty-three point three three, and actually beats the teacher on AMC23 at a hundred against the teacher's ninety-five. The paper draws this as a flask analogy, the neck width being data coverage, and the point is that you need both a wider-necked vessel and a vessel that actually contains the liquid.

The geometry analysis is the mechanism. Comparing the parameter update of each model relative to the common base, MOPD produces smaller and more spectrally concentrated updates than the mixed-RL baseline. The absolute update norm is about zero point one five four for OPD against zero point two seven eight for RL, and the top sixty-four singular directions carry about sixty-nine percent of the energy for OPD against about forty percent for RL. So the distillation update is more compact, more concentrated in a small number of directions. That is consistent with less forgetting, which is why MOPD beats mixed RL on the average out-of-distribution loss. And the MOPD update is better explained by the span of the single-teacher OPD updates than the mixed-RL update is by the span of the single-RL updates, meaning the multi-teacher distillation update has more shared structure across tasks.

The proposed fixes are modest. Geometry-based patching after distillation, which partially closes the out-of-distribution gap on AIME 25 while keeping most of the teacher's in-distribution performance. And base-preserving replay, which mixes in base-model behavior during distillation. Relative to plain OPD, replay improves scores on five of the eight out-of-distribution benchmarks with only a small in-distribution decrease, and it surpasses both the student base and the RL teacher on AIME 25.

A skeptical read. This is a well-controlled diagnosis and the two-bottleneck finding, teacher capability and data coverage, is the durable contribution. The flask framing is a bit soft, but the controlled ablations behind it are honest, including the external-teacher result that removes the obvious confound. What I would push on before adopting any of this in a pipeline is the operational in-distribution versus out-of-distribution grouping. The paper is explicit that its cross-benchmark labels are defined by a threshold on how well the teachers beat the base, so whether a given benchmark counts as in-distribution is a modeling choice, and the thresholds they report are stable at ninety-five, ninety-six, and ninety-eight percent, which is reassuring, but it is still an assumption doing real work. The out-of-distribution set is dominated by hard math problems, and I would want to see the same story hold on the coding and agent out-of-distribution splits with the same rigor, because the headline ranges are math-heavy. The compositional gap is the finding I would trust most and reuse most, because component improvement without compositional transfer is a concrete, testable property you can measure in your own consolidation runs. If you run a post-RL consolidation stage and only check the in-distribution teacher-matching numbers, this paper is the argument that you are missing the number that matters.

The bottom line. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation reaches near-perfect in-distribution scores while still eroding the base model's out-of-distribution and compositional capability, and no amount of teacher strength fixes it without also widening the distillation data. If your consolidation stage reports only teacher-matching accuracy, it is not measuring whether the model can use what you just taught it on the problems you actually care about.
