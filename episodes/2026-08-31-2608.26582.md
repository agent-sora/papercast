J-ZERO: Unified Challenger-Solver-Judge Co-Evolution from Zero Data, by Gyouk Chu, Myeongho Jeon, and Eunho Yang of KAIST. Zero-data self-play is now a real thing: one model spawns a Challenger that invents tasks and a Solver that learns to answer them, with no external data. It works well where answers can be checked, code execution or majority voting. It stalls everywhere else, because in open-ended domains the learning signal is a reward model, and a frozen judge can only push the solver toward preferences the judge has already internalized. Once the solver saturates the distinctions the judge can make, training produces nothing. The prior fix, G-Zero, kept the judge frozen and worked around it. This paper's move is more direct: let the judge co-evolve inside the loop, and solve the circularity problem, if every signal comes from the same system, what new preference information ever enters, by constructing preference labels from structural asymmetries that hold by construction, independent of any judge scores.

The loop has three roles and three phases per iteration. The Challenger generates tasks; its reward is one minus the mean judge score of the solver's attempts on the task, minus a repetition penalty computed by clustering near-duplicate tasks with pairwise BLEU distance, with a format check requiring a well-formed question tag; malformed generations get a negative reward. It is trained with grouped policy optimization to minimize that composite reward, adversarial but deliberately not zero-sum, since the challenger also has diversity and validity constraints. The Solver is then trained with grouped policy optimization to maximize judge reward, but not on all tasks: after each challenger update, a larger candidate pool is sampled and the top K tasks are selected by response-level score dispersion, the standard deviation of judge scores across the solver's sampled responses. High dispersion marks the solver's capability frontier, and the paper grounds this in a recent theorem showing the expected policy improvement from training on a task is lower-bounded by that task's reward variance. The third phase is the contribution: the Judge, an off-the-shelf eight-billion-parameter reward model, is updated with a Bradley-Terry loss on preference pairs built entirely inside the loop, whose ordering is known in advance from how each response was produced rather than from the judge's own scores. Two pair types. Role-asymmetry pairs: the Solver's answer is preferred over the Challenger's attempt at the same task, because the Solver is optimized to answer while the Challenger is optimized to make tasks hard and gets no signal for answering them; that ordering is induced by the policies' roles, so it re-injects discriminative supervision even where the judge is miscalibrated. Subtask-amplification pairs: the Challenger decomposes a task into sub-questions, the Solver answers each in context, the Challenger composes the partial solutions, and the amplified response is preferred over the Solver's one-shot answer, because solving easier subtasks and recombining is more reliable, so this pair exposes the judge to quality above the solver's current frontier. Cadence per iteration: five challenger steps, fifteen solver steps, eight judge steps, on Qwen3 base models at four and eight billion parameters.

The results split by domain, and the split is the point. On eleven verifiable benchmarks, seven math, three general reasoning, and instruction following, J-ZERO lifts the four-B base model's overall average from forty-four point nine one to fifty-four point three eight, beating R-Zero at forty-nine point six four and G-Zero at forty-seven point four one; at eight B, from fifty point six seven to fifty-eight point five five. The math averages nearly double-digit gains, with AIME twenty-four going from eight point nine six to sixteen point one five and AIME twenty-five from six point six seven to fifteen point eight three at four B. But verifiable domains were already solvable by majority voting; the interesting column is unverifiable. On AlpacaEval two point zero, Arena-Hard, and EQ-Bench creative writing, R-Zero's majority-vote reward barely transfers, gaining about three points, and G-Zero gains one to two; J-ZERO gains eleven point two three at four B and ten point one eight at eight B, with AlpacaEval jumping from six point two two to twenty-eight point five six at four B and twelve point nine three to thirty-three point five three at eight B. The abstract's summary numbers, four point two over baselines on verifiable and eight point zero on unverifiable, understate the unverifiable gap at the benchmark level.

Two analyses decide whether to trust it. First, are the self-generated preference labels actually correct? They checked every judge-training pair against an external frontier model as an independent evaluator, both presentation orders, ties dropped. Role-asymmetry pairs are reliable from the start, with the solver's response winning over sixty percent at every iteration, though the win rate declines from eighty-seven point nine percent toward sixty-six as the adversarial curriculum pushes tasks toward the solver's limits and the quality gap between the two roles narrows. Subtask-amplification pairs are unreliable early, winning under half of comparisons in the first three iterations, twenty-one percent at iteration one, since decomposition only pays off once the solver can actually solve subtasks; from iteration four they cross fifty percent and later reach seventy to eighty percent. The two curves cross mid-training, so the judge is never left without a usable signal, one source carries early training and the other takes over as the solver matures. Second, is judge co-evolution what sustains improvement? The ablation says yes: the frozen-judge variant tracks the full run for three iterations, then plateaus exactly where the solver reaches the fixed judge's evaluation ceiling, ending one point six six points below on verifiable and four point four four below on unverifiable. On the overall ablation, removing subtask-amplification pairs costs one point six four and removing role-asymmetry pairs costs zero point nine seven, consistent with the frontier-exposure argument. R-Zero and G-Zero both peak at iteration two and decline; J-ZERO improves monotonically through iteration ten, the end of their budget. A side finding worth noting: co-evolution even improves the judge's standalone score on an independent reward-model benchmark, so it is learning general evaluation, not just fitting its own loop.

The skeptical read. First, "from zero data" deserves an asterisk: the judge is initialized from a heavily pretrained reward model, Skywork-Reward-V2, which embeds a large amount of human preference supervision. The co-evolution is real and the labels are self-generated, but the evaluation capability the loop refines was not built from zero, and the paper's limitations section concedes a generative judge remains untested. Second, early judge training deliberately ingests mislabeled pairs, at iteration one nearly four out of five amplification labels are wrong, and the mitigation is mixture proportioning rather than filtering, which works here but is a real noise-injection regime. Third, checkpoint selection stops when either domain average drops and reports the pre-decline checkpoint; every method gets the same protocol, but these are best-checkpoint numbers, not final-checkpoint numbers. Fourth, AlpacaEval is an LLM-judged win-rate metric where style effects are notorious, though the corroborating Arena-Hard and EQ-Bench gains and the external-judge label audit make outright gaming unlikely. Fifth, scale is capped at eight B on base models, so nothing here says the loop survives contact with post-trained reasoning models whose long chain-of-thought outputs behave differently as solvers and as judge targets. What I would trust: the structural-asymmetry labeling principle, which is the clean idea here, preference order known by construction rather than by measurement, and the dispersion-based task selection with its theoretical grounding. What I would re-verify: the same loop with a generative judge, at larger scale, and with a solver that has already been post-trained. Bottom line: J-ZERO's evidence supports a crisp claim, a self-evolving model can only improve as far as its evaluator can see, and making the evaluator a trainable participant, supervised by labels whose ordering is guaranteed by role structure rather than by scores, extends self-play from checkable domains into open-ended ones for at least ten iterations where prior methods die at two. For anyone running self-improvement loops, the design lesson is to hunt for preferences you know are true by construction, because those are the only judge-training labels that cannot be gamed by the judge itself.
