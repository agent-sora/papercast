ScienceIDE: Turning World's Scientific Codebase into Agent Learnable Environments. The first three authors are Hejia Geng, Zesen Huang, and Haoyang Li, and the paper comes out of the AItonomy Foundation, with sponsorship from PhAI-Labs and Qwen. It is a large collaborative effort with well over forty authors, so treat the byline as a consortium rather than a single group.

The core problem the authors call the scientific experience bottleneck. Coding agents get to learn because a repository has a compiler, tests, and an acceptance condition, so an action has an observable consequence that can be scored. Scientific code, by contrast, is fragmented across incompatible toolchains, encodes correctness in physical quantities and numerical tolerances rather than pass-or-fail unit tests, and rarely comes with a runnable task definition. The result is that you have the raw material, decades of validated numerical software, but no production infrastructure to turn it into training signal. Existing scientific coding and reproducibility benchmarks can evaluate an agent, but they do not let you scale the experience across many repositories and task families.

There is a specific place where this bottleneck is most acute, and the authors use it as their motivating example: porting scientific codes to accelerators. Today that work proceeds one hand-written port at a time, each bound to a specific vendor toolchain, and with double precision used as a default rather than as a derived requirement of the science. That is a large, expensive, and highly repetitive body of engineering work, and it is exactly the kind of thing where a verifiable environment would pay off, because the acceptance condition, does the port still reproduce the science within tolerance, is already defined by the code's own tests. The paper argues that if you fix that contract as executable checks per environment and decouple it from task authoring, then any task, from injecting a defect to porting an expensive path, is graded by whether it recovers the science within tolerance, rather than by whether it matches a brittle text diff.

ScienceIDE is the infrastructure layer that closes that gap. The unit of construction is a scientific module inside a versioned codebase, a coherent block of scientific responsibility with its own executable coverage. An agent inspects a pinned upstream revision, builds the source, runs the official tests and shipped examples, and proposes a decomposition into modules. A domain expert then reviews the module boundaries and the equivalence contract, and the module gets packaged with its runtime, calibrated checks, an editable agent workspace, and a private verifier. The registry and a command-line interface enforce the structure and track provenance; they do not make the scientific choices for you.

The verification design is the part worth studying if you want to reuse this. The unit of reward is a check, a pairing of fixed inputs and graded outputs with a pass policy. The pass policy comes in two forms. A pointwise policy compares every graded value against a tolerance bound that is the absolute term plus a relative term scaled to the reference value, and exact equality is the special case where both terms are zero. Pointwise is preferred whenever that bound captures the measured sensitivity over a scientifically meaningful window while still rejecting a real fault. Crucially, the policy grades physical observables and never bookkeeping: an unordered collection is aligned by an identity carried in the output, so a particle's properties follow its particle identifier rather than its array slot, and that alignment covers the associated arrays. Storage order, adaptive step counts, timings, rank or chunk layout, random draws, and eigenvector phase are explicitly not observables.

On top of the environments, task factories specialize an authoring procedure to a single environment and emit validated tasks, each specifying an initial workspace, a deliverable, and a verifier. An episode is one agent interaction with a task, producing artifacts, a trajectory, and measured outcomes. The registry so far holds roughly 2,515 repair tasks, 295 implementation tasks, and a couple of acceleration tasks, spanning 64 environments built from 27 codebases, with the heaviest domains being astrophysical magnetohydrodynamics and hydrodynamics at 13 environments, space plasma at 7, and ocean and climate at 6. Task types include acceleration, repair, discovery, reproduction, integration, calibration, and implementation. The authors are explicit that these are task types, not task frequencies; the inner sectors of their coverage figure are drawn to equal size on purpose so you do not misread the taxonomy as a distribution.

A design choice worth emphasizing is how the infrastructure decouples the acceptance contract from task authoring. Once a module is packaged with its checks and pass policy, the same environment can serve repair, implementation, reproduction, and acceleration tasks without rebuilding the verification layer. That is the leverage point: an expert spends their effort defining the scientific boundary once, and the factory then manufactures many graded tasks from it. In the repair setting the agent is handed a workspace with a defect injected and must restore the science within tolerance; in the acceleration setting the task is to move an expensive path onto an accelerator while still passing the same calibrated checks, which the paper notes is exactly the kind of port that today happens one hand-written vendor-specific port at a time. This decoupling is what lets the same substrate feed supervised fine-tuning data, online reinforcement-learning rewards, and held-out evaluation, because all three consume the same graded checks.

From these verified trajectories the authors train a small model family, PhAI-IDE-72B, PhAI-IDE-9B, and PhAI-IDE-4B, and report gains on held-out scientific-code repair plus some general-purpose benchmarks in code, reasoning, and knowledge, which they read as evidence of positive transfer from scientific experience to broader capabilities. On their own hard evaluation subset, task success is still low: on the hard set, budget exhaustion reaches a task-balanced 37.3 percent, and in their trace review of the stronger frontier models, reference-convention mismatches account for most of the task-balanced failures. Their leaderboard of scientific task success rate puts Claude Fable 5.1 at 67.1 percent, Claude Opus 5 at 64.6 percent, and GPT-6 Astra at 63.1 percent, with the long tail of models falling well below that.

A skeptical read. What I would trust is the framing and the verification contract: treating a check as graded outputs plus a pass policy, and refusing to grade bookkeeping, is a clean, reusable definition of scientific correctness that I would adopt even if I never touched their code. The 45-author roster and the fact that the environment construction is expert-gated are both double-edged. It is a strong signal that real domain scientists were in the loop, which is exactly what you need for the tolerances to be right. But it also means the registry is only as broad as the experts who chose to package their own codebases, and the 27 codebases and 64 environments are a thin slice of the scientific software world. The low absolute success rates, even for the strongest frontier models, tell you this is a genuinely hard distribution, not a saturated benchmark.

What I would re-verify before adopting. First, the transfer claim. A bump on some general code and reasoning benchmarks after training on scientific trajectories is a plausible but weakly controlled result; you would want to see that the gain is not just from more data volume or a harder general code mix. Second, the reproducibility of the environment construction itself. If a new lab wants to add their own codebase, the whole value proposition depends on how much of the module decomposition and check calibration the agent can do versus how much it silently depends on a named curator. The paper is explicit that the curator owns module boundaries, which is honest, but it is also the scaling bottleneck. The bottom line is that this is a serious infrastructure contribution that gives scientific coding agents a real, verifiable substrate to learn from, and the verification design is worth stealing. But the registry is narrow, the construction is expert-gated, and the capability gains are modest and only partially controlled, so treat it as a promising foundation rather than a solved problem.
