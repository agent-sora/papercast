On-Policy Morphing into Hybrid Attention Models, by Disen Lan, Xuanda Wang, and Jianbin Zheng, at ByteDance Seed, Fudan University, and Nanyang Technological University, is a method for converting a pre-trained Transformer into a hybrid attention model, where a few layers keep full attention and the rest switch to linear attention, and the hard sub-problem it tackles is deciding which layers should keep full attention under a fixed budget. This is the standing-rule topology paper of the day, an alternative attention mechanism applied to a reasoning model, so it gets an episode on that basis even though its upvote rank is lower than the rest of today's slate. The method, called FlashMorph2, is an on-policy layer-selection procedure, and the central claim is that the existing off-policy method, FlashMorph, makes two mistakes that systematically pick the wrong layers, and that evaluating the actual discrete hybrid architecture end-to-end, with the model's own hidden states, fixes both.

The context is why hybrid attention matters. Full attention is expensive, both in compute and in the KV cache memory it holds, and linear attention is cheap but weaker at precise retrieval over long context. A hybrid model keeps full attention in a small number of layers where precise retrieval matters, and uses linear attention in the rest, which gives you most of the quality at a fraction of the cost, and this tradeoff is why hybrid attention is showing up in production-scale language models. But converting an existing pre-trained model into a hybrid is not a free operation, because once you swap most of the attention layers, the hidden states that flow through the network change, and the layers that were the right ones to keep under full attention in the original model may not be the right ones in the hybrid. The whole problem is therefore a layer-selection problem under a budget, and the quality of your selection determines how much quality you keep for the compute you save.

The prior method, FlashMorph, does this selection off-policy, and the paper identifies two specific off-policy mismatches. The first is that FlashMorph evaluates a continuous interpolation between the full-attention and linear-attention branches, using teacher-forced hidden states, but the actual hybrid model you deploy is discrete, each layer is either full or linear, and it runs on its own hidden states, not the teacher's. So the method is scoring a continuous surrogate that does not match the discrete system you actually ship. The second mismatch is that FlashMorph freezes the model weights during selection, but in practice you then run a recovery fine-tune after you swap the layers, and that recovery adapts the weights, so the frozen-weight selection does not reflect the post-adaptation performance that is what you will actually get. Both of these are the classic off-policy error, you are evaluating a model in a state it will not be in when it runs, and the paper's argument is that for layer selection, where the budget is tight and the choice is discrete, those errors are large enough to pick the wrong layers.

FlashMorph2 fixes both by going on-policy. The first fix is to evaluate sampled discrete hybrid architectures end-to-end using their own hidden states, so you score the actual discrete system, not a continuous surrogate, and the hidden states are the ones the hybrid itself produces, not the teacher's. The second fix is to jointly optimize the layer-selection scores and the model weights under the recovery objective, so the selection reflects the post-adaptation performance, the state the model will actually be in after you fine-tune. The two fixes are coupled, because the right layers depend on how the weights will adapt, and the weight adaptation depends on which layers you kept, so you have to optimize them together rather than select the layers first and then fine-tune. That joint optimization is the conceptual core of the method, and it is what distinguishes it from a two-stage approach that picks layers and then recovers.

The results are across Qwen3 model scales and across linear-attention variants, which is the breadth that matters for a method you might apply to your own model. FlashMorph2 consistently outperforms the existing layer-selection methods, improving the average score over FlashMorph by 2.42 points on RULER, which is the long-context retrieval and reasoning benchmark, by 1.08 on LongBench, the general long-context benchmark, and by 0.76 on recall-intensive tasks specifically, which is the category where full attention layers are supposed to be doing their work, so a gain there is the most direct evidence that the method is keeping full attention in the right layers. The efficiency result is the other half of the claim: it completes the layer selection in 0.72 to 3.57 GPU hours across the 0.6 billion to 8 billion parameter models, which is small enough that the on-policy evaluation, which is more expensive than a single off-policy pass, does not make the method impractical. The scaling of the GPU hours with model size is the number to watch if you are considering this at a larger scale, because the on-policy end-to-end evaluations are what drive the cost.

The skeptical reading. First, the gains over FlashMorph, 2.42 on RULER and 1.08 on LongBench, are real but modest in absolute terms, and they are measured relative to FlashMorph specifically, so the question for adoption is whether FlashMorph is the right baseline, because if a simpler heuristic, like keeping full attention in the layers with the highest retrieval load, gets close to FlashMorph2 at a fraction of the GPU hours, the on-policy machinery may not be worth it. The 0.72 to 3.57 GPU hour cost is small at 0.6 to 8 billion, but the cost scales with the number of end-to-end evaluations you run, and at frontier scale the on-policy selection budget is the thing that could make this impractical, so the efficiency claim is established at the small-to-mid scale, not at the scale where hybrid attention is most valuable. Second, the joint optimization of selection and weights is the method's strength and its complexity in one, and it means the result is harder to decompose, so you cannot easily tell how much of the gain comes from evaluating the discrete architecture versus from the joint weight adaptation, and that decomposition is what you would want before trusting that the method transfers to a model family where the recovery fine-tune behaves differently. Third, the linear-attention variants tested are a specific set, and the layer-selection quality could depend on which linear attention you pair with the full attention, so the results are conditional on the specific hybrids evaluated.

For implementation, the reusable idea is the on-policy evaluation principle itself, when you are making a discrete architectural choice under a budget, evaluate the actual discrete configuration end-to-end on its own hidden states rather than a continuous surrogate, and account for the downstream adaptation by optimizing the choice and the weights together. If you are converting a model to a hybrid, the concrete takeaways are the RULER and LongBench deltas as the target metrics to beat, and the GPU-hour budget as the cost you are trading for a better selection. The thing to verify on your own model is whether the on-policy selection cost stays affordable as you scale, and whether a cheaper heuristic comes within the 2.42 point gap, because that comparison is what decides whether FlashMorph2 is the right tool or just the right answer at a small scale.

To summarize the bottom line. FlashMorph2 is a principled fix to a real off-policy error in hybrid-attention layer selection, and it delivers consistent, if modest, gains over FlashMorph across Qwen3 scales and linear-attention variants, with an efficiency profile that is affordable at the 0.6 to 8 billion scale. Its limits are the modest absolute gains, the unresolved decomposition of where the win comes from, and the open question of whether the on-policy cost stays acceptable at frontier scale. If you are morphing a model into a hybrid and the budget is tight, the on-policy, jointly-optimized selection is the more defensible choice, provided the evaluation cost fits your compute.
