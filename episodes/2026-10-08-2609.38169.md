STEPQuant is from Bingchen Yao, Haobo Xu, and Haokun Lin, at Zhejiang University, the Institute of Automation at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Tsinghua University, with the full team of nine authors and Ying Wei as the corresponding author. The paper addresses a specific serving problem that keeps coming up as linear attention models get deployed: the recurrent state that replaces the growing key-value cache is itself a memory bottleneck, and quantizing it naively destroys accuracy. Their claim is that you do not need to quantize the whole state uniformly, because the damage from quantization is concentrated, and if you measure where it is concentrated and spend your precision there, you can push recurrent states down to about six bits with almost no accuracy loss.

To set up the problem, linear attention and related recurrent models, like the delta rule variants, replace the quadratic key-value cache with a fixed-size state matrix that is updated at every token. Under concurrent serving, one request carries its own state, so the state, not the weights, dominates memory. The tempting fix is to quantize that state to low precision, but the paper's first contribution is showing why that fails. A quantization error made at one step does not stay at that step. The state is read out at the current token and then fed into the next update, so the error propagates forward through successive state updates. Whether that propagated error actually matters depends on two things they isolate. Temporally, an error in a memory component that lives for a long time, meaning a state entry that persists across many decoding steps before it decays, persists itself across many steps and accumulates. Spatially, not all key rows of the state are equal; some rows are read heavily and move the output a lot, while others barely matter, and the magnitudes along rows and columns vary by an order of magnitude. So a uniform bit budget throws precision at rows and steps that do not need it and starves the ones that do.

The method has two matching pieces. On the temporal axis, they allocate bits according to the lifetime of each memory component, giving more precision to long-lived state and less to short-lived state that will decay away anyway. They characterize lifetime through the decay structure of the update rule, so a state entry whose decay coefficient is close to one, and therefore survives many steps, gets more bits than one that is wiped out within a handful of steps. On the spatial axis, they use what they call key-row-aware dual-axis fitting: they jointly fit per-key-row scales and per-value-column scales, weighting the fitting objective by a calibrated row-impact score that measures how much perturbing that key row moves the model output. The calibration statistics are computed from captured state snapshots across layers, heads, and steps, and the paper reports that the row-to-row contrast in RMS magnitude is large, with median key-row contrasts around ten times and value-column contrasts around twenty times, both exceeding three times in the large majority of snapshots. That is the empirical justification for not using a single scale for the whole state. The whole thing is a post-training procedure; you calibrate the scales on a small set of reference streams and then use them at serving time, and at runtime the state is reconstructed from integer codes and scales, the readout happens before requantization, and the new row and column factors are fit and written back before the next recurrent step. There is a proof in the appendix bounding the cumulative output error in terms of the row-impact scores and the readout error, which is the part I would actually want to read before trusting the calibration, because the entire method rests on the row-impact score being a good proxy for downstream damage.

The experiments run two real recurrent models, Qwen3.8-27B with its linear attention and Kimi-Linear-48B-A3B-Instruct, on both long and short generation benchmarks. The headline result is that at a nominal six-bit budget, STEPQuant closely matches the full precision state. On the combined math-and-coding evaluation they report, the six-bit configuration lands within a fraction of a point of the full precision score of about 84.61 percent. That is the number to hold onto, because it is the argument that low precision is feasible for recurrent states at all.

The negative baseline is more instructive. If you just quantize uniformly to four bits, the failure mode is not a small accuracy drop, it is a length explosion. On AIME, Kimi generates an average of 63,400 tokens, and on HMMT about 64,220 tokens, right up against the 65,536-token generation limit, while scoring near zero on both. The quantization error pushes the model into long, unproductive generation loops instead of failing fast. There is also a striking artifact at the extreme: on WinoGrande, a binary two-choice task, uniform four-bit scoring gives 46.57 percent on Qwen and 17.36 percent on KDA. You are scoring below chance on a two-way question because at that precision some generations lose the answer format entirely and never emit a valid choice. That is a useful warning that accuracy below fifty percent on a binary task is not a bug, it is a sign the generation is structurally broken.

The serving numbers are where the motivation pays off. Integrated into SGLang on A800 GPUs, the six-bit configuration reduces total serving memory from about 419.73 gigabytes to 131.18 gigabytes, a 68.7 percent reduction, and reduces recurrent-state memory by 80.1 percent, which is a 5.03 times compression, and cuts state-update time by 65.6 percent, about 2.91 times faster. For Qwen the state is 37,748,736 elements per request, and the six-bit packing works out to about 4.62 bits per value once you include the pivot heads and scales, giving a compact representation ratio of about 6.93 times relative to full precision. The four-bit budget for the delta-rule model is even more aggressive at 4.30 bits per value.

A skeptical read. The calibration is the whole ballgame, and it is a post-training fit, so its quality depends entirely on the reference streams being representative of your serving traffic. They calibrate on 32 segments of WikiText-2 at 2,048 tokens each, plus some AIME streams, and they show the row-impact rankings are stable across text sources, which is reassuring, but the math-heavy failure mode, the length explosion, is exactly the regime you most care about and it is not the regime you calibrated on. I would re-verify the six-bit match on long, agentic, or code-heavy workloads before trusting it, because that is where the error propagation is worst and where the uniform baseline blew up. The second thing I would check is the row-impact score itself: the paper gives a bound relating it to output error, but a bound is not a guarantee, and the dual-axis fitting couples row scales and column scales in a way that could be sensitive to calibration distribution shift. The compression ratios are also analytical counts of the compact format, not measured end-to-end including tensor-parallel sharding, so the real serving footprint may not hit those numbers exactly. The one thing I would trust immediately is the negative result about uniform low-precision quantization breaking generation length, because that is a robust, well-measured phenomenon and it is the strongest reason to bother with a non-uniform scheme at all.

The code is released, which helps, since the calibration and the dual-axis fitting are nontrivial enough that you would want to read the implementation rather than reimplement it from the appendix. Bottom line: if you are serving delta-rule or linear attention models and the recurrent state is eating your memory, non-uniform, lifetime- and row-impact-aware quantization is the right direction, and the six-bit result is credible for the calibrated regime. Before you adopt it, re-calibrate on your own workload and re-check long-generation stability, because the failure mode when you get the bit allocation wrong is not a small score drop, it is models that generate up to the token limit and score zero.
