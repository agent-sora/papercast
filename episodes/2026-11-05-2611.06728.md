Turns Matter, Advancing Streaming Video Reinforcement Learning via Turn-Level Credit, is from Shuai Dong, Kanghui Tian, and Linghan Chen, with the broader team at Fudan University, Shanghai Innovation Institute, JD.com, Stanford University, and the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory.

Streaming video understanding is a weird problem because the video does not stop. An offline model waits for the full clip, thinks, and answers once. A streaming model watches a live feed and, every turn, has to make a binary decision: speak now, or keep watching. A good assistant answers when new evidence lands, follows up when the question needs more, and stays silent when another reply would just be noise. The paper studies how to train that policy with reinforcement learning, and the central claim is that the standard recipe, Group Relative Policy Optimization, applies credit at the wrong granularity.

Here is the setup. GRPO, as used in the existing streaming-video literature, samples a group of rollouts, scores each whole trajectory, and assigns one trajectory-level advantage to every token in it. The problem the authors show in their motivating figure is concrete: in a cooking video, the model first gives a correct reply about the dish, then repeats itself several times as the stream continues. Under shared credit, every turn inherits the same positive advantage, so the repetitions get reinforced exactly like the first useful reply. Later turns that add nothing are rewarded for free, and useful follow-ups after a failure get drowned out by the earlier signal. The paper's argument is that the interaction is turn-structured but the learning signal is not, and that mismatch is what you want to fix before you start fiddling with reward design.

Their framework is called TurnWise, and it has two pieces. The first is turn-level credit assignment via silence-completed prefix scoring. The idea is that each turn ends when the model chooses silence, so the return of a given decision is what remains after the rewards already accrued before it. For each turn, they compute the remaining return from that decision onward, then build group-relative advantages from those per-turn quantities rather than from one global number. In effect, they subtract the group-relative accrued reward before each decision, so the learning signal for the fifth reply reflects only what the fifth reply contributed. In their figure, the shared GRPO column assigns roughly the same advantage of about one point to every turn, while the TurnWise column spreads a range of values, roughly from plus 0.98 down to minus 1.03, so repetitions come out negative and genuinely informative turns stay positive.

The second piece is turn-mean policy optimization. Token-level loss weighting has a hidden bias: long replies dominate the gradient simply because they have more tokens. A verbose repeat and a terse correct answer do not cost the same. TurnWise averages the token loss within each turn first, and then averages across turns, so a response decision and a wait decision get equal weight regardless of how long the response was. This is a cheap, structural fix for length-biased credit, and it does not require a learned critic or any additional rollouts, which matters because rollout cost is the dominant expense in this kind of training.

Before the numbers, a word on what the benchmarks measure, because the metric choices are part of the contribution. ProactiveVQA scores proactivity with PAUC, the area under the proactive-accuracy curve, which captures whether the model speaks when it should without spamming, rather than just whether the final answer is right. OVO-Timing scores timing quality with a timing F1, measuring how well the reply lands relative to the moment the relevant event happens in the video. StreamingBench measures general visual understanding across the stream. That split matters for reading the results: a method can win on understanding without being a better interactive agent, and the paper's claims are strongest on the interaction metrics.
Now the numbers, and these are where the paper earns its keep. They run everything on two backbones, Qwen 2.5 VL 3 billion and Qwen 3 VL 4 billion, and compare against supervised fine-tuning and GRPO-based baselines on three streaming benchmarks. On the 3 billion model, TurnWise reaches 43.56 PAUC on ProactiveVQA, 21.59 average Timing F1 on OVO-Timing, and 49.43 average accuracy on StreamingBench. Those exceed the strongest competing results by 3.96, 3.87, and 0.69 points respectively. On the 4 billion model the scores are 42.44, 11.15, and 58.96, with gains of 6.26, 1.92, and 2.38. The StreamingBench gap is small, which is worth noting: the timing and proactivity gains are much larger than the raw visual-understanding gains, consistent with the credit-assignment story.

Two results I would actually trust. First, on ProactiveVQA the 4 billion model reaches 38.40 proactive accuracy against 35.60 for the strongest baseline, and real-time accuracy improves from 78.24 to 79.52, so the improvement is in the interaction behavior, not in seeing the video better. Second, the decomposition result: combining turn-level credit with turn-mean aggregation cuts the repetition metric by 61.5 percent relative to credit-only optimization, from 15.53 to 5.98, while adding 2.96 points of Timing F1. The two components do different jobs, one points the gradient in the right direction per turn, the other equalizes the weight of long and short turns, and the ablation shows you need both.

The ablations go one level deeper than usual. They take the same sampled training groups from the 3 billion model and recompute advantages under both allocations; on 432 of 722 groups the per-turn credit changes the learning direction relative to the global advantage, and the full combination lands at the best score across all four metrics. There is also a sensitivity check on the correction strength, where full correction, alpha equal to 1, gives the best PAUC, Timing F1, and StreamingBench accuracy and the lowest repetition. That is a nice property: the method does not introduce a tuning knob you have to search.

So what would I re-verify before adopting this? Three things, and they are in order of how much they matter to me. The margins on StreamingBench, 0.69 and 2.38 points, are close enough to run-to-run variance that I would want confidence intervals or a second seed before I called the visual-understanding gains real; the proactivity and timing margins are much more defensible because they are several times larger than the noise floor I would expect from these benchmarks. Second, silence-completed prefix scoring assumes the silence token reliably marks a turn boundary, and in practice streaming models can emit degenerate silence or never terminate at all; the paper reports the clean case, and the failure mode is that a bad boundary corrupts exactly the per-turn return the method is supposed to fix, so the boundary detector is a dependency you inherit. Third, the whole comparison is within two Qwen VL backbones and three benchmarks, all from the same ecosystem; whether the credit fix transfers to a different model family, to a different video domain, or to audio-visual streams is untested, and given that the mechanism is generic group-relative scoring, I would expect it to transfer, but untested is untested.

The engineering cost is low, which is the practical argument. No critic, no extra rollouts, just a different advantage computation and a different loss aggregation, so the training loop stays the same shape and the change is a few lines of credit bookkeeping plus the averaging. If you are already running GRPO for any turn-structured policy, this is a cheap experiment to run against your baseline.

Two-sentence verdict. TurnWise is a well-motivated, cheap credit-assignment fix that produces large, consistent gains on streaming-video proactivity and timing, with a clean ablation story and no new training machinery. I would trust the interaction-quality results, treat the modest visual-understanding margins as provisional pending a second seed, and test it on my own streaming task before counting on it.
