EvolveTrade: Experience-Driven Policy Refinement for Self-Evolving LLM Trading Agents. The first three authors are Sehee Kim, Yumin Choi, and Minki Kang, and the work comes out of KAIST, with the senior author affiliated with DeepAuto.ai. It is a method paper on making a tool-using trading agent improve its own operating policy over time.

The premise. A large language model trading agent can combine market data, news, and executable analysis, but its behavior is usually controlled by a static, hand-written tool-use policy that is fixed before deployment. The prompt that tells the agent how to gather evidence, which tools to call, how to verify signals, and how to manage risk is written once, and the agent is stuck with it across every market regime it encounters. That is a real limitation, because the right way to gather evidence and manage risk in a rising market is not the right way in a choppy or falling one. EvolveTrade treats the system prompt of the tool-using agent as a text-parameterized policy, and revises that policy after each update interval using the accumulated decision traces and the realized portfolio feedback, while keeping the backbone LLM fixed. The agent is thus refining its own information-acquisition and portfolio-construction procedure over time, without retraining a single weight.

The mechanism is a separation between two roles. There is a Policy Agent that does the refining, and there is the trading agent that does the deciding. After each interval, the Policy Agent reads the accumulated traces of what the trading agent did and the realized returns and drawdowns those decisions produced, and it rewrites the policy text. The updated policy is then used for the next batch of trading decisions. So the loop is: decide with the current policy, collect traces and realized feedback, have the Policy Agent revise the policy, repeat. The paper reports that across multiple market regimes and two LLM backbones, EvolveTrade can outperform a reinforcement-learning baseline on this task, which is a notable claim, because the usual assumption is that you need gradient-based learning to get an agent to adapt, and here the adaptation is happening in text space.

There is an important constraint on what the policy is allowed to encode, and it is the thing that keeps the method honest. The paper includes the actual evolved policy text in its traces, and it is not a vague instruction like "be careful with risk." It is an explicit, inspectable decision procedure. For the allocation step it specifies a deterministic scoring rule, a normalized Sharpe term plus half a momentum rank term minus a volatility term and a drawdown penalty, converted into weights through a capped softmax with explicit floors and caps, and it lays out the portfolio constraints as hard rules, a maximum single-equity weight, a cap on top-three concentration, and a limit on how much any position can move in a single day. The agent is required to end each trace with the target weight and the rebalancing action, so the decision is machine-checkable. That is the key design choice: the policy evolves by rewriting an explicit, verifiable procedure rather than by nudging an opaque prompt, which is what makes the whole thing auditable and what separates it from the hand-wavy "the prompt got better" stories you see in other self-evolving-agent work.

The evaluation is the part to scrutinize. The authors run the agent across several market windows, in February, April, and November across two years, with two backbones, GPT-5-mini and Gemini-2.5-Flash, and report the mean and standard deviation across three runs for each. The metrics are the standard ones for this literature: a Sharpe ratio for risk-adjusted performance, a cumulative return, and a turnover term, with lower turnover being better. In the November 2025 window with GPT-5-mini, EvolveTrade reports a Sharpe of about 5.12 and a cumulative return of about 5.10 percent, compared with the static base agent at about 1.55 Sharpe and the static tool-calling agent at about 2.87. In the April 2026 window the picture is different, and the paper is honest about it: in the two April windows EvolveTrade underperforms the selected static baseline, and the authors characterize the regime and note that EvolveTrade's average cash weight exceeds the baseline's by 10.1 and 35.9 percentage points in those windows, meaning it sat in cash and missed the move. The allocation traces they include show a deterministic scoring rule, a normalized Sharpe plus a momentum rank term minus volatility and drawdown penalties, converted through a capped softmax, which is a useful detail because it tells you the policy text is encoding an explicit, inspectable decision rule rather than an opaque instruction.

A skeptical read. What I trust is the framing and the mechanism, that the system prompt is a legitimate policy parameter you can evolve in text space, and the honesty of the regime breakdown. Showing the April underperformance and explaining it as the agent sitting in cash is exactly the kind of result a weaker paper would hide, and the fact that they include the per-day allocation traces, down to the specific weights on specific tickers, makes the mechanism auditable in a way most trading-agent papers are not. The claim that prompt-level evolution can beat a reinforcement-learning baseline on this task is interesting and, if reproduced, would be a meaningful data point for the whole "evolve the prompt instead of the weights" line of work.

What I would push on before trusting the headline. First, the baselines. The paper compares against a static base agent and a static tool-calling agent, and the reinforcement-learning baseline is the one that would actually settle the "text evolution versus gradient learning" question, so I would want to see that comparison stated more explicitly with the same training budget and the same data splits, because an RL baseline trained on a different amount of experience is not a fair arbiter. Second, the regime sensitivity is the real finding and it is underweighted. A method that wins in two of the tested windows and loses in the other two, with the loss explained by excess cash exposure, is not a robust trader, it is a regime-dependent one, and the practical takeaway is that the evolved policy encodes a specific risk posture that happens to fit some markets and not others. That is a useful and honest conclusion, but it is a narrower result than "self-evolving trading agents work," and I would read the abstract accordingly. The per-day allocation traces, with the deterministic scoring rule and the explicit constraints, are the most valuable artifact in the paper for an implementer, because they make clear exactly what the evolved policy looks like at the end of a run, and that is the part worth reproducing.

What I would re-verify before trusting the numbers. First, the backtest hygiene. These are small windows with three runs, the standard deviations are wide in places, and a Sharpe ratio computed over a few weeks of a single asset universe is an extremely noisy estimate. The April cash-sitting result is a warning that the evolved policy can overfit to the regime it just saw and then be wrong in the next one. Second, the leakage question. If the Policy Agent is revising the policy using realized feedback that includes the forward returns the trading agent is being scored on, you have to be careful that the "improvement" is not the policy memorizing which window was favorable. The paper does separate the decide and refine roles, but the realized-portfolio feedback feeding the refinement is the channel through which lookahead would enter, and I would want to see the exact temporal boundary between what the Policy Agent is allowed to see and what counts as the test period. The bottom line is that this is an honest, inspectable method that makes a plausible case for text-space policy evolution as a complement to weight-space learning, but the evaluation windows are short and noisy, the regime dependence is real and sometimes hurts, and the backtest details need a closer look before the Sharpe numbers mean what they look like.
