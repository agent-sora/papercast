"Foundations of Large Language Models," by Tong Xiao and Jingbo Zhu, from the NLP Lab at Northeastern University and NiuTrans Research. This is not a research paper. It is a book, a selection of chapters from their open-source introductory NLP resource, published on arXiv as a single document of more than three hundred pages. It appeared on the Hugging Face daily list with thirty four upvotes, which is a respectable signal for a textbook, and it is worth a few minutes of airtime because it is one of the most complete single-volume accounts of the full LLM stack, from pre-training through reasoning, written in a language that an implementation-minded reader can actually use.

The central thesis of the preface is the paradigm shift that large language models forced on the field. Before them, natural language processing meant training specialized systems from scratch on large labeled datasets, one system per task. The insight the authors credit to the LLM era is that knowledge of the world and of language can be acquired through large-scale language modeling itself. Train one universal model on text at scale, and you obtain a foundation model that you then fine-tune, align, and prompt. That single move reorganized the discipline, and the book is structured around it.

The book has six chapters. Chapter one covers pre-training, the foundation layer: common pre-training objectives and the model architectures behind them, including the Transformer as the load-bearing structure. Chapter two covers generative models, the autoregressive decoder paradigm that produces the models people mean today, plus how to scale training up and how to handle long texts. Chapter three is prompting: the basic strategies, and the advanced territory of chain-of-thought reasoning and automatic prompt design. Chapter four is alignment, instruction fine-tuning and learning from human feedback, which is where the gap between a raw language model and a deployable assistant is closed. Chapter five is inference: decoding algorithms, acceleration methods, and inference-time scaling, the growing family of techniques that buy quality at test time rather than at training time. And chapter six is reasoning, how training can strengthen a model's ability to reason and how additional computation during inference helps it solve complex problems.

For an implementation-minded listener, the structure is the main value. The authors make each chapter self-contained, and they state explicitly that no prior knowledge of Transformers is assumed, which is unusual for a text at this level of detail. The book is licensed under a Creative Commons attribution non-commercial license, and the full resource, including material beyond this arXiv selection, is on GitHub under the NiuTrans organization. That combination, permissive enough to teach from, structured enough to follow end to end, and free, is what earns it a place on the list.

Two chapters deserve a closer look for the implementation-minded listener. The prompting and reasoning chapters are where the book earns its place in a 2026 curriculum, because they treat elicitation as a discipline with named failure modes rather than as prompt tricks. The prompting chapter walks through the basic strategies in order: zero-shot and few-shot framing, then the advanced territory of chain-of-thought, where the model is asked to externalize intermediate steps before committing to an answer, and automatic prompt design, where the prompt itself is treated as an object to be searched over. The reasoning chapter connects the two halves of the story. On the training side, it covers how reinforcement and preference objectives can strengthen a model's ability to carry a multi-step argument without derailing. On the inference side, it covers test-time computation, where the same model is given more steps, more sampled attempts, or more verification, and the quality of the final answer moves with the budget. That pairing, training that installs the capability and inference that spends compute to extract it, is the organizing idea of the back half of the book, and it is stated clearly enough that a reader can map the current paper stream onto it without guessing.

The alignment chapter is the other one I would flag, because it treats instruction fine-tuning and human-feedback alignment as engineering disciplines rather than folklore. The book walks through the pipeline in order: why a raw pre-trained model, which has learned to continue text, is not yet a helpful assistant; what instruction fine-tuning does to the output distribution, turning open-ended continuation into task completion; and what preference-based optimization adds on top, shaping the model not just to be correct but to be acceptable, to refuse appropriately, and to calibrate its confidence. The framing is consistently practical. Each technique is introduced as a modification to the training objective, with the failure mode it addresses stated up front, which is exactly the structure an engineer needs when deciding which stage of the pipeline to invest in.

The inference chapter is the other one with genuine currency. Decoding is presented as a family of tradeoffs rather than a single knob: greedy and sampling baselines, constrained and structured decoding for formats that must parse, and then the acceleration methods that make serving economically viable, from batching and quantization to the speculative techniques that use a small model to draft for a large one. Inference-time scaling gets its own treatment, the observation that the quality of the output can be purchased with additional computation at test time, through longer chains of thought, multiple sampled solutions with selection, and verification loops. The authors frame this as a second scaling axis, parallel to the pre-training scale, and the discussion of where each axis pays off is the part I would send to a team deciding how to allocate compute this quarter.

The pre-training and architecture chapters set the table. Chapter one treats pre-training as an objective-design problem first and a scaling problem second. It covers the common language modeling objectives, the shift from bidirectional encoders to decoder-only models, and why that shift won, in terms a reader can follow without having read the original papers. The architecture discussion is given the space it deserves, because everything downstream, attention patterns, positional encodings, context length behavior, assumes the reader can picture a Transformer block and say what each component is for. The preface's promise of self-containment is kept here, which is more than most textbooks at this scope manage.

What it is not, and the readership should know that going in, is a survey of the current frontier. The authors are explicit that the book focuses on foundational concepts rather than comprehensive coverage of cutting-edge methods. It will not teach you the latest reasoning model architecture or the newest alignment objective. It teaches you the stable layer under all of those: what pre-training optimizes, why decoder-only generation became the default, how prompting and alignment shape behavior, what the inference-time tradeoffs are, and how reasoning gets both trained and elicited. That stable layer is exactly what a working engineer needs before reading the weekly paper stream, because the papers assume it.

The skeptical notes are mostly about format. At over three hundred pages, this is a reference, not a listenable artifact, and the arXiv listing is a chapter selection rather than the complete book, so the version you get from the link is a subset of the GitHub resource. There is no new experimental claim to verify, no benchmark to reproduce, and nothing in it will change your view of the state of the art this week. Its value is entirely educational, and on that axis it competes with other open LLM textbooks, where the main differentiator is completeness of coverage across the full stack in a single volume.

My verdict is that if you are building an LLM application and want one canonical, free, implementation-oriented text that covers the entire pipeline from pre-training to reasoning without assuming prior expertise, this is a legitimate choice and a reasonable first read. It will not surprise you, but it will not mislead you, and it is properly open.
