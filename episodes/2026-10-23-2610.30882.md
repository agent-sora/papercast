The paper is Inherited, Not Invented: Tracing the Provenance of Wait in Reasoning Models, from Zhizhuo Kou, Sizai Hou, and Long Chen at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, with Jian Yang, Jingshu Peng, Yike Guo and Sirui Han at HKUST, Junyu Luo of Peking University, and Wenkai Zhang of the Huawei Noah's Ark Lab, and Sirui Han as the senior author. This is an interpretability and provenance paper about a very specific artifact in reasoning models: the word wait. There is a widespread story in the literature that reinforcement learning is what teaches reasoning models to write wait before reconsidering a step, a little verbal habit that marks the moment the model catches itself and backtracks. The authors push back on exactly that story. Their claim is that the habit is mostly inherited, not invented: the marker shows up in the model because it was in the training data, and RL rarely coins it on its own. The paper's job is to trace the word through the open training histories of a family of models and separate what RL discovers from what a model inherits from mid-training and fine-tuning. This is a small, careful, provenance question, and the kind of question that is hard to answer unless you have access to the checkpoints along the way, which is why the open-model ecosystem is what makes the paper possible.

The method is label-free, which is the part I would point to first, because it means the findings do not depend on anyone's annotation of what a wait is doing. The authors sample outputs from a set of model endpoints, one hundred twenty endpoints and recipes in total across the training series, and they compute a battery of statistics on the marker's occurrence: its frequency, its position in the trace, its form, and how it is used. The form matters, because wait shows up as a standalone reconsideration marker, as wait, wait, with capitalization variants, and the statistics track these as distinct. The injected-error test is the cleanest instrument in the paper: they introduce a known error into a problem and watch whether the model's wait usage actually tracks the error, which would mean the marker is doing real diagnostic work, versus appearing at a fixed rate regardless of whether there is anything to catch. The models in scope include the open RL-Zero and mid-training recipes for OLMo-3, MiMo, and SmolLM3, and the evaluation benchmark is MATH-500, with two to eight sampled responses per problem under fixed decoding per series.

The findings, in the order the paper presents them, form a clean narrative. The headline provenance result: in all traced lineages but one, the revision marker originates in pre-RL training data, which the authors read the way a historical linguist reads a word, from its occurrences alone. The first operational finding: zero-RL almost never coins the marker. On base models that lack the word, RL raises MATH-500 accuracy substantially while wait stays near absent, so the large accuracy gains that get attributed to RL do not require the verbal habit at all. The second: the marker arrives with training data. It appears during mid-training in OLMo-3, and fine-tuning on reasoning traces installs it in most models whose base lacks it. The third, and the one that most directly challenges the popular story: where the base model already uses the marker, the released RL-Zero recipes for OLMo-3 and MiMo multiply it, whereas KL-free GRPO from the same base does not, and OLMo-3's specific recipe wears down the marker's form, making it a more sensitive but more liberal alarm. RL after supervised fine-tuning barely changes the marker at all, and the authors' own GRPO recipe erases a marker that fine-tuning had seeded. The last finding is a methodological caution: marker frequency alone does not indicate RL-induced reasoning, and the paper argues against LLM-as-a-judge revision metrics, on the ground that automated judges can reward the appearance of backtracking without it tracking a real correction.

The injected-error test deserves its own paragraph, because it is the instrument that turns the frequency statistics into a behavioral claim. The setup is simple: the authors construct, for each problem, a prefix of correct steps and a parallel copy with one injected arithmetic error, continue both prefixes with the model, and record whether a revision marker appears within two sentences of the error. If wait is a genuine self-correction signal, its rate should rise in the error copy, the model should use it at the moment it is supposed to catch itself, and the usage should be concentrated around the error site. If it is a stylistic habit, the marker appears at roughly the same rate in both copies. The paper reports the marker's occurrence, position, form, and usage statistics against this test across the sampled outputs, and the finding feeds directly into the final methodological caution: because the frequency of the marker does not reliably indicate that RL induced a reasoning change, using marker counts as a proxy for reasoning quality, or using LLM-as-a-judge scores that reward visible backtracking, measures the fingerprint of the training data rather than the competence of the model. That is a claim about how the field evaluates reasoning models, not just about one word, and it lands hardest on the many papers that cite wait usage or similar verbal markers as evidence that RL trained genuine self-correction.

The paper also ships two reusable resources that extend its value beyond the specific finding. The provenance map covers the one hundred twenty model endpoints, with their lineages and marker indices, and it is the artifact a future model team can use to locate, in their own training history, the stage that installed the marker in their system. The blind revision audits are a sixty-sentence set of traces with expert consensus labels, and they carry the paper's sharpest methodological result: one of two LLM judges, asked to read reflective behavior on those audited sentences, reverses the human-labeled direction of the revision rate along the RL-Zero line. That is the evidence behind the caution against LLM-as-a-judge revision metrics, and it is concrete rather than theoretical: the automated judge did not just add noise, it pointed the wrong way. Both resources matter because the paper's methodological point is about a measurement practice the field uses widely, and the reusable artifacts are what make the correction actionable rather than merely descriptive.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the core provenance finding, that wait is inherited from training data rather than coined by RL, because it rests on label-free statistics across many endpoints and the zero-RL result is the kind of negative result that is hard to fake: the marker simply does not appear where the data does not. I would re-verify two things. First, the scope is a specific family of open models, OLMo-3, MiMo, and SmolLM3, with one hundred twenty endpoints, and the conclusion that RL does not coin the marker is a statement about this ecosystem and these recipes; I would want it tested on closed-model reasoning traces where the training data is unknown, because that is where the RL-coining story has the most traction, and the paper cannot reach there. Second, the injected-error test is the instrument that would connect the marker to actual error-detection behavior, and I would want that result broken down by model, because a marker that tracks injected errors in one recipe and not another is a very different object from a uniform habit, and the frequency statistics alone, which the paper is careful to flag as insufficient, could flatten that distinction. The practical value of the paper is not a new method but a corrected mental model: the backtracking habit you are seeing in a reasoning trace is mostly a fingerprint of the data it was trained on, and that changes how you should interpret it.

Bottom line: Inherited, Not Invented is a careful, label-free provenance study that reframes a popular claim about RL and reasoning: the wait marker is mostly inherited from training data, not invented by RL, and the zero-RL result showing large accuracy gains without the marker is the strongest single finding. Before generalizing beyond the open OLMo-3, MiMo, and SmolLM3 family, I would test the claim on closed-model traces and read the injected-error results per recipe, because the distinction between a real diagnostic marker and an inherited habit is exactly what those two instruments are built to separate.
