SymDiag: Explainable Diagnosis for LLM Reasoning via Neuro-Symbolic
Verification, by Wenyao Cui, Huaping Zhang, Yongyi Huang, and a co-author group
including Chunxiao Gao and Baohua Zhang, from the Beijing Institute of
Technology, Zhongguancun Academy, and the Institute of Automation at the
Chinese Academy of Sciences. This is a KDD 2026 paper.

The problem it sets up is one that has been under-appreciated in the
verification literature. A language model can produce a chain of thought whose
final answer is correct but whose intermediate steps are not, the derivation
happens to land on the right number even though a step in the middle is
inconsistent, skips a premise, or quietly changes the quantity it is reasoning
about. The standard verification signals all miss this, and the paper's first
section is really a taxonomy of why they miss it. Answer matching only sees the
outcome, so it cannot tell you which step broke. LLM-as-judge produces
natural-language critiques that are subjective and hard to audit, you cannot
re-run them and get the same answer, and they are not grounded in anything
checkable. Reward models and process reward models reduce the whole failure
picture to a single scalar score, which tells you the derivation is bad but not
where, and more importantly not why. The gap the paper targets is the distance
between outcome correctness and process validity, and it is a real barrier in
any setting where a wrong-but-plausible derivation is worse than no derivation,
which is basically every high-stakes deployment.

The proposal is SymDiag, and the core move is to reframe verification as a
diagnosis problem with a specific structure. The system takes the natural-
language chain of thought and translates it into symbolic constraints, a
formal encoding of what each step claims and what it depends on, and then it
runs step-level satisfiability and entailment checks. These are the two
operations that make it a diagnosis rather than a score. A satisfiability check
on a step tells you whether the step's claims are mutually consistent, and an
entailment check tells you whether the step actually follows from its stated
premises. Together, they let the system do two things that the scalar-reward
approaches cannot. First, localize the failing step, so the output is not just
"this derivation is unfaithful" but "step four does not follow from steps one
through three." Second, produce verifiable diagnostic evidence, which means
concrete artifacts a human or a repair loop can act on: a counterexample to the
step, an inconsistency witness showing exactly which two claims contradict, or a
missing-premise indicator showing which assumption was silently dropped.

The central technical difficulty, and the part the paper spends real effort on,
is that the symbolic encoding is itself a neural-to-symbolic translation, and
that translation can introduce its own errors. So when a step looks like a
logic violation, it might be a genuine reasoning defect, or it might be an
artifact of the translator mis-encoding the natural language. The system
distinguishes the two with a self-auditor that encodes each step in two
different symbolic forms and checks whether the two encodings agree. If the
violation appears in both encodings, it is more likely a real reasoning error;
if it appears in only one, it is more likely a translation error that should not
be reported as a diagnosis. That disentanglement, between translation error and
reasoning error, is what lets the system give a diagnosis under partial
observability, meaning under the realistic condition that the symbolic layer is
itself imperfect. That is a more honest framing than most neuro-symbolic
verification work, which tends to assume the symbolic side is clean.

The evaluation is across a range of benchmarks: mathematical, logical,
scientific, and general reasoning tasks. As a fourth contribution, the paper
constructs a unified diagnostic dataset that spans these domains, with
mathematics represented by benchmarks such as AIME, and uses it as the common
substrate for comparing SymDiag against the alternative verification
approaches. Having a single dataset across domains is important because it
lets the reader see whether the diagnosis mechanism transfers, or whether it
only works on the kind of derivation the authors happened to encode most
carefully. The claim is that SymDiag improves
detection of unfaithful reasoning compared to outcome-only verification and
LLM-based judging, and more importantly that it gives substantially more
effective feedback for multi-round reasoning repair, which is the practical
payoff. The repair framing is the one that matters for deployment: it is not
enough to flag that a derivation is wrong, the system has to give the model
something it can use to fix the specific step, and the diagnostic evidence, the
counterexample, the witness, the missing premise, is exactly the kind of signal
a repair loop can condition on.

The architecture is also worth describing in terms of what it does not do.
SymDiag is not a new reward model, and it is not a new judge; it is a
diagnostic layer that sits on top of the chain of thought and produces a
structured output. That structured output, a list of step indices with a
verdict for each, a reason for the verdict, and the concrete evidence that
supports it, is the unit of communication between the verifier and anything
that consumes it. A human reader can audit that output against the original
derivation. A repair loop can take the failing step indices and the evidence
and ask the model to redo just those steps. A training pipeline can use the
step-level verdicts as a denser supervision signal than a single end-of-chain
reward. The paper frames the third use, step-level supervision, as a natural
extension but does not fully develop it, which is a gap I would flag as the
most promising future direction, because the diagnostic output is already in a
form that could be used directly for process supervision.

Now the skeptical read. The whole system inherits the quality of the neural-to-
symbolic translation, and the dual-encoding self-auditor is a mitigation, not an
elimination. If both encodings make the same mistake in the same way, which is
entirely possible if the translator has a systematic bias on a class of
statements, the self-auditor will classify a translation error as a reasoning
error and report a diagnosis that is wrong. The paper does not, from what I can
tell, report the rate at which that misclassification happens, and that is the
number that determines whether the system is trustworthy in a deployment rather
than a research demonstration. The step-level entailment checks also depend on
how fine the symbolic encoding of each natural-language step is; if the encoding
is too coarse, an entailment failure might just mean the encoder lost a detail,
not that the model's step was invalid. And the repair gains are the most
consequential claim, because they are what turn this from a diagnostic tool into
a training or inference-time component, so the question a reader should ask is
whether the repair improvement is measured on the same model that produced the
original derivation, and whether the gain survives when the repair loop is run
for more than one round, which is where feedback quality usually degrades.

Bottom line: this is a conceptually clean reframing of reasoning verification,
from scoring to structured diagnosis, with the symbolic entailment machinery
giving it a localization and an evidence mechanism that scalar-reward approaches
fundamentally cannot provide. The open question is whether the self-auditor's
disentanglement of translation error from reasoning error holds up at the rates
that a deployment would require, and whether the repair gains are robust beyond
the first repair round. The unified diagnostic dataset is the right choice for
making the comparison fair, but it also means the result is only as strong as
the encoding of each domain, and a domain that the symbolic encoder handles
poorly will look like the model's reasoning is worse than it actually is, which
is a confound a reader should check for before generalizing the detection
improvement across all four task families.
