Truncate or Not: A Scaling Study of Looped Transformers with Truncated Backpropagation, by Xuyuan Xiong, Siyu Chen, and Zihan Shao, from MMLab at the University of Hong Kong and Yale University, with the remaining authors, including the two corresponding authors, spanning the same two institutions. This is a scaling-law paper, and its whole contribution is a single fitted law that tells you, given a compute budget, whether you should loop your transformer and, if you do, whether you should truncate the backpropagation through the loops.

The setup is that a looped transformer reuses a shared block of layers to increase computational depth without increasing the number of trainable parameters. You run the same block T times, and the effective depth is T times the block depth, but the weights are shared, so the parameter count stays fixed. The standard training approach is backpropagation through time, and the standard trick to save compute is to truncate that backpropagation to the last few loops, because the earlier loops contribute less to the final loss. The problem the paper tackles is that truncation gives you biased gradients, and it is not obvious that the compute you save is worth the bias you introduce. The question, stated plainly, is whether a fully-trained loop with fewer iterations beats a truncated loop with more iterations at the same compute. The answer is not obvious, because truncation saves compute on the backward pass, which is the expensive side of training, and the bias it introduces is hard to measure without running the full comparison, which is exactly what the paper does at a scale no prior study has.

The study is large and systematic, and the scale is the thing that makes it credible. They run nearly six hundred training runs, spanning model sizes from fifty eight million to nine hundred nineteen million non-embedding parameters, over three orders of magnitude in training tokens per parameter, and up to sixteen forward loops. They independently vary the forward loop count T and the backward loop count B, the number of loops you actually backpropagate through, and they fit a scaling law that captures the effect of looping and truncation through a quantity they call the effective parameter count. The architecture is a standard transformer with a recurrence map, and they use a Muon-style optimizer for the recurrent weights and AdamW for the embedding, output, and normalization parameters, trained on the one hundred billion token FineWeb-Edu corpus. The fitting set is two hundred thirty configurations, and they report the fit quality with root-mean-square and mean-absolute error, which is the right level of rigor for a scaling-law claim. The runs are not a single uniform grid, they are a set of targeted sweeps where, for a fixed compute budget, they vary how that budget is split across the forward loop count, the backward loop count, the model size, and the number of training tokens, which is exactly the four-way decomposition you need both to fit the law and to run the matched-compute comparisons that are the paper's main claim.

The law itself has three coefficients, c, p, and q, that capture how the loss depends on the forward and backward loop counts, and the key result is that it decomposes the question of how many parameters a loop setting is worth into a clean form. The headline finding is that the fitted law favors full backpropagation, meaning B equals T, when the forward and backward loop counts are jointly optimized. That is the counterintuitive result, because the whole point of truncation is to save compute, and the law says that the compute you save by truncating is not worth the biased gradients you get. In matched-compute runs, full backpropagation through fewer loops consistently outperforms truncated settings at the same compute. The law also says that at fixed model size, both the compute-optimal loop count and the training-token budget grow with compute, which means that as you scale up, you should loop more and train for longer, not loop more and train for less.

The concrete numbers are where the result becomes actionable. When the model size is also free, the law identifies two fully-trained loops, meaning T is two and B is two, as the best looped setting. That setting, which they report as three hundred forty four million parameters, beats the compute-optimal non-looped model, which is four hundred seventy one million parameters, on validation loss, with twenty seven percent fewer non-embedding parameters and only eight percent more training compute. In nats, the two-loop model reaches two point three six versus two point three seven for the non-looped model, which is a small but consistent gap, and the parameter savings are the bigger win. The law also predicts specific compute savings at fixed loop counts: at T is four, truncation saves five to eleven percent compute at equal loss, and at T is eight, it saves thirty three to thirty five percent, but those are the settings where the backward count is held fixed, and once you are allowed to choose the forward count freely, the full-backpropagation setting beats the truncated one on compute per effective parameter. The intuition behind the result is that truncation is a heuristic that works when the loop count is large and you are forced to use a fixed number of iterations, but as soon as the forward count becomes a free variable you can trade it against the backward count, and the law says the cheapest way to get the effective depth you want is to use fewer loops and backpropagate through all of them, rather than many loops with a short truncated window.

Here is the skeptical read, and for a scaling-law paper the read is about the extrapolation range. The runs go up to sixteen forward loops and nine hundred nineteen million parameters, and the law is a fit to that range, so the recommendation of two fully-trained loops is a result at the low end of the compute range they studied, and I would want to see it hold at the high end before trusting it for a frontier-scale model. The gap between the two-loop model and the non-looped model is two point three six versus two point three seven nats, which is small enough that it could be within run-to-run variance, and the paper relies on the parameter-count difference, twenty seven percent fewer, to make the result matter, which is a fair argument but it means the loss advantage is not the headline, the parameter efficiency is. The effective-parameter-count abstraction is the core of the law, and it is a modeling choice, and the three-coefficient form is the simplest one that fits, which is good, but it is also the simplest one they tried, and I would want to see whether a four-coefficient form fits materially better and changes the recommendation. The training is on a one hundred billion token educational corpus, so the result is a statement about that data distribution, and whether it transfers to a different corpus, say a code-heavy or a multilingual mix, is not tested. The optimizer choice, Muon for the recurrent weights, is a specific one, and the law is fitted with that optimizer, so the recommendation is coupled to an optimizer that is not the default in most training stacks.

The bottom line: this is a clean, well-scoped scaling study that answers a real practical question, should you truncate your backpropagation through loops, with a fitted law that says no, at least when you are allowed to choose the forward and backward counts jointly, and the actionable result is that two fully-trained loops beat a non-looped model at the same compute with a quarter fewer parameters. The caveats are that the loss gap is small and the parameter efficiency is the real win, the result is at the low end of the compute range studied, and the law is fitted to a specific corpus and a specific optimizer. If you are considering a looped architecture for a parameter-efficient model, this is the paper to read before you decide on your truncation strategy, and the effective-parameter-count framing is a useful way to think about the trade-off even if you do not adopt the exact two-loop recommendation.
