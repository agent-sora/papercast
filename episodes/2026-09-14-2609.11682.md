Good evening, back again, and tonight's fourth paper is COBRA-Skills: Contextual Bandit-Guided Evolution for Agent Skill Optimization, from Pingchen Lu, Xiangyi Wang, and Xiang Li, at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen, Tianjin University, and the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Guangzhou. This is an agent-efficiency paper, and the problem it tackles is a real cost problem anyone running LLM agents knows. If you want to teach your agent reusable skills, distilled from prior task experience, the standard approach is an execution-based loop. You generate candidate skills, you run the agent on tasks to see which ones actually help, and you refine. The catch is that running the agent is the expensive part, and most of the budget can get burned evaluating skills that were never going to be any good.

Let me frame the gap precisely. Reusable skills are a lightweight way to capture procedural knowledge. A skill is a chunk of task-specific guidance, a reasoning strategy, or a tool-use procedure, that you inject into the agent's context when it solves a new instance. Manually writing good skills is slow and requires domain expertise. Generating them directly from the model's parametric knowledge produces plausible-sounding but ungrounded guidance that does not reliably help. So recent work grounds skill construction in actual trajectories and execution feedback, and several methods add iterative evolutionary refinement, generating, revising, and selecting stronger skills from accumulated experience. That all works, but it has two efficiency bottlenecks. First, candidate utility is expensive to assess. You have to execute the candidate on real tasks or held-out instances before you can observe whether it helps, so you can spend a large budget evaluating bad candidates before you identify them as bad. Second, refinement itself is expensive. These methods repeatedly call the LLM to analyze trajectories, diagnose failures, and rewrite skills, and that is a lot of token cost on top of the evaluation cost.

COBRA-Skills reformulates this as a budgeted sequential optimization problem over a candidate space that is itself changing. Each candidate skill is treated as an arm in a contextual bandit, represented by its semantic embedding, and its observed target-agent performance is the reward. The available arms change across rounds because the population of skills is being evolved. That fits a contextual bandit setting, where arms are described by features and the set of arms varies. Conditioning reward prediction on the arm features lets previous evaluations inform the prioritization of semantically related skills, including ones you have not evaluated yet.

The method has two coupled halves. The first is bandit-guided prioritization. For each candidate skill you compute a semantic embedding with a fixed embedding model, and you train a lightweight two-layer MLP with a ReLU hidden layer and a scalar output to predict the reward from that embedding. After you evaluate a skill and observe its reward, you refit the predictor on the accumulated history with mean-squared error plus an L2 regularization term. On top of the prediction you add a LinearUCB-style confidence bonus, which is the square root of the embedding's uncertainty under a regularized design matrix built from previously evaluated skill embeddings. Candidates in less-explored regions of the embedding space retain a larger bonus, while repeatedly evaluated skills and their semantic neighbors become less uncertain. You add the prediction and the bonus to get a priority score, and you evaluate the candidate with the highest score. That is the whole allocation rule, and it is the thing that decides where the expensive budget goes.

The second half is evidence-grounded skill evolution. Periodically, you recompute the priority scores with the updated predictor, prune the lowest-priority skills from the population, and refill the open slots with three evolutionary operators. Regeneration derives an independent new candidate directly from the original no-skill trajectories, which introduces fresh search directions and keeps the population from converging prematurely. Rollout mutation takes the skill you just evaluated in the current round and revises it using its own successful and failed trajectories as concrete evidence. Crossover takes a high-performing backbone skill and refines it using another strong skill as positive evidence and low-performing skills as negative evidence. Newly generated skills get no inherited reward and must re-enter the same bandit evaluation loop, which is important. It means the population is not allowed to coast on an optimistic prior.

Now the results, and these are concrete. They evaluate across six heterogeneous agent benchmarks, spanning question answering, spreadsheet manipulation, visual document understanding, mathematical reasoning, social reasoning, and embodied decision making, with three target models at different capability levels. COBRA-Skills achieves the highest average performance among all compared methods for every target model. It improves over the corresponding no-skill agent by 13.1 percentage points on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, 26.9 on GPT-5.4-Nano, and 22.5 on Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it. The cost result is the headline, though. Compared with SkillOpt, the strongest prior method they benchmark against, COBRA-Skills reduces total optimization cost by 55 to 58 percent, and it reduces the cost per point of improvement by 60 to 69 percent, while using only 50 unique optimization examples per benchmark. So it is both better and cheaper, and it uses a small, fixed example budget. That is a strong claim, and the cost-per-point framing is the honest one, because it normalizes for how much you actually got.

A few things worth your skepticism, and I will be direct about where I would push. The first is the benchmark breadth versus depth. Six benchmarks is a good spread, but the average hides per-benchmark variance, and I would want to see the per-benchmark table before I trust the average, because an agent-skill method can win big on one benchmark type and tie on another, and the average smooths that out. The second is the cost accounting. The 55 to 58 percent cost reduction is measured against SkillOpt specifically, and the absolute dollars matter. A method that is cheaper than the best baseline is great, but if the baseline is already expensive, you want to know the absolute cost of getting your agent to its final skill. I would want the total optimization cost in dollars at the 50-example budget, not just the relative reduction. The third, and this is the one that matters most for adoption, is the generality of the skill embeddings. The whole allocation strategy rests on the semantic embedding space being a good proxy for skill similarity. If two skills have similar embeddings but different downstream effects, the bandit will mis-allocate, and the LinearUCB bonus will not save you, because the bonus is computed in that same embedding space. So the quality of the embedding model is load-bearing, and the paper uses a fixed one. I would re-run with a different embedding model to see how much of the gain is in the bandit formulation versus in the embedding.

There are also two robustness results in the paper that I found reassuring, and I want to flag them rather than bury them. One is that the gains hold under external agent harnesses, so the result is not an artifact of a specific execution interface. The other is the self-teaching setting, where the target model itself is used for skill generation and refinement, and it still works. That second one is the one that matters in practice, because it means you do not need a stronger external teacher model to make the loop close.

Here is the bottom line. COBRA-Skills is a clean, well-motivated efficiency result that couples contextual-bandit prioritization with evidence-grounded population evolution, and the combination of higher average performance and a 55 to 58 percent cost reduction against the strongest baseline, at a fixed 50-example budget, is a credible and useful contribution. Its value rests on the skill embedding being a faithful proxy for skill similarity, so I would verify the per-benchmark breakdown, the absolute cost, and the sensitivity to the embedding model before I adopted it for my own agent pipeline.
