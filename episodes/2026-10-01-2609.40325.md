WorldAuditBench. The first three authors are Ziyan Jiang, Jingbo Yang, and Jiabao Ji, working between UC Santa Barbara, MIT CSAIL, and the MIT-IBM Watson AI Lab, with Tommi Jaakkola, Yang Zhang, and Shiyu Chang on the byline.

The task is auditing interactive 3D worlds for anomalies. A simulated environment can look convincing while containing defects: a chair floating above the floor, a solid wall that you can walk through, an object that disappears when you look away. These defects matter because agents are increasingly trained and evaluated inside such worlds, and an agent that succeeds by walking through a broken wall should not count as a reliable navigator. Auditing is hard because it couples two capabilities that are usually studied separately. Action, for navigating the world and searching systematically under a limited budget. And visual reasoning, for identifying anomalies from observations. The coupling is the point: an observation may suggest a possible anomaly without confirming it, and the agent has to decide which action would gather the decisive evidence.

The benchmark has 213 anomaly tasks across 13 environments, built with Unreal Engine 5 and Three.js, spanning five anomaly families organized into a taxonomy of 15 categories. The evaluation fixes the exploration budget, which is the right call for this task, because success under unlimited exploration is not very informative. They evaluate five frontier models under two paradigms. The two-stage paradigm uses a vision-language-action model, Open-P2P 1.2B, to collect 60 seconds of simulated exploration, with observations sampled every half a second, and then a vision-language model audits the recorded trajectory. The end-to-end paradigm runs a single VLM agent in which visual reasoning directly guides action selection step by step.

The headline result: success rates range from 6.6 percent to 42.3 percent across the evaluated models and both paradigms, against a human success rate of 83.4 percent. That is a large gap, and it is the paper's central finding. The end-to-end VLM agents do better than the two-stage pipeline, at 28.2 to 42.3 percent, compared with 6.6 to 17.4 percent for the VLA-then-VLM auditors. The best model is GPT-6 Astra at 42.3 percent, followed by Gemini 3.8 Flash at 32.4, Claude Opus 5 at 28.2, Muse Spark 1.3 at 15.0, and Qwen 3.8 Flash at 8.5.
The model-to-harness mapping is specific: GPT-6 Astra runs with Codex CLI, Claude Opus 5 with Claude Code, Gemini 3.8 Flash with Gemini CLI, Muse Spark 1.3 with OpenCode, and Qwen 3.8 Flash with Qwen Code. The harnesses manage model calls and conversation history, so the numbers are a model-plus-harness result, which is the honest way to report agent benchmarks, though it does mean part of the gap between models is harness quality.

The construction pipeline is worth a sentence because it affects how much you can trust the negatives. The environments are built in two engines, Unreal Engine 5 and Three.js, so the benchmark spans both heavyweight and web-based scene stacks. Anomalies are planted against an otherwise-consistent scene, so each task has a known ground-truth defect, which is what makes the complete-discovery and anchor-discovery metrics well defined. The five anomaly families correspond to different failure modes of scene generation, and the 15 categories under them are granular enough that a future model could be reported per category rather than only in aggregate. That structure is what makes the benchmark extensible, and it is also why the per-family sample counts are the first thing to check before reusing the aggregate numbers.

The breakdown by anomaly family is where the difficulty concentrates. Static physics anomalies, the floating-object style defects, are the relatively easy cases. The hardest family sits at or below 12.5 percent success rate for every model and every paradigm, and that family is the one requiring the agent to actively test behavior over time rather than inspect a single frame. The budget analysis quantifies the exploration problem: the default budget corresponds to 40 steps for the VLM agent and 60 seconds for the VLA pipeline. At half budget, success drops from 33.3 to 23.0 percent for the VLM agent and from 5.6 to 4.0 for the VLA-VLM auditor. At 1.5 times budget, it rises to 34.9 and 7.1. The VLM agent is roughly budget-saturated around the default, while the two-stage pipeline keeps spending budget it cannot use, which is part of why its ceiling is so much lower.

The multi-anomaly results show the failure mode is search, not recognition. With one or two anomalies planted, anchor success, finding at least one, is 42.9 percent, and with three it is 47.6. But complete discovery, finding every planted anomaly, collapses: the agent identifies all targets in only 3 of 42 two-anomaly runs, 7.1 percent, and 2 of 42 three-anomaly runs, 4.8 percent, with target recall around 29.8 and 29.4 percent. The agents can find a defect when they stumble on it, but they cannot systematically sweep a large space under a fixed budget. There is also a cost trade-off between the paradigms: given prerecorded trajectories, the VLA-VLM analysis is 2.7 to 10.3 times faster than interactive VLM auditing, with lower inference cost, at the price of the much lower success rate.
The remaining ablations, run with Gemini 3.8 Flash on the 126 Unreal Engine tasks, isolate the ingredients. Moving the starting position closer to the anomaly, reducing the route distance to about a third, lifts VLM success by 4.8 points and the VLA-VLM auditor by 1.6, which is a direct measurement of how much of the task is navigation rather than perception. Removing in-context examples from the task prompt costs 2.4 points for the VLM agent and nothing for the two-stage pipeline, and removing both the examples and the anomaly-type hint costs 17.5 points for the VLM agent, from 33.3 to 15.9, and 3.2 for the auditor. The two-stage pipeline is largely insensitive to prompt guidance because its analysis stage is a fixed offline procedure, while the interactive agent is doing most of its reasoning in the prompt.

What I would trust here. This is a well-built benchmark: the fixed exploration budget, the two paradigms, the anomaly taxonomy, and the per-factor ablations together give you a usable decomposition of why current agents fail at world auditing. The finding that the gap is in search, not recognition, is supported by the complete-discovery collapse and the starting-distance effect, and it is the kind of result that tells a world-builder where to invest. The human baseline of 83.4 percent is also appropriately measured, leaving a gap that is large but not absurd.

What I would re-verify before adopting this. The five models are each locked to one harness, so the cross-model ranking conflates model capability with harness quality, and the 34-point spread between the best and worst VLM agents could shrink or reorder under a better harness for the weaker models. The 213 tasks across 13 environments is a reasonable size, but the per-family sample counts matter for the family-level conclusions, and the hardest family at or below 12.5 percent across the board is the one I would want to see expanded, since it is where the benchmark is currently least discriminative. The multi-anomaly runs, only 42 each, are too few to make strong claims about complete-discovery rates, and the budget scaling was measured at three points on one model, so the saturation claim for the VLM agent is suggestive rather than established. The benchmark is a preprint without a released leaderboards infrastructure that I could verify, so before building an evaluation loop on top of it I would want the task splits, the anomaly-placement procedure, and the human-rating protocol in their full form.

Bottom line. WorldAuditBench is a clean, well-decomposed benchmark for a real gap in embodied AI, and its central result, that multimodal agents can recognize anomalies they encounter but cannot systematically search for them under a budget, is well supported by the ablations. The practical takeaway for anyone using interactive 3D worlds as testbeds: until the search problem is solved, your world is only as trustworthy as your manual audit of it.
