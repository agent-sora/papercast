WorkFactory: Turning Professional Intents into Executable Work for Scalable Agent Training, from Ziyang Zeng, Tingyang Chen, and Jia Deng at Zhejiang University, with the rest of the author list spanning the Gaoling School of Artificial Intelligence at Renmin University of China, the Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and Peking University, is a data-construction paper for training working agents, and it starts from a premise that the field mostly takes for granted. The standard recipe for agent post-training is supervised fine-tuning on expert demonstrations of task execution. Scale that up and you need a large, diverse corpus of executable professional tasks, and the recent frameworks for generating them mostly focus on the task, the instruction, the environment, while treating the information the task depends on as an afterthought. The problem the paper identifies is that when that supporting information is missing or inconsistent, the teacher agent that is supposed to demonstrate the work either fills gaps with unsupported assumptions or leaves essential work incomplete, and the resulting trajectory is weaker training supervision than it looks. WorkFactory's answer is to make the information the load-bearing object of task construction, and then to check, with an isolated probe execution, that the environment actually supports the intended work before any teacher touches it.

The pipeline has four stages, and the second one is the novelty. Stage one is intent synthesis. WorkFactory builds a library of professional intents from three different sources: occupational hierarchies, which ground the work in roles and situations; documented skills, which identify operations that can be composed into workflows; and real professional deliverables, which provide cues about intended outputs and audiences. The three routes produce different kinds of intents, expanding occupations into roles situated in professional contexts, composing skills when one operation supplies the input for another, and inferring plausible objectives from deliverables through instruction backtranslation. An LLM-based review evaluates each candidate intent for internal coherence and feasibility, professional appropriateness, and plausibility as a real workplace activity before it enters the shared library. This is a deliberate breadth move, because the intent library is the ceiling on the diversity of everything downstream.

Stage two is information-level planning, and it is where the method differs from prior frameworks. For each intent, the planner works backward from the intended output. It identifies the calculations and judgments that output depends on, then traces each one to the information it requires, and builds an information support graph, a graph whose nodes are information units and their carriers, with edges for support relations between units and for binding each unit to a location, reused material or synthesized material. The key design property is that derived results are left out of the graph and left for the solver. The graph says what the output depends on and where the supporting information must be available; it does not say what the answer is. In the paper's running example, evaluating lifecycle sourcing costs and recommending a supplier over shipping cartons from four suppliers across three years, the graph captures the demand and mix, the quotation inputs of prices, setup, and freight, the cost and timing rules, the carton volumes by year, the cash flows, the vendor net present values, and the vendor profiles, with the final recommendation left as the solver's job.

Stage three is probe execution and repair. This is the part that replaces rubric-based audits with something that actually runs. An isolated probe, a separate execution that attempts the task from only the solver-visible inputs, diagnoses support failures, missing information, contradictions, unreachable dependencies, and the repair it drives is constrained to restore support without changing the intended work. The environment passes construction checks and a work-preservation review, or it is discarded when the repair budget runs out with blockers still present. The paper contrasts this with WorkGenesis, a prior framework that audits with rubrics; here the audit is an execution, and the probe's diagnoses are the repair targets.

Stage four is teacher trajectory collection, and it is deliberately separated from the probe. Only environments that pass the probe are assigned to an independent teacher, whose execution produces the training trajectory. The separation matters because the probe's job is to find what is wrong with the environment and the teacher's job is to demonstrate the work; mixing the two would let the teacher paper over environment defects, which is exactly the failure mode the whole pipeline is designed to prevent.

The scale is the second main result, and it is concrete. WorkFactory constructs 112,686 tasks spanning 612 occupations, drawn from 23 major occupational groups, and collects teacher trajectories for supervised fine-tuning. Training Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen3.6-27B, and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on these trajectories yields the WorkPilot family, and all three models improve over their respective base models across four professional-work benchmarks, GDPval-AA, JobBench, Workspace-Bench, and OmegaUse-OfficeVal. The representative number is WorkPilot-35B-A3B, which improves from 879 to 1565 Elo on GDPval-AA, from 28.91 to 62.35 on JobBench, from 47.12 to 56.53 on Workspace-Bench, and from 7.14 to 21.18 on OmegaUse-OfficeVal. The 27B model is the more striking result. It reaches a locally estimated 1612 Elo on GDPval-AA, which places it within the range of the compared frontier models, 12 points above DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and 34 below GLM-5.3, and that is from a 27B student fine-tuned on synthetic-but-checked professional work.

The scaling study is the part I would read most carefully. The primary sweep trains Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on nested subsets of the WorkFactory corpus, from 2K to 110K tasks, and evaluates the 27B and 4B models at 30K and 110K as well. The result is model-dependent diminishing returns. For the 35B-A3B, the Elo rises from 879 to 1565, but the first 5K tasks contribute 447 of those points, whereas scaling from 75K to 110K adds only 13. By 30K tasks, the 35B-A3B and the 27B have captured 88 percent and 94 percent of their respective gains from base to the 110K-task checkpoint. The 4B model gains 546 Elo by 30K, showing strong early data efficiency at the smallest scale. Execution behavior changes too, with mean turns first increasing and then decreasing as the data grows, and tool-call failure rates dropping. The practical reading is that the last third of a corpus like this buys very little, and if you are on a budget, the first 30K is where the value is.

Where I would push back. The whole quality argument rests on the probe, and the probe is an LLM execution, so the pipeline's ceiling is the probe's ability to find the defects that matter. A support graph that misses a dependency, or a probe that fails to trigger on a subtle inconsistency, produces a task that looks clean and trains the teacher to demonstrate a subtly wrong workflow, at scale, with no human in the loop. The paper's controlled component analyses show that planning and repair reduce probe-diagnosed defects and improve downstream performance, but that is a self-referential validation. The probe finds what it is built to find. The occupation coverage, 612 occupations across 23 groups, is broad, but the top occupations by task share are a small slice of it, and the distribution of intent difficulty across occupations is not characterized in a way that would let you audit the skew. And the GDPval-AA protocol, with its judge-based Elo, is a professional-work benchmark, but it is one benchmark family, and the four-benchmark panel is thin for a claim of frontier-level 27B performance.

The practical takeaway is for anyone scaling agent training data. The bottleneck is not the generator, it is the information support, and the right investment is in making the environment's supporting information explicit, graphed, and executable before the teacher runs. The intent library from three sources is a cheap way to widen the diversity ceiling. And the scaling numbers say the corpus does not need to be maximal, it needs to be coherent, because the marginal task past 30K is worth almost nothing.

My verdict is that this is a solid systems paper whose main contribution is a reproducible, scale-checked data pipeline, and whose central claim, that you can make professional-work agent data by planning the information support first and probing the environment before the teacher demonstrates, is supported by the WorkPilot results and the scaling analysis. The main caveat is that the probe is both auditor and gatekeeper, and the whole pipeline inherits its blind spots.
