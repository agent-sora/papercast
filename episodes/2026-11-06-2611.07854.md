Towards Foundation Decision Models, Drex, is a technical report from the Nace.AI Research Team. The byline is a research team rather than a single institution, and the individual authors listed are Nischay Dhankhar, Alisher Amantay, and Zhanibek Datbayev among others. The paper introduces Drex, a family of four open decision models, and the central claim is that for a large class of production tasks, you do not need autoregressive generation at all.

The problem is a production one, not a research curiosity. Autoregressive large language models, with long chain-of-thought reasoning, have become the default because they reach high accuracy on a wide range of tasks. But in production they give inconsistent answers, they require you to parse free-text output, and they add cost and latency. The paper frames this as a resurgence of discriminative inference in the form of decision models. A decision model takes a question and a set of options supplied by the user, and returns a probability distribution over those options without any autoregressive decoding. The options can be a multiple-choice set, a yes-no judgment, or a score on an ordered scale. The state of the request is a document, a record, or an agent context, optionally with images, and the questions are about that state. The paper's claim is that decision models match frontier large models on well-defined tasks at a fraction of the cost.

The four models differ in size and architecture. There is an eight-billion-parameter model called DLM, a nine-billion model called version one point five, a thirty-five-billion model with three billion active parameters called version two point zero, and a twenty-seven-billion model called Max. The largest, Max, is the one the report benchmarks hardest, and it is described as competitive with frontier models on real-data decisions while remaining fast, calibrated, and stable under reordering of the options. That last property, stability under option reordering, is the one that is hardest to achieve and the one that matters most for a decision model, because if your answer changes when you shuffle the order of the choices, the model has not actually learned to evaluate the options, it has learned a positional bias.

The training is supervised fine-tuning combined with reinforcement learning from adjusted feedback, which the paper abbreviates RLAF, plus vision tuning for Max. The controlled ablations show that RLAF improves both accuracy and consistency, which is the specific claim that the reinforcement stage is doing real work beyond what the supervised stage already achieved. The readout head for Max is worth understanding because it is the mechanism behind the option-reordering claim. Each question is encoded as a sequence of images, the state, the instruction, the options, and a final answer marker. Each option is preceded by a single-token option code, from A through Z and then AA and on, and the answer is read from the hidden state at the answer marker position. A bias-free linear readout maps that hidden state to two hundred and fifty five logits, one per option code, and only the logits for the options actually present in the question are kept, with a temperature near one point zero zero nine fitted on held-out records. The readout head has about 1.3 million parameters. The comparison the paper draws is with a pointer head, where each option is represented by its own hidden state, and the readout head deliberately sidesteps the symbol-binding problem of multiple-choice prompting by identifying options by code, which is what makes the reordering robustness a learned property of the backbone rather than an accident of the head.

The benchmark numbers are the part to hold the report to. On Decision Index zero point four one, Max sets a new state of the art among the decision models compared, scoring sixty-seven point five two on the public suite versus sixty point seven zero for Jev one point one four zero. On the independently run Artificial Analysis Decision Index, it has the second-highest score among decision models, sixty-four, against sixty-five for the three leading models, which is a close result and an honest one. On Decision Bench v4, Max beats Jev one point one four and the other Drex models, is statistically tied with d3-turbo and GPT-6 Sol, and trails Claude Opus 5.5 by 1.6 points. It has the highest accuracy among decision models on DecideBench and is competitive with the top model on JevBench-231. The latency is the other headline: Max answers a typical request in 183 milliseconds, against about two seconds for GPT-6 Sol and 3.5 seconds for Claude Opus 5.5. That is roughly a ten-to-fifteen-fold speed difference, and for a decision task that is called many times per user session, it is the number that justifies the whole category.

The licensing is worth noting for an open release. Max, version two point zero, and version one point five are released under the Nace.AI Open RAIL-M license, with Max on a modified version one point zero, while DLM is under the CC BY-NC 4.0 license of its backbone, and the repository code is Apache 2.0. The three larger models are also served through a hosted API and OpenRouter. So it is a genuinely open release with weights and inference code, but the largest model is under a custom license rather than a permissive one, which matters if you are considering it for a commercial product. The fact that all four are open, with the smaller DLM under a permissive CC license, means you can prototype the decision-model interface on the eight-billion model and only face the custom-license question when you scale up to Max, a reasonable on-ramp for a team still deciding whether the category fits its tasks.

A skeptical read has a few things I would verify. The decision-model framing is a real category with a real cost-latency advantage, but it is scoped to well-defined tasks with user-supplied options, and that scoping is doing a lot of work. The moment your options are not well-formed, or the right answer is not in the set the user supplied, a decision model has no graceful fallback the way an autoregressive model does, because it is constrained to the option set by construction. The benchmark comparisons are against a specific set of decision models and a couple of frontier autoregressive models, and the tie with d3-turbo and GPT-6 Sol and the small trail to Claude Opus 5.5 are the honest framing, but I would want to see the variance across the decision tasks, because decision benchmarks can be dominated by a handful of task types, and a model that is calibrated on those specific types may not transfer. The RLAF ablation is the methodological contribution, and I would want to see the adjusted-feedback mechanism in detail, because the difference between RLAF and standard RLHF is in how the feedback is adjusted, and that is the part most likely to be a non-trivial design choice.

The engineering gotcha for adopting this is the option set. The entire interface is you-supplied-options-in, probability-distribution-out, which means the quality of the options you construct is now a first-class input to the system, not a detail. A decision model will confidently return a distribution over bad options, and the readout head is optimized to be stable over the options you give it, not to tell you when the options are wrong. If you are moving a production decision task from an autoregressive model to Drex, the option-construction layer is the new component you own, and it is where the failure modes will live.

Bottom line: Drex is a credible open release of a new model category, decision models, with four open sizes, a clean readout design that makes the option-reordering robustness a learned property, and benchmark results that put the largest model at or near the top of the decision-model field with a ten-to-fifteen-fold latency advantage over the frontier autoregressive models it is compared against. The main caveats are that the category is scoped to well-defined tasks with well-formed user-supplied options, the largest model is under a custom license, and the option-construction layer becomes a new failure surface you own if you adopt it.
