Vela: User Behavior as Supervision Sparks LLM Intelligence, by Yang Xiao, Yusong Sun, and Haoyi Wu, from the AllSpark Team. The premise is that large language models have a missing dimension of intelligence: the ability to infer an individual person's preferences, intent, and likely future actions from weeks or years of their actual behavior. Every model is trained on static text or synthetic data, and neither captures how a specific user's behavior evolves over time. Vela's argument is that real user behavior data, already available at scale on content platforms, is a natural supervisory signal, and that converting it into training tasks and trajectories improves not just user-facing capabilities but general benchmarks. The paper is a data and training pipeline paper more than an architecture paper, and it matters because it is a concrete recipe for post-training on a data source most labs cannot replicate but many can approximate.

The data comes from a large social media platform: anonymized behavior records from 100,000 users, each history spanning several weeks to several years. Names, contact details, and account identifiers are removed, and any record containing personally identifiable information is excluded entirely. Each user's events are organized into six categories: views, searches, purchases, posts, follows, and dislikes. The central construction is a cutoff index that splits each behavior sequence into a visible history and a hidden future. Tasks are built from the visible history, and supervision is derived from the hidden future. There are 91 question templates across the six behavior categories and four question types in two families. The predictive family includes objective questions, where the model picks from a candidate list the items the user will actually buy or view in the hidden future, with distractors sampled from other users, and open-prediction questions, which are the same but free-form with no candidate list. The rubric family includes open questions, asking the model to summarize the user's attributes or interests from the visible history, and attribution questions, which show the model one observed future behavior and ask it to explain that behavior from the visible history. These last two are scored against task-specific rubrics, which an LLM composer generates alongside each question.

Task synthesis is where the engineering detail lives. Each user's history is segmented into pieces of 72,000 to 120,000 tokens using the Qwen tokenizer, and within each segment roughly the first two-thirds become the visible history and the rest the hidden future, with complete behavior records kept intact. For one future behavior event you pick either a predictive template or an attribution template, never both, because the attribution question would reveal the prediction target and let the model answer the predictive question without inferring anything. In total this produces 100,000 candidate tasks, which then pass through difficulty-based filtering and trajectory acceptance criteria, retaining about 75,000 tasks with selected training trajectories. Those selected trajectories form two corpora: Vela-Instruct and Vela-RFT. The composer and the scorer both use Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B with role-specific prompts at the highest thinking level, which is worth noting because the pipeline's supervision quality is bound by that model.

Training is fine-tuning of Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, the base model, using the Swift framework. Both variants share identical settings: packed sequence length of 131,072 tokens, two epochs, learning rate of 10 to the minus 5. Vela-35B-A3B is the fine-tune on Vela-Instruct, and Vela-35B-A3B-RFT adds a reinforcement learning stage on Vela-RFT. Evaluation uses three benchmark groups. VelaBench is 982 held-out tasks from the same 91 templates. User-related out-of-distribution benchmarks are PurchaseIntent, ProfileSummary, PersonaBench, and AdRecommendation. General out-of-distribution benchmarks are AIME 2026, GPQA-Diamond, IFBench, CL-bench, and CL-bench Life. The headline: Vela-35B-A3B improves over the base model by 11.5 points on VelaBench, reaching 63.0 against the base's 51.5, and by up to 11.2 points on the user-related out-of-distribution set. On general benchmarks the average gain is 4 points. The model lands at 63.0 on VelaBench, above DeepSeek-V4-Flash at 58.6 and above Qwen3.5-397B-A17B at 55.6, and on AIME 2026 it scores 91.8, slightly behind DeepSeek-V4-Flash at 92.8.

The most interesting result is the reinforcement learning interaction. Running RL from the fine-tuned Vela checkpoint beats running RL from the base model by up to 7.7 points on both VelaBench and ProfileSummary, which suggests the behavior-grounded fine-tune is not just a capability add-on but a better starting point for downstream RL, a claim most data papers do not get to make. There is a scaling result too: training on more user behavior data improves user-related performance. The difficulty-filtering ablation is more sobering. At 37,500 training examples, removing difficulty-based filtering changes VelaBench by zero and the out-of-distribution user benchmarks by at most about a point, which means the filtering contributes little at that scale and the gains come from the data itself.


The scoring mechanics are worth laying out because they define what the model is actually being trained to do. Predictive questions compare predicted answers against ground-truth sets derived from the hidden future. Objective questions use exact matching of selected candidates. Open-prediction questions go through an LLM-based scorer that extracts the predicted items, maps semantically equivalent predictions to their ground-truth counterparts, and computes set F1. Rubric questions are scored by evaluating each answer against a set of task-specific criteria, each in the unit interval, with the question score as the equal-weight mean. The task score is the mean across its k questions. That means a task can be answered well on its easy questions and poorly on the hard ones and still register a middling score, which is one reason the difficulty filter exists even though the ablation shows it barely matters at 37,500 examples. One structural detail keeps the setup honest: for the same future event, predictive and attribution templates are mutually exclusive, because an attribution question that shows the observed behavior and asks for an explanation would leak the answer to the matching predictive question. The paper also positions itself against a large body of user-memory and behavior-prediction work, from P5 to LongMemEval to FineRob, and its distinct contribution is the breadth of task coverage and the use of observed future behavior as the source of ground truth, rather than persona modeling or simulation.


The benchmark table has a few numbers that bear repeating because they frame the transfer claim precisely. On VelaBench the fine-tuned model reaches 63.0 against the base model's 51.5. On ProfileSummary it scores 22.8, behind DeepSeek-V4-Flash at 30.5, which is a reminder that the in-domain VelaBench gain does not transfer evenly across user-facing tasks, and the 11.2-point out-of-distribution improvement is the average over tasks where some are near parity. On IFBench it scores 62.5, below every compared model, while on GPQA-Diamond it reaches 86.9, close to the top. The general-benchmark average gain of 4 points is thus a mixture of small wins on knowledge and reasoning sets and small losses on instruction-following, which is a pattern to check against your own evaluation mix rather than take at face value.

Skeptical read, in three parts. First, the data is the product, and it is proprietary: 100,000 real users of one platform, with the authors promising to release templates, prompts, and code, but not the behavior records. You can replicate the pipeline shape, not the pipeline. Second, the supervision is LLM-scored in places, and the rubric questions in particular inherit the biases of a 2.4-trillion-parameter composer and scorer; the predictive questions are more defensible because their ground truth is observed future behavior. Third, the general-benchmark gains of a few points on a 35B model, while real, are exactly the kind of broad transfer claim that needs replication on your own task distribution before you invest a post-training budget in it. The user-facing gains, especially the RL-from-fine-tune result, are the part I would trust and the part most worth stealing as a design principle.

The bottom line: Vela is a well-specified recipe for converting real user behavior into fine-tuning and RL supervision, with the RL-interaction result being the most transferable finding in the paper. Before adopting it, I would re-verify the out-of-distribution user benchmarks with my own rubrics, because LLM-scored rubric supervision is where the pipeline is most likely to be quietly wrong.
