Video Generation Meets Agent Harness, by Liaoyuan Fan, Yuhao Zheng, and Dongxing Mao, all equal contributors from a twenty-one-author team spanning the National University of Singapore, Nanyang Technological University, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, the University of Science and Technology of China, Central South University, and the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China, is a position paper about where video generation is heading: away from a single end-to-end synthesis call and toward agentic generation, where planning, tool use, and feedback loops produce coherent multi-shot video. The paper's organizing move is to borrow the notion of an agent harness from the LLM-agent world and apply it to video. A harness is the executable runtime that sits around a model: it constructs context, exposes tools, maintains state, routes feedback, and enforces budgets. The report then studies three directions through that lens. First, agentic video generation, where a harness coordinates script and shot planning, asset management, production, and editing. Second, agentic world modeling, where the harness mediates observation, control, and execution, including designs where a game engine or a program written by a coding agent holds the world state while the video model only renders it. Third, and most provocatively, recursive self-improvement for video model development itself, separating revisions of a current output from updates to the harness state, the generator parameters, the evaluators, and the procedure that produces later updates, and tracing the channels through which an improvement could re-enter the development loop.

What makes this worth a listen, as opposed to another survey with a trendier name, is the second direction, agentic world modeling. The observation is that a video world model already does most of what an environment does: it takes actions and produces observations, and it predicts futures. The gap to a real interactive environment is state. Current world models are stateless in the sense that each rollout starts fresh, and the paper argues the fix is structural, not a better video model. Put the state somewhere the model does not have to hold it in latent space: a game engine with deterministic physics, or a program that a coding agent maintains, and let the video model do what it is actually good at, rendering. That decomposition, state in code or in an engine, appearance in the diffusion model, is the most concrete and testable claim in the paper. The third direction, recursive self-improvement, is the part that will age best or worst. The paper is careful to distinguish four different loops that the literature conflates: fixing the current video, updating the harness, updating the model weights, and updating the evaluator that scores videos. Conflating them is why a lot of self-improvement claims in the video literature are unfalsifiable, because it is unclear which loop closed. The paper's contribution on that front is mostly taxonomic, but it is the right taxonomy, and it names the open channels, provenance, cost accounting, and evaluation that links video quality to sustained model improvement, which are the boring questions that will determine whether agentic video is a field or a demo.

The first direction, agentic video production, is the least novel and the most commercially legible. A harness that plans a script, assigns shots, manages assets, produces shots, and edits them is, functionally, a video version of the coding-agent harnesses people have been building for text. The paper's specific contribution is an evaluation argument: when you evaluate such a system, you have to separate the quality of the plan from the quality of the execution from the quality of the final cut, because a beautiful video produced by a bad plan and a bad plan rescued by a strong editor are different failures, and lumping them into one score hides which component to improve. That is a fair point and probably true of every agentic pipeline, but the paper does not operationalize it with a benchmark, which is the gap.

There is a subtler point in the paper that I think underplays itself, about the agent capabilities that video specifically imposes. Text agents have had their capability taxonomy worked out over a few years, planning, tool use, memory, self-correction. The paper argues video changes the weighting of those capabilities in ways that are not obvious. Perception has to be audiovisual and continuous rather than a discrete observation. Planning has to be over long horizons measured in shots rather than function calls. And the memory problem is different, because a multi-shot video has to keep a character, a location, and a physical object consistent across minutes, which is a binding-constraint problem that a text agent never faces in the same form. The paper also singles out interaction through code and computer use as a capability video uniquely demands, because a video agent that wants to change the lighting in shot four has to reach into the engine or the scene graph, not just re-prompt. That capability list is the paper's quiet contribution: it is a checklist of what a video agent must be good at that a text agent is not, and it is the kind of thing that will shape how the next generation of video agent benchmarks are designed. I would push on one of those items, lifelong memory. The paper lists it as an open challenge, but it is arguably the load-bearing one, because a video agent that cannot remember what the world looked like in shot two cannot produce a coherent shot ten, and no amount of planning or tool use fixes a memory that evaporates between shots.

The scope is also worth noting. This is a 73-page report, which tells you it is a survey plus a position, not a method paper. There are no new models, no new training runs, and no headline numbers to check, and the absence of a benchmark for the evaluation decomposition is the main weakness I would press. The related-work coverage of harness engineering in the LLM world is thorough, and the bridge to video is where the value sits.

There is a third direction in the paper that I want to flag even though it is the least developed, because it is the one with the longest tail. Recursive self-improvement for video model development is presented as a research agenda rather than a result, and the paper is explicit about what it is not claiming: no video model in the paper improved itself. What it offers is the decomposition, the separation of output revision from harness update from weight update from evaluator update, and the channel analysis, which is the question of how an improvement made in one loop can re-enter the development process in another. That question is the one that separates a research program from a slogan, and the paper gets it right in a way the popular version of the idea does not. The popular version says models improve themselves, full stop, which is a claim that is almost unfalsifiable. The paper's version says there are four loops, each with its own feedback signal and its own failure mode, and the interesting research question is which combinations close. That is a much more useful frame, and it is the one I would want the next two years of work in this space to inherit. The open challenges section, with its list of autonomous production, lifelong memory, programmable hybrid control, provenance, and cost accounting, reads less like a limitations paragraph and more like a grant proposal, which is a fair description of the state of the field.

The one thing I would want to see next, and the paper gestures at it in its open challenges, is a small controlled experiment for the state-in-the-engine design: take one existing video world model, back it with a simple engine that owns the state, and measure whether long-horizon consistency improves. That experiment would separate the real claim from the plausible one, and until it exists, agentic world modeling is an argument, not a result. There is a second experiment the paper does not run that I would consider equally diagnostic: a cost accounting of the harness itself. A video agent harness that plans scripts, calls tools, and iterates on feedback is burning model calls at every stage, and the paper correctly names cost accounting as an open challenge but never quantifies it. If the harness triples the compute of a direct generation call, the trade against a slightly longer, better-prompted single pass is a real engineering question, and the field will not know where it stands until someone publishes the dollar-per-second-of-video numbers for an agentic pipeline versus a direct one. That missing table is, in a way, the paper's most important omitted result.

My verdict is that this is a useful map of a field forming right now, with the right three directions and the right taxonomy of recursive-improvement loops, and the state-in-code versus render-in-diffusion decomposition is the idea worth stealing. But it is a position paper without a benchmark, so its force is argumentative: read it to know what questions agentic video should answer, not for an answer.
