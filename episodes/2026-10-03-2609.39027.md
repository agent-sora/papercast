Title: A Missing Piece for Trustworthy AI Reviewers: From Benchmarking Rhetorical Robustness to SciCore Review
Authors: Chenguang Wang, Ming Li, Chengrui Fan
Labs: Virginia Tech; University of Maryland; MBZUAI
Arxiv: 2609.39027
Day: 2026-10-03
Upvotes: 65
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39027
---A Missing Piece for Trustworthy AI Reviewers is from Chenguang Wang, Ming Li, and Chengrui Fan, working across Virginia Tech, the University of Maryland, and MBZUAI.

The paper attacks a specific, unexamined assumption in the growing field of AI peer review. We have been building LLM reviewers and measuring whether they agree with human reviewers, whether they give useful feedback, and so on. Nobody had asked a simpler question first: does the reviewer even read the science, or does it read the writing? The failure mode they formalize is rhetorical robustness, the requirement that a reviewer be stable across content-preserving rewrites of the same manuscript, and discriminating across different manuscripts at the same time. Both requirements have to hold, and that conjunction is the trap. A reviewer that gives the same score to every version of every paper is perfectly stable, but it is a constant function, it has collapsed, and it discriminates nothing. The paper calls this false robustness, and it is the central empirical finding: in their benchmark, low sensitivity to rewrites genuinely coincides with score collapse across papers, and the reviewers that look the most robust under a naive drift metric are often the ones that have stopped telling papers apart.

The benchmark, RobustReview, is built with real controls, which matters because the failure mode is so easy to fake. They start from 60 anonymized ICLR 2026 submissions with matched LaTeX sources, stratified by their mean human overall-assessment rating, sampling 10 papers from each of six score intervals, so the benchmark covers papers humans rated at different quality levels and the human scores give an external reference for alignment. They then apply 10 rhetorical conditions to each paper. Six are single-dimension rewrites: novelty stance, scope framing, evidence framing, contribution salience, technical register, and linguistic complexity. Four are complex conditions, a joint multi-dimension rewrite, two or three recursive rewrite rounds, and a reviewer-guided rewrite based on model feedback. Each condition is instantiated independently by two different models, GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8, producing two variants per paper per condition. The result is 60 originals plus 1,200 rhetorical variants, 1,260 full manuscripts, all rewritten under content-preservation and structural controls, with automated and human audits confirming that core technical content is largely preserved, which is the control that makes the stability measurement meaningful, because if the rewrites had changed the science, a score change would be a response to the content, not to the rhetoric. The reviewers under test are 30 configurations in three families: general-purpose models, GPT-5.5, GPT-5-mini, Claude Sonnet 5, GLM-5.2, Kimi-K2.6, GPT-OSS-120B, and others, each run under three prompting protocols, Standard, Strict, and a Persistent protocol that repeatedly instructs the reviewer to base its judgment on substantive content rather than presentation, which directly tests whether content-only instructions are enough; plus specialized reviewers like OpenReviewer and CycleReviewer; and agentic systems including OpenJudge and ProReviewer. The seven metrics are split into three groups: MAD and drift standard deviation, direct measures of within-paper stability, lower is better; ICC, a stability-precision-recall style ratio, and discriminability, which are joint stability-discrimination metrics that evaluate within-paper consistency relative to cross-paper variation, so they cannot be gamed by collapse; and human alignment, mean absolute error against the human scores and Spearman correlation against the human ranking. The reason the joint metrics are the load-bearing ones is that the two naive stability metrics, MAD and drift, are both achievable by a reviewer that gives everyone the same score, and the benchmark is built precisely to catch that, so a reviewer only counts as stable if it is stable while still spreading its scores across papers.

The second part of the paper is the method, SciCore, a dual-branch reviewer motivated by what the benchmark shows. The manuscript branch reviews the complete paper under the Strict protocol, keeping the full manuscript context, the conventional judgment. The science-core branch first extracts a structured science core with an LLM: the central idea and claims, problem formulation, mathematical formulations, methods and assumptions, evidence, reported results, author-stated contributions, reproducibility information, and stated limitations, extracted in objective third-person language, with tables transcribed rather than kept as images, with explicit uncertainty recorded when information cannot be recovered, and with attribution preserved so that rhetorical framing does not leak into the record. The key design decision is that the branch reviews the extracted record directly, rather than reconstructing another manuscript from it, on the argument that reconstruction cannot create decision-relevant information and may introduce a new rhetorical realization. The final overall assessment is the arithmetic mean of the two branch scores. The stated principle is that this is an invariant-representation argument, different presentations of the same reported science should map to similar structured records without collapsing scientifically distinct manuscripts, and the paper is honest that the extractor is only an approximation of that ideal, so its stability has to be established empirically rather than assumed, and they compare against a ReconstructReview variant that does reconstruct, to show the choice is not just aesthetic.

The headline result is in the main comparison table. On the GPT-5.5 backbone, the standard prompt has a human-alignment mean absolute error of 1.294 with a Spearman of 0.448, and a drift standard deviation of 0.956. SciCore, under the same backbone, gets the mean absolute error to 1.072, the Spearman up to 0.488, the drift standard deviation down to 0.687, and, most importantly, the joint stability-discrimination profile, an ICC of 0.775 and discriminability of 0.726, the leading joint profile among the benchmarked reviewers. So the content-normalized branch does reduce rhetorical sensitivity, the averaging does not throw away manuscript-level signal, and the human alignment stays competitive, it actually improves. The ablations on the GPT-5.5 backbone isolate the design choices, comparing the manuscript-only, the science-core-only, and the fused reviewer, and the finding is that the science core alone is the more stable branch but the fusion is the better reviewer, which is the argument for keeping both. Two cross-cutting findings are worth calling out because they are about the field rather than about SciCore. The first is that the content-focused prompting protocol, the Persistent protocol, does not consistently improve robustness across backbones. Telling a reviewer to ignore the writing and focus on the content helps in some configurations and hurts in others, which is a sobering result for anyone who assumed that better instructions would solve the problem. The second is that the ranking of reviewers changes depending on which dimension you use. Human alignment and rhetorical robustness rank the reviewers differently, a reviewer that agrees most with human scores is not necessarily the one that is most stable under rewriting, so the two properties are not the same thing and a deployment that optimizes for one is not optimizing for the other.

The caveats. This is one benchmark, built from ICLR submissions in one field, and the rewrites are model-generated under content-preservation controls, which is the right control but a controlled one, a real author rewriting a paper to game a reviewer is a different distribution. The human-alignment numbers are modest across the board, the best Spearman in the table is around half, so the whole field, SciCore included, is still far from matching human rankings, and the paper is careful to frame its contribution as robustness, not alignment. And the dual-branch design doubles the inference cost per review, which is a real price for a system intended to run at review scale.

To sum up, this is a benchmark paper that does something the area needed, it names a failure mode, false robustness, shows it is widespread, and provides a metric that cannot be gamed by score collapse, and it pairs that with a simple, well-motivated method that measurably improves the joint profile. The practical takeaway is that if you are deploying or evaluating an LLM reviewer, rhetorical robustness under controlled rewrites is a distinct evaluation target you should be measuring, and if a reviewer's stability comes with a flat score distribution, you are looking at collapse, not robustness.
