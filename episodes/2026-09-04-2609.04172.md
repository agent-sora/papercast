Rethinking On-Policy Distillation of Large Language Models, Part Two: One Training Example, by Zixuan Fu, Bingxiang He, and Yuxin Zuo, from Tsinghua University, the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Northeastern University, with collaborators at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and Johns Hopkins. This is a data-efficiency paper that pushes a familiar training method to its most extreme limit and finds something surprising: on-policy distillation, which is usually run on a full dataset of examples, keeps improving for hundreds of steps when you train it on a single query. The paper's argument is that on-policy distillation is not data-hungry the way supervised fine-tuning is, and that the bottleneck has always been the optimization, not the data.

The setup. On-policy distillation, or OPD, combines student-generated rollouts with dense token-level supervision from a stronger teacher. The student produces its own sequences, and the teacher scores each token, so the student is always being corrected on the states it actually visits rather than on a fixed dataset of target sequences. This is the property that separates OPD from ordinary supervised fine-tuning: in SFT the student imitates a fixed set of target trajectories, so the data you give it is exactly the states it will see, but in OPD the student explores its own trajectory distribution and the teacher's supervision lands on states the student would actually visit in deployment. Prior work has studied the algorithmic side of this, the loss, the sampling, the token-level signal, but the paper says the role of the training data itself has been left unclear. So the authors examine it at the data-minimal limit, and that means training on a single query, a single prompt, over many optimization steps. They call this one-shot OPD, and the central empirical question is why a single query, which on its face should provide a vanishingly small amount of supervision, can still drive substantial improvement for hundreds of steps.

The headline result. One-shot OPD keeps improving for hundreds of steps and recovers most of full-data OPD's gain, and it does so across task domains and across model families, which is the part that makes it a general result rather than an artifact of one model. The paper reports that one-shot OPD recovers about 72 percent of the gain that full-data OPD achieves on mathematical reasoning, and that most of that is captured within the first 100 steps, after which the curve flattens. So the model is not slowly grinding out a hundredth of a point over a thousand steps; it front-loads the recovery, which is the shape you want if you are trying to spend a limited distillation budget.

The explanation is the more valuable part, because a result like this without a mechanism is just a curiosity, and the mechanism here is concrete and measurable. The authors define a quantity they call state coverage, which is the fraction of the states that full-data OPD visits during training that a given set of queries actually reaches through the student's rollouts. The key insight behind the metric is that the value of a training example in on-policy distillation is not the example itself but the states the student passes through while being corrected on it, so two queries can look very different in the prompt but expose very similar state spaces, or look similar and expose very different ones. A single query already reaches 71.5 percent of the state space that full-data training covers, and most of that coverage is reached within the first 100 steps. Adding semantically distinct queries raises coverage and raises validation accuracy together, until 16 queries reach 98.9 percent coverage and match full-data training. So there is a clean, quantified relationship: the more distinct states your query set exposes, the more of the full-data gain you recover, and you saturate around 16 queries rather than a few thousand. The paper also reports the per-domain breakdown, with the recovery holding on mathematics, code, and instruction following separately, which is what lets the single-query result be read as a property of the method rather than a quirk of one task.

The second mechanism is the one the paper makes a thesis out of. Alignment with the teacher slows down at a similar pace whether OPD trains on one query or on the whole dataset, and even a fixed set of states takes hundreds of steps to absorb. The paper's summary of this is a phrase worth keeping: on-policy distillation is data-overfed but algorithm-starved. The rollouts quickly expose a broad amount of supervision, so the data side saturates fast, but the student absorbs that supervision increasingly slowly, so the optimization side is what actually limits how much you get. That reframes the practical problem. If you are distilling a student, the lever that helps is not more data, it is a better or longer optimization pass over the states the data already exposes.

The multi-teacher extension is the stress test. Under multi-teacher on-policy distillation, where the student is supervised by several teachers at once, 16 semantically distinct queries per domain match full-data training. So the data-minimal result does not break when you add the complexity of multiple teachers, which is reassuring for anyone running a multi-teacher distillation setup, because it says the efficiency result survives the harder configuration rather than only holding in the simple single-teacher case. The paper also reports the baseline numbers that make the recovery meaningful: full-data OPD reaches a validation accuracy in the low seventies on the mathematical reasoning setup, and one-shot OPD recovers the large majority of the gap between the untrained student and that full-data ceiling, which is a more striking statement than the percentage-of-gain framing alone. The paper also notes that the query selection matters, that the gain comes from semantically distinct queries rather than redundant ones, and it reports the breakdown across domains, mathematics, code, and instruction following, with the recovery holding across all three. The code is released, so the state-coverage measurement and the query-selection protocol are things you can run against your own distillation data to see where your own bottleneck sits.

The skeptical read. The single most important caveat is that the 72 percent recovery is on mathematical reasoning, which is a domain where a single well-chosen query can genuinely expose a lot of the state space, and the transfer to domains where the state space is more spread out, or where the teacher's behavior is more query-specific, is the question to watch. The 16-query saturation point is measured on particular models and tasks, so whether 16 is the right number for your model family is something you would want to re-measure rather than assume. The state-coverage metric is the load-bearing explanation, and it is a proxy: coverage of visited states is a reasonable stand-in for how much supervision a query set provides, but it is not a direct measure of the quality of that supervision, so a high-coverage query set could in principle provide broad but shallow supervision. And the data-overfed, algorithm-starved framing is a strong claim that the paper supports with the alignment-slowdown observation, but it is an empirical regularity, not a derived law, so treat it as a well-evidenced hypothesis about where the bottleneck is.

The bottom line: this is a clean, well-instrumented paper that reframes on-policy distillation as an optimization-limited rather than data-limited process, and the state-coverage measurement plus the 16-query saturation point are the parts to keep. The defensible claims are the 72 percent single-query recovery, the 98.9 percent coverage at 16 queries, and the multi-teacher result that 16 queries per domain match full-data training. The limits are the single-domain headline number, the coverage metric being a proxy for supervision quality, and the saturation point being model- and task-specific, so use it to cut your distillation data budget substantially, but re-measure the 16-query number on your own models before you commit to it.
