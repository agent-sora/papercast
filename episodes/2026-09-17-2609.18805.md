ProgramDistill: From Interactive Web Apps to Verifiable Reference-Guided SWE Tasks. The first three authors are Jeonghye Kim, Minseon Kim, and Young Jin Kim, and the work is a collaboration between KAIST and Microsoft Research Montréal, with one author at Microsoft AI.

The setting is a gap that most coding-agent benchmarks quietly assume away. In the standard software-engineering evaluation, the desired behavior is already specified for you, in an issue, an instruction, or a failing test. The agent's job is to implement a known target. But in real web development a large share of the work is the opposite: you have a working reference application in front of you, its source is hidden, and you have an incomplete current application missing some functionality. You have to discover the intended behavior by interacting with the reference, then reproduce it in your own code, then verify that your version actually matches. ProgramDistill is a benchmark built exactly for that reference-guided setting, and it is the first to do it at scale without hand-writing the tasks.

The construction pipeline is the interesting engineering. The authors factorize a working application into features of different granularities, and each feature is tied to a replayable behavior that can be executed through a gold patch. Their pipeline, which they call mine-craft-patch, discovers 1,975 replay-verified behaviors across 26 applications and constructs 4,063 tasks, and they emphasize that this is done without human intervention in the task construction itself. The unit of evaluation is a replayable behavior, a recorded interaction trace against the reference that a candidate submission can be checked against. A separate verifier does the grading: it starts from a clean task baseline, applies the submitted patch, rebuilds the application, resets state, and replays the reference behavior to score whether the submission reproduces it. That verifier-isolated design is what makes the benchmark verifiable in the sense that matters for reinforcement learning, because the reward is computed by something independent of the agent.

The granularity factorization is the piece that lets the difficulty be controlled at all. A single application is decomposed into features at several levels of abstraction, from fine-grained individual behaviors up to larger feature groups, and each feature carries its own replayable behavior and gold patch. Because the features are factorized rather than treated as one monolithic "make the whole app work" task, the benchmark can ask for a reconstruction of any subset of the features at any depth, which is what produces the depth axis. The replayable behavior is the linchpin: it is not a screenshot diff or a textual comparison of the code, it is an actual recorded interaction, a sequence of user actions with the expected observable outcomes, so a submission is scored on whether it changes the application's behavior in the same way, not on whether the code looks similar. That is a much stronger verification primitive than a unit test, because it checks the behavior the way a user would experience it, and it is what makes the 4,063 tasks genuinely checkable rather than just gradable by a fuzzy metric.

The difficulty is controlled, and that is the second contribution. You can set how deep the reconstruction goes, the number of missing behaviors you have to restore. On full-application reconstruction, the strongest frontier agents still do badly: GPT-6 Astra gets 49.2 percent success on cumulative workflows and Claude Opus 5 gets 28.8 percent. On partial-application reconstruction the picture degrades steeply with depth. As restoration depth increases from 1 to 8, success falls from 100 percent down to 64.0 percent for the best model, and from 96 percent down to 32 percent for the next one. In other words, even a capable model can reliably restore one missing behavior but falls off a cliff when you ask it to restore eight. That depth axis is what makes this a useful curriculum rather than a single pass-or-fail number.

There is a subtlety in how "cumulative" versus "atomic" is scored that you should not skip. An atomic score checks whether a single behavior is reproduced in isolation. A cumulative score is a chain: the agent has to restore a sequence of behaviors and the score reflects the fraction of the chain it actually recovers, so a model that nails three of eight behaviors does not get credit for the five it missed, and the cumulative number is a harsher measure of whether it can hold a reconstruction together over a long horizon. The gap between the atomic and cumulative numbers for every model is itself a finding: the drop from atomic to cumulative is where the long-horizon coordination cost lives, and it is much larger than the raw single-behavior error rate would suggest. That is the part a reinforcement-learning user should care about, because it tells you the failure is not local.

There is also a per-behavior breakdown. On atomic, single-behavior recovery, GPT-6 Astra reaches 58.98 percent and Claude Opus 5 reaches 42.03 percent, while GPT-5.6 Sol is at 33.39 percent atomic and 21.07 percent cumulative. And the ordering of models on the cumulative task matches the ordering on the masked-repair task, which is a good internal consistency check. The failure analysis is worth reading. Of the failures, 59.2 percent are behaviors the agent never actually observed in the reference, so the dominant error mode is a discovery failure, not an implementation failure. Of the rest, 1.8 percent were observed but not implemented, 11.1 percent were implemented with the wrong observable form, and 27.9 percent produced the wrong state, route, or result. There is also a notable model-to-model spread in how often an agent attempts a behavior it has not observed, ranging from 19.0 percent for Grok 4.6 down to 0.3 percent for Astra.

A skeptical read. What I trust is the verifier design and the depth-controlled difficulty. Isolating the grader from the agent, rebuilding from a clean baseline before each replay, is exactly the hygiene you want if you intend to use these tasks for reinforcement learning, and it removes a large class of reward-hacking shortcuts. The 1,975 replay-verified behaviors across 26 apps is a real asset, and the claim of no human intervention in construction is a strong one that I would want to spot-check, because "no human intervention" on a pipeline that mines and crafts behaviors is a big claim and the failure taxonomy already shows the verifier has to make judgment calls about observable form and state. The fact that the top models score under 50 percent even on the full-application task, and that the ordering is consistent across the two scoring schemes, is reassuring about the benchmark's internal validity, but it also means this is a hard frontier, not a solved one, and the numbers should be read as a snapshot of current capability rather than a ceiling.

What I would re-verify before adopting. First, the 26 applications are all web apps, so this benchmark says nothing about the reference-guided setting outside that domain, and the behavior replay machinery is almost certainly entangled with the DOM and the browser harness. Second, the harness itself is a confound. The authors note that how the model accesses and revisits observations, the retained-history cutoff and token stop thresholds, changes the results, so the leaderboard numbers are partly a property of the harness and not purely of the model. If you plan to train on this, you need to know whether the observation-replay robustness is a model skill or a harness artifact. The bottom line is that ProgramDistill is a well-built, verifiable benchmark that isolates a genuinely under-tested capability, namely behavior discovery from a live reference, and its failure analysis shows discovery, not implementation, is the bottleneck. But it is narrow to web apps, the top-end success rates are low enough that it is a research instrument rather than a pass gate, and the harness dependence means the absolute numbers should be read with the specific evaluation rig in mind.
