Random Attention: Rethinking KV Cache Eviction for Efficient Reasoning, by Heng Wang, Jielin Qiu, and Wenting Zhao, from Salesforce AI Research and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. This is a result that sounds like a joke until you check the numbers: the paper argues that the scoring function at the heart of every KV-cache eviction method contributes almost nothing, and that evicting tokens uniformly at random within each attention head matches the best prior evictor while being much faster to serve. If you have built a KV-cache compressor, this paper is proposing you delete your scorer.

The problem. Large language models do their best work on tasks that require extended reasoning, and the way they do it is by generating long chains of thought. Long chains of thought make the KV cache a severe memory bottleneck, because every token the model reasons over stays in the cache and has to be attended to. The standard response in the literature is a shared paradigm: score each cached token by some estimate of how much it will matter later, and keep the top-scoring ones. Methods like SnapKV and VaSE are built on exactly this idea, and they invest real engineering in the scoring function, whether that is an attention-magnitude heuristic, a learned importance estimator, or a redundancy-aware variant. The paper's opening claim is that this selection signal, the whole reason those methods exist, is doing almost no work, and it frames the question as an empirical one that the field has not actually checked: everyone assumes the scorer is the point, but nobody has run the obvious control experiment of evicting at random and measuring the gap.

The method is the opposite of what you would expect. Random Attention keeps the prompt and evicts the rest uniformly at random within each attention head, computing no score at all. There is no importance estimate, no attention-magnitude threshold, no learned selector. It is, in the implementation sense, the null model for eviction. The per-head detail matters for the mechanism: eviction is drawn independently within each attention head, so a head that keeps its own redundant copy of the trace is not competing with a different head for the same tokens, and the redundancy that makes the random draw safe is preserved across heads by construction. And across four models and six reasoning tasks, it matches the strongest prior evictor while serving 32 to 43 percent higher throughput in a vLLM deployment. At their own largest batches, the paper reports the random method beats SnapKV by 16 percent on throughput and VaSE by 20 percent. So the no-op method is both as accurate and meaningfully faster, which is the result that makes the rest of the paper worth reading.

The controlled experiments are where the paper earns its credibility, because a null-model result is only as good as the explanation for why it works. The authors run ablations and land on two mechanisms. First, the prompt is the fragile part of the cache, and most of the gap between different eviction selectors is really just whether a given selector happened to keep the prompt alive. This is concrete in a way that is easy to miss: on reasoning traces the prompt is only a few hundred tokens while the chain of thought that follows it is orders of magnitude longer, so a selector that happens to evict some prompt tokens loses a disproportionate share of the signal, and a selector that protects the prompt looks much better than one that does not, for reasons that have nothing to do with the quality of its scoring function. Second, the reasoning trace protects itself against eviction through redundancy at two levels. At the text level, the model restates what it still needs as it works, so the information is repeated across the sequence. And across attention heads, each head keeps its own copy of the trace. So a random draw retains enough copies of what the model still needs, and no score is required to pick them. This is the interesting scientific content: it is a claim about the structure of reasoning traces, not just a serving trick. The paper also situates itself against the other scoring evictors, R-KV with its SnapKV-style score and redundancy penalty, LazyEviction with its future-importance estimate, and score-guided randomized eviction, and the controlled comparisons are what let it argue that the scoring signal is the redundant part rather than the essential part.

The engineering detail that makes this deployable is that the whole method is a change to the eviction policy, not the model. You keep the prompt, and for the generated tokens you drop the scoring pass entirely, which is what buys the throughput. The paper frames the gain from simply skipping the scoring pass as the source of the 32 to 43 percent, which is a useful way to think about it, because it means the win is not from a cleverer selection but from not paying for a selection at all. The throughput numbers are worth reading carefully: the 32 to 43 percent is the serving-speedup over the strongest evictor at matched batch sizes, and the paper also reports a much larger figure, up to 28.8 times full-attention throughput, when it is compared against running the model with no eviction at all, which is the number to keep if your bottleneck is the cache cap rather than the scoring cost. The two numbers answer different questions, and confusing them is the easy mistake to make when you go to quote the result.

The skeptical read. The claim is strong, so the scope matters. Four models and six reasoning tasks is a reasonable but not exhaustive sweep, and matching the strongest evictor on accuracy while beating it on throughput is a different statement from beating it on accuracy, so the honest framing is parity on quality with a speed win, not a quality improvement. The redundancy explanation is the load-bearing part, and it is argued through controlled experiments rather than a formal proof, so whether it holds on task families where the model does not restate its working state, or on models with different attention structures, is the open question. The throughput numbers are deployment-specific to vLLM, so the 16 and 20 percent margins over SnapKV and VaSE are the figures for that stack, not constants. The accuracy parity is also reported as the random method being within a small number of points of the best evictor on the individual reasoning benchmarks, which is the right way to read it, because the value proposition is that you get that near-parity for free by removing the scorer, and the small accuracy cost is the price of the throughput. And the method inherits the risk of any random eviction: if the prompt-protection assumption fails on a given workload, there is no scoring signal left to fall back on, because the whole design removed it, which is exactly why the paper's own framing is to keep the prompt and randomize only the reasoning portion.

The bottom line: this is a clean, counterintuitive, and genuinely useful serving result, and the two-level redundancy mechanism is the part to keep in your model of how reasoning traces actually behave. The defensible claims are the throughput win, the 32 to 43 percent in vLLM and the 16 and 20 percent margins over the two named evictors, and the prompt-is-fragile finding. The limits are parity rather than improvement on accuracy, the vLLM-specific numbers, and the removed fallback, so adopt it where prompt protection is reliable and keep a scoring evictor for the workloads where it is not. The most useful reframe it offers is a design one: before you invest in a cleverer scoring function for your evictor, run the random baseline, because if your scorer is not beating uniform random, the engineering you spent on it is not buying you accuracy.
