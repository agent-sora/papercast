Normalized Low-Rank Adaptation, by Jiale Kang, Ziyin Yue, and Zheng Zhan, from Yuanshi Intelligence, Microsoft Research, and the Chinese University of Hong Kong. The one-line version: normalizing LoRA's down-projection matrix, either continuously or just at initialization, consistently speeds up convergence and improves accuracy across pretraining, finetuning, and reinforcement learning, with no extra parameters or inference cost.

LoRA is the default tool for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and the reasons are practical: you train a tiny low-rank pair of matrices instead of the full weights, and the update can be merged back into the base model at deployment. But the training dynamics of that low-rank pair have never really been interrogated. The observation this paper builds on is asymmetric: because LoRA initializes the up-projection to zero, the very first gradients flow almost entirely through the down-projection. Whatever the scale structure of that down-projection is at step zero largely dictates the early trajectory of the whole adapter, and that structure is currently left to random initialization.

The fix is a one-line constraint. For a down-projection A with rank dimension r, NoRA normalizes each column of A to unit norm along the rank direction, so every input coordinate is projected through a unit-length latent direction. The key design choice is that this normalization depends on the LoRA parameters, not on the input. That keeps the update linear in the input, which means the normalized adapter can still be absorbed into the pretrained weight matrix after training. You keep the mergeability that makes LoRA deployable, and you only pay a per-step normalization during training.

The motivation for why this matters comes from a related architecture, multi-head latent attention, which the paper uses as the seed idea. In MLA, the low-dimensional latent feature that gets projected back to the output space is explicitly normalized before the up-projection, and that input-dependent normalization is what stabilizes its dynamics. You cannot copy that directly into LoRA, because normalizing the input-dependent latent would make the update nonlinear in the input and break mergeability. The authors' move is to move the scale control from the feature to the matrix: since modern transformers are pre-normalized, the input magnitude is already controlled by the surrounding layer norm, so the only remaining uncontrolled scale is the column norm of the down-projection itself. Normalize that, and you get the same scale-control effect while keeping the whole thing a plain low-rank linear map.

A second, even cheaper variant is NoRA-init: apply the same column normalization once, at initialization, and then train with the standard un-normalized LoRA parameterization. The paper's gradient-norm analysis suggests most of the benefit is locked in at the start of optimization, and the experiments back that up. There is also a structured special case called BIMI, which initializes the down-projection as repeated identity blocks, and the paper shows the recent MiSS parameter-efficient method is itself a fixed-instance special case of NoRA, which retroactively explains MiSS's early empirical wins.

The pretraining results are the cleanest evidence. On a 340M-parameter MLA model trained on ten billion SlimPajama tokens, standard low-rank parameterization on multi-head attention actually collapses: its LAMBADA perplexity diverges and several downstream scores crater, with gradient norms dying to abnormally low levels during training. NoRA-init recovers that, matching or beating the full-parameter baseline on the commonsense suites. That is a real stability result, not a marginal one.

It is worth unpacking what the collapse actually looks like, because it is the paper's most concrete contribution. When they parameterize all four attention projections, query, key, value, and output, as rank-64 low-rank factors in a standard multi-head-attention 289M model, the un-normalized LoRA update produces a model that has effectively stopped learning on the long-range benchmark: the LAMBADA perplexity entry is a dash, meaning it blew up, and the accuracy scores on the commonsense tasks sit well below the full-parameter model trained in the same run. The diagnosis in the optimization logs is that the gradient norms sink to abnormally low levels partway through training, which is the signature of a parameterization that is not passing useful signal to the optimizer. NoRA-init, applied to the exact same low-rank setup, lifts that model back to within a point of the full-parameter baseline on most suites. The contrast is between two configurations that differ only in how the down-projection is initialized and constrained, which is why I find the result more compelling than a typical accuracy table.

On supervised finetuning of Llama 3.2-3B, the numbers are more modest but directionally consistent. Averaging GSM8K, Math, HumanEval, and MBPP, NoRA reaches 43.37, NoRA-init 42.38, against 37.93 for standard LoRA and 40.26 for PiSSA. The forgetting story is where the method earns its keep: standard LoRA loses 0.56 points on average across the retained benchmarks while NoRA holds to plus 0.02, and NoRA-init lands at minus 0.19. So the normalization is doing double duty, improving task accuracy and protecting the prior knowledge.

There is also an ablation worth sitting with. They vary the initialization distribution of the down-projection, uniform, Gaussian, and Kaiming uniform, and compare no normalization, row-wise normalization, and the rank-direction column normalization. Row-wise normalization does essentially nothing, which makes sense: it is the per-input-coordinate scale that matters, not the row. The column normalization lifts every initialization scheme by roughly the same margin, so the benefit is not an accident of a lucky random seed and the gain is largely independent of how you initialize. That is the kind of robustness that tells you you have found the right control, not a tuning trick. BIMI, the deterministic block-identity initialization, lands within a point of the normalized random initializations, which is the practical takeaway: if you want the benefit without the per-step normalization call, the identity-block init gets you most of the way there for free.

The paper also extends the method to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards under their ReRL framework, and reports the same pattern there: faster convergence and more stable reward curves. I will be honest that the RL section is thinner than the pretraining and SFT sections in the main text, with the detailed settings deferred to the appendix, so I would weight the RL claim accordingly. The three settings together, pretraining, SFT, and RL, are the paper's strongest structural argument, because a method that only helped in one regime would be a curiosity, but one that helps in all three suggests it is fixing something fundamental about low-rank optimization rather than patching a single setup.

Now the skeptical read. First, the SFT gains are single-digit points on a 3B model and the paper reports one model scale for the main table; I would want the 7B and 13B Llama rows before trusting the magnitude, and the pretraining result, while striking, is at 340M with ten billion tokens, a regime where a collapse-and-recover story is easier to produce than at scale. Second, the mechanism claim that column scale imbalance is the binding constraint is supported by gradient-norm plots and one ablation, not by a controlled comparison that isolates the normalization from the initialization change. The Normk-versus-Normr ablation shows the rank-direction is what matters, which is the right control, but it does not separate how much comes from the persistent constraint versus the initialization. Third, the connection to MiSS is elegant but somewhat reverse-engineered: it is plausible that a fixed block-identity down-projection is a normalized instance, but that does not prove the normalization is the active ingredient rather than the particular structure. If you implement this: NoRA-init is the pragmatic choice, it is a few lines in your adapter init and it keeps your merge path intact. Start there, watch your gradient norms and your retained-benchmark deltas, and only move to the persistent NoRA constraint if the init-only variant underperforms on your task. The riskiest thing to copy is assuming the stability win at 340M generalizes to the pretraining scales where LoRA is actually used.

Bottom line: this is a small, clean, well-motivated regularization result that fixes a real, reproducible failure mode in low-rank pretraining and gives a modest but consistent lift in finetuning and RL. It is the kind of change you would adopt in an adapter stack without much ceremony, with the caveat that the headline stability claim lives at a small scale that the paper does not yet scale up.
