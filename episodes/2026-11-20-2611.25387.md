TuringSpace, by Xuwei Ding, Skylar Zhai, and Linxin Song, at the University of Pennsylvania, the University of Minnesota, and the University of Southern California, is a synthetic web environment of interactive CAPTCHAs designed to test whether multimodal large language models can ground spatial intelligence in actual screen actions. The core idea is that humans solve CAPTCHAs through a loop that models are bad at: they infer the 3D layout behind a 2D view, predict how a click or drag will change the scene, and then correct themselves after seeing the result. Existing CAPTCHA benchmarks are mostly already solved by strong models and rarely test spatial reasoning, while spatial reasoning benchmarks rarely require the model to act. TuringSpace sits in that gap: 125 interactive CAPTCHAs spanning five spatial abilities, each of which must be solved by clicking and dragging on a rendered screen, with the model observing the consequence of every action.

The design detail that makes this a real test rather than a multiple-choice spatial quiz is that every task is a program. Each CAPTCHA is generated by a code program that can produce new instances on demand, and it ships with both a checker that scores a solution and an oracle that knows the correct answer. That means the benchmark is not a fixed set of 125 images, it is a generative task family, which is what lets the authors use the same environment as a training gym. A fixed image set saturates once the model memorizes the distribution, but a generative program lets you keep sampling fresh instances, and that is exactly what they do when they fine-tune a model on the environment and then test it on held-out generated tasks and out-of-distribution benchmarks. The five spatial abilities are the dimensions the tasks are organized along, so a score is a per-ability breakdown, not a single number.

The headline results are stark, which is the point of a diagnostic benchmark. Humans solve 94.1 percent of the tasks. The best model they test, GPT-6 Astra, still fails 25.9 percent of them. And the best open-weight model, Kimi K3, fails 68.0 percent. So there is a large gap between the best proprietary model and humans, and a much larger gap to the best open-weight model, and the failure rate is high enough that this is a genuinely hard task, not one that is close to saturation. That is the contrast with most CAPTCHA benchmarks, where strong models already score near the human level and the benchmark has run out of signal. The fact that even GPT-6 Astra fails more than a quarter of these means the environment is measuring something current models do not have.

The test panel is itself informative. The benchmark scores humans plus 16 multimodal large language models organized into three groups, frontier proprietary models, strong open-weight models, and specialized computer-use or agent models, all measured at pass-at-one so there is no retry advantage. The spread within the panel is wide enough that the diagnostic value is not just the gap to humans but the ordering, because you can see that the models that are strong at general spatial reasoning still fail the action-grounded subset, which is the specific capability the environment is built to isolate. The per-task timing is a small but telling detail: humans take on average 33.1 seconds per task, which is the pace of a person actually clicking and dragging and correcting, and the model evaluation is measured against that interactive standard rather than a single-shot answer, which is what keeps the benchmark in the action regime rather than drifting back toward a screenshot question.

The analysis of why models fail is the more interesting part for anyone trying to build this capability. The paper finds that most failures come from misreading the screenshot, that is, the model forms a wrong belief about the spatial state of the screen, and then, crucially, the model's reasoning rarely corrects the wrong belief. Instead of catching its own error when it sees the consequence of a bad action, the model tends to hallucinate evidence that defends the original wrong interpretation. That is a specific and damning failure mode: the closed loop that is supposed to let the model correct itself is being used to rationalize rather than to update. For a lab building an agentic spatial model, this is the finding to internalize, because it says that adding more reasoning steps on top of a misperceived screenshot does not help, and may actively hurt, because the reasoning is co-opted into defending the perceptual error rather than checking it.

The learnability result is the second contribution, and it is the part that makes this more than a diagnostic. They fine-tune Qwen3.5 9B on the TuringSpace gym, using 55,000 verified trajectories generated by the environment, and the resulting model, which they call TuringSpace 9B, has its success rate rise from 1.6 percent to 33.1 percent, which is a large absolute jump and puts it above both Kimi K3 and Qwen3.8 Max on the same task. More importantly, the gains transfer: the fine-tuned model improves on all seven out-of-distribution benchmarks they test, including a 9.9 point gain on VSI-Bench, which uses real-world indoor video rather than synthetic CAPTCHAs. So the spatial ability learned in the synthetic action environment generalizes to real scenes, which is the claim that this is a scalable way to teach the capability, not just a way to measure it. The 9B open model getting there by fine-tuning on a generative gym is the actionable result, because it does not require a frontier-scale model to make progress on this class of task.

The skeptical reading. First, the gap to humans is large, but the task is synthetic, and a synthetic CAPTCHA environment has a known distribution of spatial layouts that a fine-tuned model can overfit to, so the 33.1 percent and the out-of-distribution gains should be checked against how much of the OOD benchmark distribution overlaps with the generative program's layout statistics. The 9.9 point VSI-Bench gain is the most convincing transfer signal because it is real video, but it is one benchmark, and the seven OOD benchmarks as a set still share more structure with the synthetic gym than a truly novel spatial environment would. Second, the misreading failure mode is a strong claim, and it rests on the authors' analysis of the model's reasoning traces, so if you are going to act on the finding that reasoning defends perceptual errors, you would want to verify that the trace analysis is not conflating a genuinely wrong belief with a correct belief that was expressed ambiguously. Third, the fine-tune is on a single 9B model, and the result is a demonstration of learnability, not a recipe, because the data generation, the reward from the checker, and the training schedule for turning a gym into a fine-tuning set are the details that determine whether the 1.6 to 33.1 jump reproduces on a different base model.

For implementation, the two assets are the generative task programs and the checker-oracle pair. If you want to build a spatial reasoning gym of your own, the pattern is the one to copy: write each task as a program that samples instances, score solutions with a checker, and keep an oracle for labeling, because that is what turns a fixed benchmark into a training environment. The diagnostic value is in the per-ability breakdown and the failure attribution, so when you run your own model, the thing to look at is whether its reasoning corrects or defends perceptual errors, because that is the failure mode the paper identifies and the one that limits the benefit of adding more reasoning steps.

To summarize the bottom line. TuringSpace is a well-constructed diagnostic and training gym for spatial reasoning grounded in screen actions, with a clear finding that even the best model misreads screenshots and then rationalizes the error rather than correcting it, and a learnability result showing that a 9B model fine-tuned on the gym transfers its gains to real-world video. Its limits are the synthetic task distribution and the single-model fine-tuning demonstration, so treat the failure-mode analysis as the primary contribution to verify and the generative-gym pattern as the reusable engineering idea.
