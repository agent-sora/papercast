ASCENT is from Haodong Lu and Dong Gong, both at the University of New South Wales, working out of the Artificer AI Lab. It is a method for online test-time training of long-horizon agents: the agent's own verified trajectories, collected as it works through a stream of tasks, are distilled back into the model's weights while deployment continues.

The setting is a deployed agent facing a stream of related tasks. Each task is long-horizon, solved over many reasoning and action turns, and there is exactly one verification signal, at the end of the episode. The core observation is that the trajectories the agent produces are a natural learning resource. The regime they study has a specific set of constraints that is worth enumerating, because it is what makes the problem hard and what prior work does not cover. The task stream is single-pass, so the agent sees each task once and cannot revisit it. There is one attempt per task, no best-of-N sampling to manufacture a success signal. The only supervision is the terminal environment verification. And the updates must persist across tasks, so the agent is genuinely evolving rather than adapting per-episode and resetting. That combination, single pass, single attempt, terminal verification, persistent updates, is the regime they name Online Agentic Test-Time Training, and it is strictly harder than the standard test-time training setting where you control the number of rollouts per problem. Prior work splits into two camps. In-context adaptation agents store reflections, memories, or retrieved skills in a harness and condition the frozen model on them; they change the input but never the weights, so they are bounded by retrieval quality and by how well the frozen policy executes the retrieved context over a long interaction. The other camp, test-time training, adapts the weights within a single episode and resets them afterward, or requires pre-deployment training of the adaptation behavior, or needs multiple sampled attempts per problem. Reinforcement-learning post-training optimizes the policy before deployment on a fixed task set, typically with many rollouts per task. None of those adapt a long-horizon agent's weights at test time, in a single pass over the task stream, with one attempt per task, carrying the updates across tasks. That is the gap ASCENT targets, and they name the regime Online Agentic Test-Time Training.

The mechanism is self-distillation of verified experience. The agent executes each task once. The environment verifies the terminal state. Each trajectory, together with its verification result, drives a weight update that persists to the next task. The learning signal is self-generated: the agent teaches itself from its own rollouts. The critical design choice is what to distill from. A trajectory that succeeded carries a verified, correct reasoning path; a trajectory that failed carries a warning, but the supervision is much noisier. The method focuses on the verified, successful experiences as the clean distillation target, which is what makes the weight updates safe to accumulate across a stream without a critic or a reward model in the loop. The paper ablates the choice of distillation divergence, comparing forward and reverse KL, with forward KL as the default, and reports that the divergence choice moves pre-update success rates but the overall direction of the gain is stable. The weight updates are applied through a persistent cross-task LoRA state, so the adaptation is low-rank and bounded, which keeps the risk of catastrophic drift across a long stream manageable.

The base models are Qwen3.5 at 4B and 9B parameters, both natively multimodal agent models. The two evaluation environments are ALFWorld, a long-horizon household task simulator with text and visual state, and WebShop, a simulated e-commerce site where the agent must search, filter, and purchase. These are standard long-horizon agent benchmarks with a clear terminal verification, which is exactly what the method requires.

The headline result is that ASCENT improves exact task success over the un-evolved base model by more than 22 points on both ALFWorld and WebShop at both the 4B and 9B scales. That is a large margin for a method that adds no new data, no reward model, and no pre-deployment training phase, only online self-distillation from the agent's own verified trajectories. The paper also includes an ablation comparing in-context adaptation against parametric adaptation on the same tasks, and reports that the parametric, weight-level updates generalize better over the long interaction than the harness-level reflection storage, which is the direct test of the paper's central claim.

There is a subtlety in how the gains are measured. The paper distinguishes tasks solved by both the base and ASCENT from tasks newly solved by ASCENT alone, and reports the improvement on exact success, not partial credit. That is a stricter metric than the average over all rollouts, and it means the 22-point gain is in fully completed tasks, not near-completions. It also reports a comparison against in-context baselines under the same single-pass, one-attempt-per-task constraint, so the margin is not an artifact of giving ASCENT more rollouts.

Three further results strengthen the picture. First, the gains concentrate where the base is weakest: on ALFWorld, the base rarely solves the Heat and Cool task families, and ASCENT improves both at each scale, with the gain over the base much larger on later tasks for the 4B model while the base stays flat. Second, the evolved policy is not just more accurate, it is more efficient: ASCENT reduces the mean number of turns per episode at both scales on both benchmarks, and the paper shows a concrete trajectory where the base plus five in-context adaptation variants exhausts the turn budget on a cool-egg-in-microwave task, while the evolved agent solves it in 20 turns using LoRA updates from two earlier tasks. Third, the experience transfers: on unseen ALFWorld scenes, the frozen evolved policy already leads the online comparators, and resuming online updates on top of it pushes the 4B model substantially further, which indicates the seen-stream experience is retained in the weights rather than memorized as surface patterns.

One comparator deserves a name: TT-OPSD, the authors' test-time adaptation of the OPSD distillation method, which uses the same self-distillation but whose teacher sees only the current turn rather than the complete verified trajectory. ASCENT beats it, which isolates the value of hindsight: the full verified trajectory, not just the current state, is what makes the distillation target informative. The paper also reports that direct imitation and policy-gradient baselines severely degrade under the online regime, which is why they are excluded from the main tables, and that is itself a finding: naive gradient methods on the same sparse verification signal do not work online.

Where I would push back. First, the evaluation is on two environments that are text-and-action heavy with a clean verifier. The method's dependence on a reliable terminal verification signal is a real constraint: in domains where success is ambiguous, partially verifiable, or only observable after long delays, the distillation target is noisier and the method's guarantee weakens. Second, the base model is a single family, Qwen3.5, at two scales. Whether the method transfers to a different backbone, or to a model whose native tool-use is weaker, is not tested. Third, the paper is honest that this is a single-pass stream, which means the agent cannot revisit an earlier task with a better policy; the learning is monotonic and irreversible in the forward direction. Fourth, 22 points is a big number, but the absolute success rates matter too, and the gap between the base and the evolved agent on the hardest subtasks is where the method's value either concentrates or evaporates.

The verdict: this is a clean, well-scoped demonstration that a long-horizon agent can improve its own weights online, from its own verified trajectories, without any external supervision, and the 22-point gains at both scales across two environments are substantial. The open question is the transfer: does the verified-experience self-distillation hold up where the verifier is weak or the backbone is different, and that is the next experiment the community should run.
