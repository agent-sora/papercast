SO-Arena: Benchmarking the Capability Spectrum and Reliability of System One Models, by Lichen Li, Ziping Dong, and Sicheng Feng, from the National University of Singapore, is a benchmark for System One models, the fast decision models that answer a question with a typed output, a choice among options with a probability for each, without generating a long chain of tokens, and the paper's central finding is that a model that scores well on average accuracy can still be a bad decision-maker in the ways that matter, because the best-performing model in their set ranks only seventh in reliability.

To set up the motivation, System One models are pitched as a way to do general-purpose decision-making quickly and cheaply, the fast intuitive counterpart to a slow deliberate language model, and they are used for things like routing requests, selecting tools, flagging unsafe prompts, and recognizing intents. But the authors' point is that aggregate performance on existing benchmarks does not tell you two things you actually need. One is the breadth of what the model can do, the capability spectrum, how wide the set of domains and difficulty levels it handles well really is. The other is the reliability of its decisions, whether it gives you a stable answer when you change the framing of the choices, and whether it knows when there is no valid answer. Existing evaluations, they argue, give limited insight into either, so they build SO-Arena to measure both.

The capability-spectrum side has three components. The first is evidence-grounded evaluation, where the reference labels come from reliable sources and are manually verified against the inputs the model actually sees, which is meant to fix a common problem in decision benchmarks where the labels are ambiguous or do not clearly follow from what is shown to the model. The second is fine-grained capability assessment through domain-specific difficulty criteria, so instead of a flat set of problems you get a stratified spread from easy to hard within each domain, which is what lets you see the shape of the capability rather than just the average. The third is realistic interactive evaluation, where the model iteratively executes actions in sandboxes, so it is not just answering a single multiple-choice question but actually operating in a setting that unfolds over time. The benchmark spans eleven domains and five capability families, and the reasoning-intensive tasks are designed so that you can directly compare a System One model against a System Two model on the same task.

The reliability side is the part I find most interesting, and it uses two complementary measures. The first is decision stability under controlled changes to the option set, which is a test of whether the model's answer is robust to the framing, or whether it just flips its choice when you reorder or rephrase the options it is choosing among. The second is missing-answer recognition, which is whether the model can say there is no valid option when the correct answer is genuinely not on the list, rather than being forced to pick the least-bad wrong answer. These two, taken together, are a definition of reliability that goes well beyond raw accuracy, and that is the conceptual contribution, the claim that capability breadth and decision reliability are complementary dimensions you have to measure separately.

The main empirical result comes from evaluating twenty-one models. The finding is that the benefits of scaling and of adding reasoning vary a lot across domains, so a model that improves a lot in one domain might barely move in another, and the more important result is the decoupling between accuracy and reliability. Strong overall performance does not imply reliable decision-making, and the specific data point they lead with is that the model with the best aggregate accuracy ranks only seventh when you rank the models by reliability. That is the headline, and it is a clean, reproducible claim if you trust their reliability measures.

Now the skeptical part. This is a benchmark, so the load-bearing question is whether the reliability measures are measuring something real and stable, or something that is an artifact of how they constructed the controlled option-set changes. Decision stability under option-set changes is the right idea, but the way you construct the changed option sets matters a great deal, and if the changes are too trivial the measure just re-measures accuracy, and if they are too aggressive it measures something closer to brittleness than to the reliability you would care about in deployment. I would want to see the specific perturbation protocol and an analysis of how the stability score responds to the magnitude of the change before I treated the seventh-place ranking as a robust fact.

The second concern is the manual verification of reference labels. It is the right thing to do and it is what separates this from a lot of decision benchmarks, but manual verification at the scale of eleven domains and interactive sandboxes is exactly where label quality tends to degrade, and I would want to know the inter-annotator agreement and the verification sample size, because the whole evidence-grounding claim rests on the labels being right. The interactive sandbox evaluation is the most ambitious part, and it is also the part most likely to have hidden confounds, because a model's interactive performance depends on the sandbox's action space and state representation as much as on the model itself.

Third, the twenty-one models are a snapshot, and the accuracy-reliability decoupling, while striking, is a claim about a particular set of models and a particular definition of reliability. The generalization to future models is the question, and I would want to see the decoupling hold on a model family that was not obviously optimized for the accuracy benchmarks they use. The engineering gotcha, if you are building on this, is that the interactive and reliability components are substantially more expensive to run than a plain accuracy benchmark, which is going to matter if you want to use this as a regular evaluation rather than a one-off study.

The five capability families and eleven domains are the part that makes this more than a single-task benchmark, and the design intent is to get a profile of the model rather than a score. A model that is strong on intent recognition and weak on tool selection, or strong on easy items across the board but collapsing on the hard stratified tier, is a very different deployment candidate than one with a flat strong profile, and that is the information a single aggregate number cannot give you. The difficulty stratification is the mechanism that makes the profile possible, because without a within-domain spread from easy to hard you cannot tell whether a domain score is a ceiling the model hit or just the difficulty of the items you happened to draw. The interactive sandbox component is the most forward-looking piece, because it is the one that tests whether the model can operate over a horizon of actions rather than answer a single multiple-choice question, and it is the component that most directly connects to how these models are actually deployed inside agent harnesses. The reasoning-intensive tasks that enable a system-one-versus-system-two comparison are useful in the same way, because they give you a head-to-head on the same task with the same inputs, which is the cleanest way to see where the fast model stops being sufficient.

The bottom line is this: separating capability breadth from decision reliability, and showing that the top accuracy model is a middling reliability model, is a useful and concrete reframing of how System One models should be evaluated, and the evidence-grounding and missing-answer measures are the right instincts. But the reliability ranking only means what the construction of the controlled option-set changes and the label verification let it mean, and I would re-run the stability protocol on my own decision workloads before I trusted a specific reliability ranking.
