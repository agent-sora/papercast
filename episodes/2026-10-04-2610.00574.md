Make Sparse Rewards Count is from Tong Zheng, Skylar Zhai, and Zhan Cheng, with collaborators across the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, the University of Wisconsin–Madison, Stony Brook University, and several other institutions. It is a method for balancing multiple rewards during reinforcement-learning post-training of language models.

The problem is familiar to anyone who has run multi-reward reinforcement learning on a language model. You want the model to get answers right, follow a format, stay within a length budget, and use tools correctly, all at once. The standard fix is group-relative policy optimization, and a recent variant, GDPO, normalizes each reward dimension independently before aggregating, which preserves the relative signal of each objective. That is an improvement over naive weighted sums, but the paper's observation is that uneven learning persists even under GDPO. Some rewards improve fast and others crawl, and the model ends up prioritizing whichever objectives are easiest to satisfy. The authors want to understand why, in a way that gives a principled lever rather than a hand-tuned set of weights.

The analysis is the interesting part, and it is genuinely simple. The authors define advantage energy for a reward as the sum of its squared advantages over a batch, which is a measure of how much that reward is actually pushing the policy update. Then they make a key observation specific to group-relative methods. A reward only contributes a group-relative learning signal in a rollout group when its values differ across the responses in that group. They call that group active for that reward, and active-group density is the fraction of rollout groups in which a reward is active. Under idealized GDPO normalization, they show that a reward's advantage energy is proportional to its active-group density. The consequence is the diagnosis. A reward that is active in only a small fraction of groups, a sparse reward like a format constraint that most responses already satisfy, carries proportionally less energy into the update, so it learns slowly. GDPO fixed the within-group scale, but it left a residual batch-level imbalance, the sparser the reward, the quieter its voice in the aggregate gradient.

The method, DARA, turns that relation into a correction. It derives an inverse-square-root density weighting, giving greater weight to signals from rewards that are less frequently active. Concretely, each reward's weight is computed from its active-group density measured in the current rollout batch, so a sparser reward is upweighted by the inverse square root of how often it fires. The important property is that the weights are recomputed every batch, which means the method adapts automatically as reward activity shifts during training. Early in training, when a format constraint is rarely satisfied, it gets a large boost; later, once most responses comply and the reward becomes dense, the boost fades on its own. Nothing about the underlying policy optimization changes, it is a reweighting layer on top of GDPO, and it uses no extra hyperparameters to tune per task.

The experiments run two suites. The first is tool calling, on Qwen2.5, both the 1.5 billion and 3 billion instruct models, evaluated on BFCL-v4 across the Live, Non-Live, and Multi-Turn subsets. The claim here is speed of format acquisition. At the final checkpoint, DARA lands at essentially the same format compliance and tool-calling accuracy as GDPO, so final performance is competitive. But the learning curve is different. DARA reaches high format compliance in up to 26 percent fewer training steps. The mechanism is visible in the plots. The format reward's rapid increase coincides with a peak in active-group density, and DARA brings both that density peak and the rapid-learning phase earlier, because the upweighting keeps the sparse format signal active while it matters. There is also a three-reward variant that adds a length reward, and this is where the calibration really earns its keep. Naively adding a length reward to GDPO drops average format compliance from 97.11 to 94.39, and multi-turn format from 91.40 to 83.17, a clear interference between the objectives. DARA largely avoids that, with average format moving only from 97.90 to 96.93 in the asymmetric variant, while still achieving higher average length compliance than GDPO.

The second suite is mathematical reasoning, trained on the full DeepScaleR-Preview collection, where the length reward asks for a short, bounded thinking segment. The evaluation is a macro-average pass@1 accuracy over five public benchmarks, MATH-500, AIME 2024, AMC 2022 and 2023, Minerva Math, and the competition subset of OlympiadBench, with checkpoints probed every 10 steps so the authors can measure optimization speed directly. The headline is that DARA reaches near-saturated length compliance in up to 65 percent fewer training steps than GDPO, while keeping accuracy competitive. The comparison against fixed weights is the more instructive result. If you instead set the length weight to five times the correctness weight by hand, you do improve length compliance, but at a real cost to accuracy, with the pass@1 dropping in the asymmetric variant from 48.03 to 46.84. The adaptive DARA weight gets most of the length-compliance benefit without that accuracy hit, because the weight decays as the length reward becomes dense rather than staying pinned at a fixed high value.

The skeptical read is that this is a well-motivated reweighting with a clean theory, and the theory is the durable contribution. The active-group density to advantage energy relation is a genuinely useful diagnostic for anyone debugging uneven multi-reward learning, and it is cheap to compute, just a fraction of groups per reward per batch. The concern is the scope of the evidence. Both suites are small models, the 1.5 and 3 billion instruct variants for tool calling and small DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3 models for math, run for at most 100 steps. The claim is about optimization speed, faster compliance, not final quality, and the final-checkpoint numbers are deliberately framed as competitive rather than superior, which is an honest framing but also a limit on the claim. The inverse-square-root form is derived under an idealized normalization, so its tightness at scale, or with many more than three rewards, is an open question. And the fixed-weight comparison, while fair, is a strawman in the sense that a practitioner would tune the weight to the same end point, so the speed advantage is the real story and the quality story is mostly neutral.

Verdict: DARA offers a principled, no-hyperparameter fix for the slow learning of sparse rewards in multi-reward RL, grounded in a clear density-to-energy analysis, and the speedups of 26 to 65 percent in fewer training steps are a practical win for short training budgets. The caveat is that the evidence is confined to small models and short runs with at most three rewards, so treat the scaling behavior as the open question before adopting it on a large frontier post-training pipeline.

If you are running multi-reward RL in production, there are two concrete things to try before committing to DARA. First, instrument your current run with active-group density per reward per batch, because it is nearly free and it will show you exactly which objectives are going quiet, the ones that will learn slowest. Second, run a short 100 step comparison of your current aggregation against an inverse-square-root density correction on your hardest sparse reward, and look at the step to reach your compliance threshold rather than the final number. The method is a drop-in reweighting on top of GDPO, it introduces no new hyperparameters, and the code is public, so the cost of the experiment is a single training run, not an architecture change.

The one thing to watch is how the density estimate behaves when a reward is active in only a handful of groups, where the inverse-square-root weight can grow large and inject noise into the update, so in a low-signal regime you would want to floor the weight rather than let it diverge.
