Urbex: Agentic Spatial Grounding for City-Scale 3D Scenes, by Yu Wang, Rui Dai, and Wei Chen, from Wuhan University and the DreamX Team at Alibaba Group. The question it tackles is finding a specific object in a city-scale 3D point cloud from a natural language description, and the claim is that the standard way of doing this is structurally wrong. Today, city-scale grounding is usually candidate ranking: run instance segmentation first, get a bag of proposals, then score each candidate against the text. Urbex argues that turns an inherently sequential search problem into a one-shot retrieval problem, and instead lets a vision language model agent roam the scene, querying landmarks, zooming into regions, rendering oblique views, and only committing a bounding box when it has enough evidence.

The problem is real and the failure mode is concrete. A city-scale outdoor scene can contain hundreds of thousands to tens of millions of points, while indoor grounding methods downsample to about fifty thousand points, discarding the fine geometry small urban objects need. Referring expressions are also far more abstract outdoors, something like, a white car parked next to the orange car to the left of the West Midlands Police Custody Suite. That demands reasoning over named buildings and road directions, not just fine-grained visual matching. The ranking pipeline copes by precomputing candidates, but then its accuracy is hostage to proposal quality and its inference cost grows with the number of candidates you have to score. Urbex sidesteps both: it receives no candidate set at inference time and outputs a free-form box in a bird's-eye-view-aligned frame that the environment projects back to world coordinates.

The interface is the interesting part. Every scene is exposed as an interactive multi-view environment built around a global bird's-eye image and a consistent view-to-world coordinate mapping. There are three tools. Landmark Query resolves a named building or road to its world position and its projection in a view, which anchors the search when the expression contains proper nouns. Zoom-in takes a 2D crop box, maps it back to the original high-resolution bird's-eye image, whose long side can exceed ten thousand pixels, and returns a local crop with a fresh transform, recovering detail that is invisible in the downsampled global view. Oblique Rendering renders a candidate region from a chosen cardinal direction, giving a 3D-aware check on shape and height that the top-down view cannot provide. The policy is a vision language model that sees the current views plus the full interaction history and either emits a tool call or commits a box.


A few implementation details matter if you plan to build this. The agent expresses boxes in a normalized 2D coordinate frame, and the environment owns the view-to-world transform, so the model never has to reason in metric 3D directly; that keeps the policy's action space small and consistent across views. Because every tool response, including view identifiers and coordinate conventions, is appended to the interaction history, the model can refer back to earlier views and maintain coordinate consistency while switching between global and local evidence, which is most of the reason a prompted baseline with this interface still gets non-trivial accuracy at all. The evaluation protocol is worth noting: in the with-ground-truth setting, Urbex still outputs a free-form box and matching happens after prediction, ranking candidates by 2D IoU with the predicted box, so Hit at k measures target identification without a minimum overlap requirement; the thresholded Hit at 1 and direct overlap metrics are reported separately. On the timing side, all numbers include model generation and online tool execution but exclude offline scene preparation, and the oblique cache build cost is reported separately rather than amortized into per-example time.

Training is pure reinforcement learning on CityRefer, no supervised trajectory demonstrations. The episode is a finite-horizon decision process with a terminal reward made of three terms: an IoU term rewarding precise overlap, an exponential center-distance term that keeps a dense learning signal alive when overlap is zero, which is the norm early in training on scenes this large, and a step cost that penalizes long trajectories. Notably there is no discrete success bonus, so the reward grows smoothly with localization quality instead of introducing a threshold cliff. On top of that sit lightweight tool-level shaping rewards: zoom-in gets positive reward when its center moves closer to the target and negative when it moves away, oblique rendering gets reward proportional to overlap with the target region, both capped so they stay shaping signals. Invalid tool names and malformed arguments get negative rewards. All of these are privileged training signals derived from ground truth, never visible to the agent at inference time. Optimization is Group Relative Policy Optimization, so there is no separate value network; the baseline is estimated from a group of concurrent rollouts of the same query, with advantages normalized by the group mean and standard deviation and a clipped objective with a token-level KL penalty to a reference policy.

Results on CityRefer, the main benchmark with novel-description and novel-object splits. In the setting where ranking baselines may use ground-truth candidate boxes, Urbex gets the best Hit at 1 on both splits, around 25.8 on the novel-description split and 23.9 on novel-object, versus 23.4 and 14.2 for the strongest ranking baseline, CityAnchor. CityAnchor still holds the best Hit at 5 on one split, which is an honest failure-mode difference: exhaustive ranking retains several plausible candidates, while precise single-box search can miss a target it never visited. The bigger gap is in the deployment-relevant setting without ground-truth candidates, where Urbex scores 15.6 for 2D IoU and 11.0 for 3D IoU on novel-description, and a proposal-based pipeline built from a segmentation model trained on fifteen other scenes only reaches 4.2 and 2.4. Speed is the other headline: CityAnchor needs about 32 seconds per example because it scores many candidates, while Urbex takes about 3.2 seconds, an order of magnitude, because the tools cost only about 0.36 seconds per query. Building the optional oblique cache takes about 2.3 hours across the twelve scenes; rendering on demand instead runs an estimated 4.4 seconds per query.

Zero-shot transfer to STPLS3D-Refer is where the paper makes its strongest claim. Trained only on CityRefer, the policy is applied to entirely different geometry and appearance and beats every compared method on every reported accuracy metric. The authors are careful to note that aggregate results suggest, but do not by themselves prove, a reusable search strategy, so they also run a subset of 30 manually verified landmark-free queries, removing named geographic anchors, and the policy still generalizes. Two caveats worth carrying. First, the tool interface is a big part of the win: GPT-5.4 and Qwen3.6-plus, connected to the same tools, get non-trivial accuracy but stay far below the trained policy, so the interactive environment helps even strong prompted models, and most of the reported gain is the learned policy on top of that substrate. Second, the evaluation is in-domain and near-domain; the reward shaping, including the oblique rendering reward, depends on ground-truth targets being available, which is a training-time privilege that does not survive if your deployment scene has no annotations. If you are building this, the pieces to replicate are the bird's-eye plus transform environment, the three tools, GRPO with the smooth terminal reward, and the shaping terms, all of which are stated with enough detail to implement. The code is at github.com AMAP-ML Urbex.

The bottom line: recasting city-scale grounding as active search with a small set of coordinate-aligned tools is a sound and well-motivated design, and the 10 times inference speedup over the strongest ranking baseline is the result I would trust most. I would re-verify the absolute IoU numbers on my own scenes before adopting it, since the without-ground-truth setting is still only as good as the bird's-eye rendering quality of your particular dataset.
