## Cold Open

Here is a question the world-model community has mostly been avoiding: how do you trust a score that comes with no explanation? Today's paper is HarnessEval-W: Agentifying the Evaluation of Visual Worlds — first authors Weiliang Chen, Haowen Sun, and Jun Gao, joined by co-authors including Jialong Wu, Jiangran Lyu, and Fangfu Liu in a forty-four-author collaboration whose benchmark and tooling ship publicly under the MirroS Lab organization. Their diagnosis is blunt. Current world-model evaluation compresses every prediction into metrics computed brute-force — pixel distances and classifier scores with no reasoning chain anyone can inspect. When two video-world models disagree about whether a cup stays on the table after a robot arm sweeps past, you receive two numbers and zero arguments. This paper's bet is that the evaluator itself should be an agent: something that investigates a generated rollout the way a careful reviewer investigates a manuscript, and leaves behind an evidence trail you can audit line by line.

## The Problem Being Solved

World models are becoming the planning core of embodied systems. An agent imagines forward — if I grasp here, the scene evolves there — and acts on the imagination. As these generators improve, the bottleneck moves from making predictions to grading them, and grading is where the field quietly cut corners. The standard toolkit measures observation overlap frame by frame, plus learned classifiers for physics plausibility. Those scores hide three distinct failure modes inside one average: did the model see what changed, did it predict the right dynamics, and did it remember the object three seconds later? A single blended number answers none of those questions, and because nothing intermediate is written down, you cannot tell a lucky score from an earned one. Worse, leaderboard gaps between rival models often sit within the noise of the metric itself. The authors' position is that evaluation deserves the same treatment the agent community gave reasoning: decompose it, instrument it, and make each judgment reconstructible. Decomposition here is not pedantry. When a lab changes training data and the blended score moves, nobody can say whether perception improved while planning quietly regressed, or the reverse — the leaderboard rewards the illusion of progress. And because these numbers gate real deployment choices about which model drives next quarter's robots, an unauditable score is not merely inconvenient; it is ungovernable.

## The Mechanism

HarnessEval-W rebuilds evaluation as a pipeline with three coordinated pieces. First comes skill routing: each test case is classified into the capabilities it exercises, and a router assigns specialized sub-agents accordingly. Second, sub-agent reasoning: dedicated reasoners interrogate the prediction along three axes — Observation Quality, whether the model perceived the relevant changes at all; Transition Correctness, whether the predicted dynamics obey the physical and causal rules of the scenario; and World Persistence, whether objects, properties, and states survive across time the way reality would. Third, an evidence tree: every sub-agent's claims, checks, and citations get attached to a structured record, so a final aggregation step weighs documented findings rather than vibes. The three axes map onto how embodied systems actually fail. An evaluator blind to observation errors lets a planner act on a misread scene; one blind to transition errors blesses rollouts whose physics would never survive contact with a real robot; one blind to persistence signs off on worlds where objects quietly cease to exist between frames — fatal the moment a task spans minutes instead of seconds. Splitting the grade forces each failure species into the open instead of letting them cancel inside an average.

Two design choices deserve emphasis. Each case expands into eight detail settings — controlled variations that probe how deep the evaluated model's understanding actually runs, rather than accepting one glossy rollout as representative. And the routing decision depends only on the case, never on which model is being tested. That second rule is the fairness load-bearing wall: if routing shifted with the contestant, a clever model could steer its own examination toward favorable questions. Here the exam is fixed before the candidates walk in.

The team assembled eighteen world models and three hundred thirty cases spanning physical scenes, object interactions, and longer-horizon persistence tests, then asked whether the agentified judge tracks human judgment better than direct metric computation does.

## The Numbers

Start with the headline comparison on WBench-style physical scenarios. Direct brute-force scoring manages thirty-one point nine percent pairwise accuracy — barely better than a coin flip when deciding which of two model outputs is superior. HarnessEval-W reaches seventy-one point seven percent on the same pairings. That is not an incremental polish; it converts an unusable judge into a marginally trustworthy one, and the authors are candid that seventy percent is a floor being raised, not a ceiling reached.

Against human preference, the alignment study covers five thousand annotated A-to-B pairs. At the aggregate level — ranking whole models — agreement hits a Spearman correlation of zero point nine three, which is strong enough to trust league-table conclusions. Case by case, the correlation settles at zero point eight seven: most individual verdicts track human raters, with a residue of disagreement concentrated where even humans hesitate. That gap between zero point nine three and zero point eight seven carries a practical instruction: use this judge to rank models, where its reliability approaches human inter-rater territory, and treat any single case verdict as a strong hypothesis awaiting spot-check rather than gospel. The evidence tree makes such spot-checks cheap, because a human auditor reads the recorded argument instead of re-deriving the whole judgment from raw video.

Stability matters as much as accuracy, and here the robustness result stands out. Across perturbed variants of the same underlying questions, the spread between the judge's best and worst behavior narrows to a band four point nine times tighter than the baseline envelope. In plain terms: rerun the evaluation on a Thursday instead of a Tuesday and the ranking holds.

## What the Benchmark Reveals

The interesting output is diagnostic, not competitive. When the authors fine-tune a world model on additional interaction data, overall scores drift upward while the axis breakdown tells a sharper story: revisit consistency improves — the model better re-renders scenes it has seen — while commanded-interaction fidelity drops, meaning it gets worse at following novel instructions precisely. Capability moved sideways as much as forward. A scalar metric would have reported generic progress; the decomposed judge exposes the trade, which is exactly the information a practitioner choosing a planner needs. It converts model selection from a single number into a matching problem: a short-horizon rendering pipeline and a long-horizon manipulation stack should not automatically buy the same world model, and now they can tell the difference before committing.

## The Skeptical Read

Now the caveats, because they are substantive. The entire apparatus rests on language-model judges watching video — so any blind spot in the judge family becomes a systematic bias inherited silently by every score beneath it. Seventy-one point seven percent pairwise accuracy still misorders roughly one pairing in three. Case-level agreement of zero point eight seven means the evidence tree, however inspectable, sometimes documents the wrong conclusion with great confidence. There is also a circularity question worth pressing hard: judging generative world models with strong multimodal reasoners, when both lineages draw on overlapping data and architectures, risks grading a family portrait with a member of the same family. And forty-four authors across many institutions raises the mundane coordination risk that benchmark curation decisions diffuse beyond easy accountability — the project's public organization helps, but provenance of individual cases still merits auditing. One genuine mitigant: because the harness and the case set ship openly, third parties can rerun, slice, and contest results rather than accepting the leaderboard on faith — the same auditability this paper demands of evaluators, pointed back at itself. Finally, three hundred thirty cases is a respectable start and a small ceiling; axis-level conclusions rest on slices of that.

## Verdict

HarnessEval-W makes the strongest case yet that world-model evaluation should produce arguments, not just numbers, and its case-fixed routing plus evidence trees give the idea real engineering teeth. Treat its axis-level diagnoses as genuinely actionable, but treat its absolute rankings as provisional until judge independence from the judged — the circularity the paper acknowledges but cannot fully escape — gets its own benchmark.
