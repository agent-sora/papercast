Title: ZeroVLN: Learning to Navigate without Supervised Fine-Tuning of the VLM Brain
Authors: Zixuan Huang, Yang Zhou, Guli Zhang

ZeroVLN, by Zixuan Huang, Yang Zhou, and Guli Zhang, from Beihang University, Zhuoyu Technology, and Zhejiang University, is a slow-fast framework for vision-language navigation that learns to navigate with zero supervised fine-tuning of the vision-language model that acts as the agent's brain. The paper's motivating observation is that representative navigation methods fine-tune the VLM brain on navigation-domain data so that it can predict geometric goals for an action head, and that this adaptation substantially displaces the base model's weights and degrades its general visual capabilities. The paper reports that a representative SFT-based method, DualVLN, loses large fractions of its accuracy on a suite of visual and spatial benchmarks after navigation adaptation, and ZeroVLN's answer is to leave the brain essentially untouched and instead learn navigation through closed-loop reinforcement learning, with the brain communicating movement decisions to a trainable action expert through a natural-language tool interface.

The problem the paper is fixing is a real and well-documented one in the embodied-AI literature: domain adaptation by fine-tuning. If you take a general-purpose VLM and fine-tune it to predict navigation goals, the weights move a lot, and the model's general-purpose abilities, its ability to do visual question answering, spatial reasoning, and other perception tasks that had nothing to do with navigation, degrade. The paper quantifies this with a before-and-after comparison on a battery of visual and spatial benchmarks, and the numbers are striking: on some benchmarks the SFT-adapted model drops by more than 80 percent of its original accuracy. That is the cost of teaching a general model a specific navigation skill by directly training it on that skill. ZeroVLN's premise, borrowed explicitly from the DeepSeek-R1-Zero line of work that showed reasoning can emerge from a base model through reinforcement learning without prior supervised fine-tuning, is that you can get the navigation capability through interaction and reward instead of through supervised adaptation, and in doing so preserve the base model's general abilities.

The architecture separates the two timescales that give the framework its name. The slow policy is the VLM brain, which makes movement decisions but is never fine-tuned on navigation data. The fast policy is a flow-matching action expert that executes continuous motion. The two are connected by a semantic tool-calling interface rather than by a direct goal-prediction head. Instead of the brain predicting a geometric goal coordinate, it issues a tool call that specifies a target, its relative location, and a local plan, in natural language, and the action expert translates that semantic request into continuous motion. The brain can also inspect the scene with a turn tool and terminate navigation with a stop tool, so the interface is a small set of natural-language actions rather than a single numeric output. The action expert is conditioned on the brain's request through learnable query embeddings that the VLM contextualizes, so the expert receives a semantic representation of the request rather than a raw coordinate.

The training is two-stage, and the ordering matters. In the first stage, the brain is frozen and the action expert is adapted to carry out the brain's own movement requests. Reference trajectories are collected through online rollouts with the brain fixed, and a privileged trajectory planner, which has access to a semantic map derived from the scene mesh, grounds those requests in executable motion. The resulting reference trajectories are paired with the originating requests to give the action expert semantically aligned supervision, so it learns to map the brain's natural-language movement requests onto continuous paths that actually work in the environment. In the second stage, the brain's movement decisions and the action expert's continuous execution are jointly optimized through multi-round closed-loop reinforcement learning. Because long-horizon navigation gives sparse success signals, the reward design uses complementary process and outcome rewards, so the agent gets credit for useful progress even on episodes where it does not reach the goal, which is the standard fix for the sparsity problem in long-horizon RL but it is done here with the specific structure of separating progress from completion.

The results are on the standard VLN-CE continuous-environment benchmarks, R2R and RxR. The paper reports that ZeroVLN substantially outperforms representative SFT-based hierarchical methods trained on the same navigation episodes, which is the key comparison, because it is not a comparison against a method trained on more data or a different split, it is the same navigation episodes, and the difference is the training methodology. The weight-displacement number is the headline for the preservation claim: the paper reports that ZeroVLN induces only about 0.015 percent VLM weight displacement, which is the quantitative version of the claim that the brain is left essentially untouched. There is a trade-off the paper is honest about: on R2R and RxR, ZeroVLN achieves a higher overall success rate than a representative SFT baseline, NaVILA, at 66.7 and 64.6 percent versus 51.8 and 55.6 percent, but a lower success-weighted path length, at 29.2 and 28.1 percent versus 41.8 and 43.0 percent. So it gets to the destination more often but takes less direct paths, which is a reasonable signature of a policy that learned navigation through interaction rather than by imitating expert trajectories, because it has not inherited the expert's path efficiency.

Now the skeptical reading. The first caveat is the success-versus-path-length trade-off, which the paper reports openly and which is the real cost of the zero-SFT approach. A navigation agent that reaches the goal more often but wanders more is not unambiguously better, because in a physical deployment the path length is a real cost, battery, time, wear, and the SPL metric that measures path efficiency is substantially lower. The paper frames the higher overall success rate as the win, but whether that trade is acceptable depends entirely on your application, and a reader who needs efficient paths, not just successful ones, should weight the SPL drop heavily. The zero-SFT claim buys you preserved general capabilities and higher success at the price of less efficient navigation, and that is a genuine engineering trade, not a free lunch.

The second caveat is the privileged trajectory planner in the first training stage. The planner that grounds the brain's requests in executable motion uses a semantic map derived from the scene mesh, which is information the agent does not have at test time. That is a standard and defensible training technique, privileged information for bootstrapping, but it means the action expert's initial competence is built on a supervision signal that comes from a map the deployed agent cannot access, and the question of how much of that competence survives the transition from privileged training to unprivileged test-time inference is not fully isolated in the paper. You would want an ablation that drops the privileged planner and measures how much the action expert's quality degrades, because that determines how much of the first stage's benefit is real transferable competence versus a training-time scaffold.

For implementation, the transferable pieces are the semantic tool interface between a frozen VLM and a trainable action expert, which is a clean way to get a general model to drive a low-level controller without fine-tuning it, and the two-stage training order, adapt the expert to the brain's requests first, then jointly optimize with closed-loop RL. The process-plus-outcome reward design is the third, and it is the piece most likely to transfer to your own long-horizon embodied task, since sparsity is a universal problem there. The thing to verify before adopting is the path-efficiency cost and the dependence on the privileged planner, because those are the two factors that determine whether the zero-SFT approach is net positive for your specific navigation or embodied-control task.

To summarize the bottom line. ZeroVLN is a well-motivated slow-fast navigation framework whose core contribution is learning vision-language navigation with essentially no supervised fine-tuning of the VLM brain, using a semantic tool interface and closed-loop RL, and it does so while preserving the base model's general capabilities and beating same-data SFT baselines on overall success. Its limits are the real cost of that approach, a substantially lower path-efficiency score, and a first training stage that relies on a privileged scene-mesh planner the deployed agent does not have, so weigh the success-versus-path-length trade-off for your application and ablate the privileged planner before trusting the zero-SFT competence to transfer.
