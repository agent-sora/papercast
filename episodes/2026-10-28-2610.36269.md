Cross-modal Steering Vectors Exist Across Both Image and Text Generation, by Donghao Zeng, Nishant Subramani, and Tamar Rott Shaham, from Mila at McGill University, Carnegie Mellon University, MIT CSAIL, and Northeastern University, with the senior author Benno Krojer shared across Mila and Northeastern. This is an interpretability paper, and the question it operationalizes is one of the big open ones in multimodal modeling: are concepts implemented once and shared across a unified model's modalities, or does each modality get its own separate machinery for the same idea?

The method is deliberately causal rather than correlational, and that distinction is the whole contribution. Instead of asking whether the text and image representations of a concept look similar, which prior work on representational similarity does, they ask whether a vector that steers generation in one modality still works when you add it to tokens of the other modality. A steering vector is a direction in the model's hidden space: you find one that pushes generation toward a concept, say formal language or a large object size, and then you test whether the same vector, added to the other modality's activations, produces the corresponding effect there. Steering is causal because the vector is something you act on, not something you merely observe, so a positive transfer result is a stronger claim about shared structure than a similarity score ever is. The way they extract the vector is standard but worth noting: for a given concept, they collect activations on examples that do and do not have the concept, take the difference between the two mean activation directions, and that difference is the steering direction, which is then added to the activations of a held-out generation to measure its effect. The whole point of doing this in one modality and testing it in the other is that the vector never sees the target modality at extraction time, so there is no way the transfer could be an artifact of the vector being fitted to the image activations, which is the confound that a correlational similarity analysis cannot rule out.

The models studied are unified multimodal models, the kind that share a single set of parameters for both language and image generation, and they use three of them: UniAR, Emu3.5, and Liquid. The concepts they steer are diverse, including emotion, age, and object size, and the result is that steering vectors transfer zero-shot in both directions, text to image and image to text, across all three models. The asymmetry is a real and useful finding: text to image is easier than image to text. Text-derived vectors steer images for nearly every concept, and in two of the three models they actually steer images better than the image-derived vectors do, with a recovery above one, which they attribute to the image-derived vectors being weak in those two models rather than to unusually strong transfer. The image-to-text direction is only widely successful in one model, UniAR, which recovers up to ninety six percent of the intra-modality effect, while the other two only get close in late layers. The finding also generalizes beyond the concepts you would expect to be shared: even for a concept that is highly specific to language, like the difference between formal and colloquial register, the steering vector for it induces a consistent and interpretable effect in the image modality, namely formal clothing and design, which is a striking example of a purely linguistic concept leaving a geometric trace in the image representation.

The measurement of transfer is worth spelling out, because it is what makes the result defensible. They measure a steering vector's effect as a recovery ratio: how much of the change in concept rate you get in the target modality, relative to the change you get when you steer the same modality with its own native vector. A recovery of one means the cross-modal vector works as well as the native one, above one means it works better, and below one means it is weaker. They also report the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve as a second, more robust measure, because a single steering strength is a noisy statistic. The concept rate itself is measured with a separate evaluator, so the thing being steered and the thing being measured are not the same model, which removes the circularity that plagues some interpretability claims. On top of that they check for a quality trade-off, asking whether steering the concept strongly comes at the cost of the rest of the generation, and they find that the trade-off is present but modest, which is the result that makes steering vectors practical rather than just an interesting artifact.

The geometry is the second finding, and it is the one I would take away. There is a clear modality gap separating the text and image representations, which everyone already knew, but the steering vectors that transfer well across modalities tend to point in similar directions for the same concept, even though the underlying representations live in different places. For five of the eight concepts they study, the cosine similarity between the text-extracted and image-extracted steering directions is between zero point one four and zero point six zero, far above a random-direction baseline of plus or minus zero point zero three. The concepts that do not transfer, size, near-far, and a spatial left-right axis, have directions around zero, which is a clean negative result. Emotion is the concept that transfers most evenly, with a mean recovery of zero point nine five in both directions, and age transfers into images particularly well, in the range of one point zero two to one point eight seven across models and layer ranges. What this tells you is that the shared structure is not in the raw representations, which are separated by a modality gap, but in the directions of change that the representations support, which is a more specific and more useful claim than a generic statement that the modalities are aligned, because it says exactly which geometry is shared and which is not, and it gives you a measurable quantity, the direction similarity, that you can use to test whether a concept is genuinely shared in a new model.

Here is the skeptical read. The transfer is real and it is causal, which is the right experimental design, but the effect is concept-dependent, and the paper is honest about which concepts fail: spatial left-right essentially does not transfer in any setting except moderate image-to-image steering in one model, and size transfers strongly in one direction and barely in the other. The recovery above one in two of the three models is explained by weak image-derived vectors, which is a reasonable explanation but it means the headline that text vectors are better for steering images is partly an artifact of those two models having weak image steering to begin with, and I would want to see that on a model where image-to-image steering is strong before trusting the asymmetry as a general property of unified models. The layer-dependence is also a real limitation: image-to-text transfer only works in late layers for two of the three models, which means the shared structure the paper is finding is a late-layer phenomenon, and whether it exists earlier in the network, where the representations might be more genuinely shared, is not established. The concept set is eight, which is a reasonable pilot but a small one, and the conclusion that unified models implement concepts cross-modally is strongest for the handful of concepts that happen to transfer, namely emotion, age, and formal versus colloquial style, and weaker for the spatial and size concepts that do not.

The bottom line: this is a small, careful, well-designed interpretability result that replaces a correlational similarity argument with a causal one, and the clean negative results, which concepts fail to transfer and at which layers, are as informative as the positive ones. If you work on unified multimodal models and want a cheap probe for whether two modalities actually share a concept, the cross-modal steering test is a concrete tool, and the geometric signature, well-transferring vectors pointing in similar directions despite a modality gap, is a useful way to think about what unification looks like in activation space.
