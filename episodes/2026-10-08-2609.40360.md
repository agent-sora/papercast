Semifactual Credit-Augmented Policy Optimization is from Junshu Pan, Zhizhang Fu, and Shulin Huang, at Zhejiang University and Westlake University, with the full team of eight authors across Zhejiang, Westlake, the Shanghai Innovation Institute, and the Shanghai AI Laboratory, and Yue Zhang as the corresponding author. The paper works on reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, the RLVR setup that has driven a lot of the recent gains in LLM reasoning, and it targets a specific weakness: even after RLVR training, the model's predictions are still sensitive to task-irrelevant features in the prompt. They investigate this with what they call semifactual prompt interventions, where you change the surface features of a problem, the wording, the connectives, the framing, while preserving the underlying problem and its answer, and they use the sensitivity under those changes as a signal for where the model is reasoning and where it is latching onto spurious cues.

The motivating observation is about Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, which is the standard RLVR objective. GRPO derives an advantage from the outcome, and it assigns that same outcome-derived advantage to every token in the response. That means the token that actually carried the reasoning gets the same credit as a token that is just a discourse connective, a filler, a restatement of the prompt. The paper's claim is that this coarse credit assignment can reinforce spurious dependence alongside useful reasoning, because the tokens that are most sensitive to the irrelevant prompt features, the ones that should get less credit, get the same advantage as the stable, genuinely-reasoning tokens. So the problem is not that RLVR does not work, it works, but the credit it assigns is at the wrong granularity.

The method, SCAPO, is a causally inspired variant of GRPO that folds semifactual stability into the token-level credit. The procedure is this. For a fixed response, you apply a semifactual intervention to the prompt, changing the irrelevant features, and you measure how much each token's probability drifts under that intervention. Tokens that barely move are stable, they are not depending on the surface features, and tokens that drift a lot are the ones latching onto spurious cues. You compute a normalized stability score from that drift. During early training, SCAPO uses these stability scores to reduce the advantage for the relatively unstable tokens, while explicitly granting no additional credit for stability alone, which is an important detail, because it means the method is a penalty on instability, not a reward for stability, and that asymmetry is what keeps it from just teaching the model to produce boring, invariant text. The signal is used as a modulation of the existing outcome advantage, not as a separate objective, so it drops into the GRPO training loop with relatively little change.

There is also a purely inference-time result, which is the part I would trust most because it is the cleanest. If you suppress the high-drift token candidates during decoding, without updating any weights at all, you improve reasoning accuracy. On the 4B model, that decoding change takes accuracy from 15.8 percent to 30.0 percent on a 1,000-question evaluation, a 14.2 point gain, from the frozen base model before any RL. That is a large, weight-free effect, and it is the direct demonstration that the token-level sensitivity is a real signal, not just an artifact of the training procedure, because the same drift measurement helps at inference time on an untrained model.

The intervention itself is worth describing, because it is the part that makes this a method rather than just an observation. A semifactual prompt intervention takes a problem and rewrites the task-irrelevant features, the phrasing, the discourse connectives, the surrounding framing, while holding the mathematical problem and its answer fixed. They sample a large number of these intervened variants per problem and run the frozen model on them, and the token-level drift is measured as how much each response token's probability changes across the variants. The analysis of that drift is the empirical core: the sensitivity is not uniform, it is concentrated. Discourse connectives and framing words drift much more than the mathematical content, with the paper reporting that the connective tokens show mean drifts of roughly two to three times the overall mean, while the mathematical symbols and numbers show drifts well below the mean, around zero point four times. That concentration is what makes the stability score informative, because the unstable tokens are a small, identifiable subset, and it is also what makes the decoding fix work, because suppressing exactly those high-drift candidates removes the spurious dependence without touching the tokens that carry the reasoning.

The trained results are on Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-1.7B-Base, trained on the DAPO-Math-17K dataset of about 17,000 problems with rule-based rewards and R1-style prompting, with response lengths up to 16,384 tokens. SCAPO improves AIME accuracy over GRPO by 5.63 points on the 4B model and 4.17 points on the 1.7B model, with HMMT improving by 4.46 and 2.98 points respectively, and on the out-of-distribution benchmarks it goes from 36.26 to 42.45 percent at 4B and from 27.42 to 31.25 percent at 1.7B. At 4B it is best on six of eight metrics, and at 1.7B it is best on all eight. The code is released.

One more detail on how the stability score is actually used in training, because the asymmetry is the whole point. The score modulates the existing outcome advantage during the early part of training, where the policy is still moving the most and the spurious dependence is being reinforced hardest, and it tapers off as training progresses, so the method is a shaped credit signal in the early phase rather than a permanent objective term. The reason for restricting it to early training is that once the policy has settled, the token-level drift structure changes, and a stability penalty applied to a converged policy would start to punish the very tokens that now carry stable, correct reasoning, so the schedule is doing real work, it is not just a convenience. The fact that they report gains on out-of-distribution benchmarks in addition to the in-distribution AIME and HMMT numbers is the piece that matters most, because it is direct evidence that the finer credit assignment is improving generalization, not just in-sample accuracy.

A skeptical read. The inference-time drift result is the strongest piece, and it is exactly the kind of weight-free control that makes me take the rest more seriously, because it shows the sensitivity signal is not an artifact of the training loop. But I would want to understand the cost of the stability measurement, because to compute token-level drift under semifactual interventions you need to run the model on the intervened prompts, and doing that during training, at every step, for credit assignment, is a nontrivial compute overhead, and the paper does not make the wall-clock cost of SCAPO versus plain GRPO prominent enough for me to judge whether the 5 point gain is worth the extra inference. The second thing I would re-verify is the claim about no credit for stability alone, because a penalty on unstable tokens and a reward for stable tokens can look identical in the aggregate and behave very differently in the limit, and I would want to see that the trained model has not just learned to be invariant but has actually gotten better at the reasoning, which the out-of-distribution gains suggest it has, but the distribution-in numbers are the ones to scrutinize. The third: this is a 1.7B and 4B result, and credit-assignment granularity effects can change at scale, where the token drift structure under interventions may look different for a larger model that already has less spurious dependence.

Bottom line: if you are doing RLVR and you want finer-grained credit than the flat outcome advantage GRPO gives you, using semifactual intervention drift as a token-level stability signal is a well-motivated, causally-grounded direction, and the weight-free decoding gain is the piece to trust. Before you adopt the full training method, check its inference cost per step and confirm the gains hold on your own problems, because the elegance of the signal does not by itself settle whether it is worth the extra compute at training time.
