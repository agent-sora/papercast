The paper is "Relevance Does Not Imply Applicability: Experience Activation for Personal GUI Agents," by Fuyao Zhang, Xuan Wang, and Zherui Li, from Nanyang Technological University and Alibaba. It has 18 Hugging Face upvotes, fifth on the day, and it attacks a specific and under-examined assumption in personalized GUI agents: that if you retrieve history relevant to a task and append it to the policy's context, it keeps being useful for every decision in the episode. The paper's empirical claim is that this help is almost entirely spent at the first step.

The setting is personal GUI agents that operate apps on a user's own device. Much of what the user wants is never stated. "Order my usual coffee" names neither the app, the drink, nor the store. Two use cases matter. Personalized execution: resolving an ambiguous instruction using the user's history and carrying it out the way this user would. Proactive suggestion: deciding whether a recurring routine is due before the user asks at all. The standard approach to both is task-level retrieval — pull the records, profiles, or intent memories most similar to the task, append them to the context, and let the policy figure it out. The implicit assumption is that task-level relevance is a good proxy for decision-level applicability. The paper's diagnosis is that it is not, and they show where exactly it breaks.

For execution, the mechanism of the failure is that the instruction stays fixed while the screen changes at every step. A retrieved trajectory tells the agent where to begin — it strongly improves the opening decision, which commits to an application — but it does not tell the agent which past action fits the current screen. The paper quantifies the decay: retrieved history strongly improves the first step but provides little sustained benefit over the remaining 90 percent of steps. For proactive suggestion, the same routine can be retrieved at 8 a.m. at home and during an evening commute, even though it is appropriate only in the former, and even methods that organize routines by time and scenario leave the policy to decide whether the routine actually applies. The gap is between relevance, which is determined at the task level, and applicability, which depends on the situation at decision time.

Their solution, ExpActivator, is training-free, and it recasts personalization as a decision-time selection problem. It operates at two granularities. Step-level activation, for execution: after task-relevant records are retrieved once, every new screen is matched against the historical states of those records in the frozen GUI backbone's visual latent space, and the action associated with the best-matching historical state is supplied as a reference. The match is recomputed after every observation, so the reference can jump between historical steps and trajectories without assuming the current run follows their step order. The screen representation is built from the backbone's pooled visual token blocks, mean-centered against a calibration set and L2-normalized, and the compatibility score is the cosine similarity between the current screen and each historical state. Critically, the historical action is a reference, not a prescribed operation — the frozen policy still sees the current screen to judge applicability and locate the target. Task-level activation, for suggestion: repeated intents are grouped into routine neighborhoods by embedding similarity, and each routine's support under the current context combines scenario agreement with temporal proximity, using a circular 24-hour distance so that 1 a.m. and 11 p.m. are close. A routine is only eligible if it clears absolute support thresholds for total occurrences, same-scenario occurrences, and contextual match; if no routine qualifies, the agent abstains. Otherwise it ranks by mean contextual support per occurrence and supplies the most representative same-scenario intent as a reference for the frozen backbone to generate a suggestion — again a reference, not an authorization to act.

The evaluation is on AndroidIntent, built from long-term mobile records of 82 users across 135 applications and 13 scenario categories, with the chronological first 80 percent of each user's records forming history and the rest held out. Personalized execution has 775 episodes and 7,915 steps with deliberately ambiguous instructions; proactive suggestion has 315 states, 215 positive and 100 negative. They test across four frozen open GUI backbones with different visual encoders and GUI training — Qwen3-VL-8B, MAI-UI-8B, Qwen3.5-9B, and GUI-Owl-1.5-8B — plus the proprietary QwenVL-Max as an external reference. Every method reads the same history and shares model revisions and inference settings, so the comparison is paired step by step. The baselines are the benchmark's own history mechanisms: recent records, similarity retrieval, an offline LLM-written profile, and a hierarchical intent memory.

The results are consistent across all four backbones, which is the point of using four. ExpActivator is best on action-type accuracy, step success rate, and cumulative success rate on every backbone. On Qwen3-VL-8B, step success rate goes from the 22 to 23 range for the best retrieval baselines to 30.9, and cumulative success from about 41 to 47.9. The paper reports an average 28 percent relative improvement in within-trajectory step success, the best personalized execution on every backbone while using about one-fifth as many history tokens, and a Matthews correlation coefficient for proactive suggestion roughly 2.3 times that of the strongest baseline. The MCC number is the one to sit with, because MCC is zero for any rule that cannot separate positive from negative states, and several of the baselines — plain retrieval, the LLM profile — sit at or near zero, meaning they essentially cannot tell when a routine is due from when it is not.

Two pieces of evidence in the appendix make the mechanism concrete rather than hand-wavy. The threshold-sensitivity analysis of the task-level support score shows a plateau rather than a sharp peak: as the minimum-occurrence threshold n_min rises, the suggestion quality holds steady and then falls away, to 73.24 at n_min of 16 and 58.95 at 24, which the authors characterize as a coverage effect — requiring more occurrences prunes the routines that should fire — rather than an instability in the scoring. The case studies show the step-level mechanism doing what it claims on individual episodes: in a next-bus task, each current screen activates the most similar recorded state among eight task-relevant trajectories, with cosine similarities of 0.98, 0.87, 0.96, and 0.93 across the steps, and the reference moves from one past trip to another as the screen changes while the frozen policy's taps land on the activated target. For proactive suggestion, the figures show the abstention path working at the hour level: routines with a fixed morning schedule activate in the morning hours and abstain otherwise, and a routine that belongs on a vehicle does not fire for the user who is on the street, even when the times overlap.

The skeptical read. First, the gains are large in relative terms but the absolute step success rates are still in the twenties and thirties, so "best on the benchmark" is doing real work in that framing, and the underlying task remains hard. Second, the entire method is a frozen-backbone wrapper: it does not train the policy, which is both its selling point — you can bolt it onto an existing agent — and its ceiling, because the reference-action mechanism is only as good as the backbone's willingness to follow a reference it does not fully trust. Third, the screen-matching lives in the backbone's visual latent space, and the paper calibrates the visual layout and thresholds on held-out history per backbone, so the transferability to a new device or UI family is an open question the four-backbone result does not settle. If you adopt this, the piece worth replicating is the recompute-every-step reference rather than the specific encoder, because the diagnosis — relevance spent at step one — is the generalizable finding.

Bottom line: a clean, training-free result that pinpoints where task-level retrieval actually helps and replaces a one-time append with a decision-time selection, with consistent gains across four heterogeneous backbones and a striking abstention result on proactive suggestion. The limits are modest absolute success rates and a design that inherits whatever trust its frozen backbone places in a supplied reference, but the diagnosis is the durable contribution: if you build a personalized agent, measure where your retrieval help is actually spent, because step one is probably not where it stays.
