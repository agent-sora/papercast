The paper is "Scaling Agentic Data for Long-Horizon Terminal Intelligence," by Shun Zou, Ziyu Ma, and Yi Zou, from the University of Science and Technology of China, AMAP at Alibaba Group, and the Southern University of Science and Technology. This one is about a data synthesis engine, and it carries the day's top Hugging Face upvote count at 64, which is telling, because the terminal-agent training data space has been getting crowded with claims and thin numbers.

The problem they start from is concrete. Terminal agents have moved past running individual commands toward completing multi-stage professional work, but the training trajectories used to teach them are mostly short. The paper quantifies this: they sample 300 tasks from several open-source terminal-task synthesis methods, run them under the same teacher protocol, and measure interaction length. Prior datasets average between 3.9 and 15.6 turns per trajectory. What a long-horizon task actually requires is a chain of behaviors — exploration, hypothesis testing, revision, cross-validation — that unfold across dozens of steps and feed back on each other. The catch, and this is the interesting part of their motivation, is that synthesizing a hard task does not automatically produce those chains. If the environment you build already contains the solution to a subproblem, the agent skips the exploration you wanted it to do. The task is solvable, but it never exercised the intended behavior.

Their engine, TerminalHorizon, has two stages. Stage one is challenge-guided synthesis. A planner agent picks research directions to fill coverage gaps in the behavioral-pattern taxonomy, which they define as 27 patterns across five families: exploration and judgment, diagnosis and error correction, construction, verification, and strategy coordination. A deep-research agent grounds each brief in real documentation and repositories, and a construction agent builds a full task package: public instruction, environment, reference solution, private verifier, and execution config. Then an independent challenger — a model that had no part in building the task — attempts it. The trajectory is analyzed for gaps: did the environment pre-solve the core work? For instance, if the task asks the agent to implement a core function but the environment already provides it, the remaining work reduces to changing a constant. The diagnosis drives a reconstruction that restores the missing work while keeping the public specification intact, and the loop repeats until the intended behavior actually shows up in execution. Stage two is progressive horizon expansion: an evolution agent extends an accepted task under the same goal, adding requirements that depend on earlier results — broader roles, deeper mechanisms, richer evidence, or method upgrades — so the behavioral patterns continue across stages instead of ending at first success. The two stages together are what make the trajectories long: the challenge loop makes each task genuinely harder, and the horizon expansion makes each trajectory genuinely longer, and both properties are measured, not assumed, in the final dataset statistics.

The output is TerminalHorizon-3K: 1,535 task environment packages and 2,852 retained training trajectories after filtering, spanning 25 professional domains including chips, physics, and astronomy. The trajectories average 52.8 interaction turns, with a 90th percentile of 98, and a median feedback chain of 25 turns where actions are linked by terminal output. Each trajectory contains on average 8.6 of the core behavioral patterns. The collection protocol is worth noting for reproducibility: two trajectories were collected per task using DeepSeek-V4-Pro as the teacher model with the Terminus2 harness, then filtered by three criteria described in the appendix, and the authors report rigorous benchmark decontamination before training, which is the step most synthetic-data papers skip or hand-wave. They also ran the same 100-task sample with GLM-5.2 as a comparison teacher: mean turns of 46.1 against 53.5, mean feedback chain of 19.2 against 30.3, exact-repetition rate of 15.8 percent against 13.2, and a task pass rate of 44.0 against 50.0, which is why they picked DeepSeek for the actual dataset.

The training recipe is a plain supervised fine-tune, which is the part worth the standing-recipe attention. They fine-tune Qwen3.5-27B on the 2,852 trajectories with a maximum sequence length of 262,144 tokens, fused AdamW at a learning rate of one times ten to the minus five, weight decay 0.1, betas 0.9 and 0.95, cosine schedule after a 5 percent warmup, gradient clipping at 1.0, 3 epochs in BF16 with DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 on 144 H20-141GB GPUs across 18 nodes. That is a small dataset by agentic-data standards, and the results are the paper's real claim.

On Terminal-Bench 1.0, 2.0, and 2.1, the model scores 55.0, 55.8, and 60.67, improving over the base Qwen3.5-27B by 8.7, 14.2, and 13.85 points respectively. The base model sits at 46.3 on Terminal-Bench 1.0. Against the nearest same-base competitor, Terminal-Universe-27B, which trained on 32,000 trajectories, TerminalHorizon-27B wins by 2.2 points on Terminal-Bench 1.0 with less than one-tenth of the training data. On the agentic side, AutomationBench pass rate jumps from 5.5 to 17.2 and the partial score from 26.4 to 49.7, VitaBench cross-domain improves from 20.5 to 31.8, and tau-bench airline and retail move by 5.5 and 2.6 points. General reasoning does not degrade: MATH-500 goes from 96.9 to 98.9, AIME 2025 from 86.7 to 93.3. Scaling from 1,000 to 3,000 trajectories raises Terminal-Bench 2.1 to 60.67 with no sign of saturation, and the same recipe applied to Qwen3.5-35B and 122B variants yields double-digit gains in each case. They close with 12-hour recursive-self-improvement case studies where the model continues past an initial solution and folds improvements into final deliverables.

Now the skeptical read. First, the data-efficiency comparison is the strongest result in the paper, but it is a comparison against other synthetic trajectory datasets, not against a careful curriculum of real human terminal work, and the field has not established that human-sourced long trajectories exist at scale anyway. Second, the entire pipeline is teacher-dependent: 2,852 trajectories generated by one teacher model, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, mean the student's ceiling is bounded by the teacher's, and their own GLM comparison shows the teacher choice moves trajectory quality in the exact dimensions they claim to care about. Third, the behavioral-pattern taxonomy is self-defined and the 27-pattern analysis is descriptive, not causal; nothing in the ablations isolates which patterns actually drive the Terminal-Bench gains versus which are correlated with mere trajectory length. A 52-turn trajectory is long, and length itself is a confound they only partially control for. If you want to adopt the idea, the piece to replicate is the independent-challenger loop, because that is the mechanism that distinguishes this from "generate harder tasks." But before betting a training run on it, I would want to see the decontamination details behind their benchmark claims and an ablation that holds trajectory length fixed while varying pattern coverage.

One practical note for anyone considering this as a data source: the task packages follow the standard five-component formulation, with the public instruction and environment being everything the agent sees at runtime, and the verifier's completion contract required to be satisfiable from the public requirements alone — scoring cannot demand something the agent cannot discover. That constraint is the right one for terminal tasks, because it is precisely what prevents the verifier from silently encoding knowledge the agent was supposed to explore for, and it is the reason the packages are usable as an independent data source rather than a benchmark-locked artifact.

Bottom line: this is a competent, well-documented open training recipe — real hyperparameters, real hardware, a small and inspectable dataset — and the data-efficiency result on Terminal-Bench is the kind of number worth taking seriously. The open questions are whether the gains survive a stricter length-controlled ablation and whether the teacher model is the quiet rate-limiting step in the whole pipeline.
