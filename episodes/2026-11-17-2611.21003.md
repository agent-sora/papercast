Today's paper is K2-Horizon: A Frontier Fleet of Open Source Language Models, from the K2 Team of the Institute of Foundation Models at the Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence. This is a foundation-model release, and it is the kind of release that is unusual in what it ships alongside the weights: six fully open language models from a 0.9 billion dense model up to a 375 billion parameter mixture-of-experts with 23 billion active, plus the full development history — intermediate checkpoints from every training stage, the pre-training data, the training logs, and all the training, evaluation, and infrastructure code. The report is a 150 page technical document, and I will walk through the architecture, the data, and the benchmark claims, and where I would apply pressure.

The architecture story has three pieces. First, the family-wide norming: grouped root-mean-square normalization across all six models. Second, and the more interesting contribution, Mixture-of-Value Attention, which the authors call MoVA: it applies the mixture-of-experts idea to the attention value projections. Standard attention computes values from the hidden state with a single value matrix; MoVA routes across several value experts, so the value pathway gets the same conditional sparsity that the feed-forward pathway gets in a standard MoE. The 36B-A4B model in the fleet uses MoVA plus a softplus attention gate — a gate that is more flexible than the sigmoid alternatives and, the authors argue, theoretically motivated. The headline architecture result: the 36B-A4B, with 4 billion active parameters, nearly matches a 32 billion dense model trained on the same data, with one-seventh the active parameters. Third, Uno, a set of lossless diffusion adapters that speed up decoding by up to 2.2 times without a separate draft model — the word lossless here means the decoding distribution is unchanged, so the speedup costs no quality by construction, which is a strong claim for a speculative-style method to make.

The pre-training design is where the transparency claim gets specific. Four of the six models share one 22 trillion token pre-training corpus and one recipe, with hyperparameters scaled together across sizes, including two pairs trained on identical data batches. The stated purpose is a production-scale test of the scaling-collapse approach from recent work: when you scale model size and hyperparameters together, the normalized loss curves should collapse onto one another. The report presents them following nearly identical normalized loss curves across dense and sparse architectures spanning nearly an order of magnitude in size. All pre-training, mid-training, and context extension run on xLLM, the group's open-source training infrastructure for dense and MoE models at production scale, and the reinforcement-learning stack is RL360, also released. The data section reports roughly 10 trillion synthetic pre-training tokens generated by their own pipelines, and the report releases and documents every pipeline that built the data — which the authors themselves note is the part of model development that open releases most rarely disclose.

The fleet layout follows deployment settings. The 0.9 billion targets phones and memory-constrained devices. The 3.7 billion and 7 billion target local assistants and coding tools on consumer hardware and serve as fine-tuning and reinforcement-learning research platforms. The 32 billion dense and the 36B-A4B target self-hosted technical and agentic assistants. The 375B-A23B targets multi-GPU server deployment.

Now the benchmark claims, which I will take one at a time. The comparison set is four open-weight models released around the same time — GLM-5.2, MiniMax M3, Nemotron 3 Ultra, and Inkling — plus two frontier closed models used as reference points. The 375B-A23B is smaller than most of the open comparison set in both active and total parameters, and the report claims it ranks first or second on most agentic and coding benchmarks. Specifically: it leads all four open models on Toolathlon-Verified at 65.3, where it also surpasses one frontier reference model and comes close to the other; it leads them on repository-level code understanding without internet access, SWE-Atlas-QnA, at 48.4; and it exceeds both frontier reference variants on the tau-banking agentic task. The reported gap is expert-level scientific reasoning, on HLE and CritPt. The 36B-A4B matches or stays within a few points of the dense 32 billion on every benchmark. The 7 billion outperforms similarly sized open-weight models on most benchmarks, and both the 7 billion and 3.7 billion lead SWE-bench Verified by wide margins, which is the notable claim at the small end — the small open models are competitive on the standard software-engineering benchmark.

The Uno adapter deserves its own paragraph because it is the part of the report that changes the deployment story. The claim is that a lossless diffusion adapter attached to the autoregressive backbone can accelerate decoding by up to 2.2 times without a separate draft model, which is the same speedup you would expect from speculative decoding with a small draft, but with none of the draft-model maintenance. The mechanism is that the diffusion step generates a block of tokens in a single pass, conditioned on the autoregressive context, and the lossless property means the output distribution is unchanged from the base autoregressive model — the adapter is a pure implementation win. The engineering implication is real: if you are serving K2-Horizon models at the 375B-A23B scale, the inference cost per token is the dominant term, and a 2.2 times speedup with no quality trade is the difference between a model you can serve at a given latency budget and one that needs two more GPU hours. The caveat the paper itself raises is that the adapter is trained separately and its quality depends on the base model's representation — a retrained base model invalidates it, so it is a per-checkpoint asset, not a reusable one.

Skeptical assessment, and this is where a 150 page report gets interrogated. First, the open-source definition. The release follows what the authors call the LLM360 definition of open source — weights, data, checkpoints, logs, code. That is more than most releases, but "open data" in a 22 trillion token corpus with 10 trillion synthetic tokens means you get the data and the pipelines, and the question is whether the data is actually usable at that scale, or whether the interesting content is in the pipeline code. The honest test is whether someone else can reproduce one of the four shared-corpus models from the released artifacts, and the report is structured to make that test possible — same recipe, scaled hyperparameters, intermediate checkpoints at every stage. That is the right design for a reproducibility claim, even though the compute to actually run it is not something a small lab can do.

Second, the benchmark table. Agentic and coding benchmark leadership against four named contemporaries is a strong claim, but it is a snapshot: those four models are the comparison set, and the agentic benchmarks in particular — Toolathlon, tau-banking, SWE-Atlas — are moving targets where harness choices and tool availability matter as much as model quality. I would want the exact harness configurations for every number, and the report does ship the evaluation code, which is the mitigation. The scientific-reasoning gap the authors concede is the tell: the fleet is tuned for agentic and coding work, and the loss curve collapse does not say anything about whether that objective mix was the right one for science. Third, MoVA and the softplus gate: the one-seventh active parameters result is the architecture's headline, but it is measured against a same-data dense control at one size, and the report should be read as evidence that value-pathway sparsity is a real knob, not as a general law. The lossless 2.2 times decoding from Uno is the most immediately useful claim, because lossless is a property you can verify on a fixed eval set, and I would do exactly that before putting it in a serving stack.

Engineering notes. If you are evaluating this fleet, start with the 36B-A4B: it is the model that concentrates the architecture claims, it targets self-hosted deployment, and the one-seventh active parameter result is where the economics live. The xLLM and RL360 releases matter if you do your own training, because the infrastructure code is the part that usually stays proprietary. The intermediate checkpoints mean you can inspect where in the training run a capability appears — pre-training, mid-training, or the RL stage — which is a research feature that most releases do not give you.

Bottom line: K2-Horizon is the most complete open release of a frontier fleet we have seen, with the data pipelines and stage checkpoints that make the reproducibility claims testable, and benchmark leadership on agentic and coding work against same-generation open models. I would trust the release discipline and the lossless decoding claim, and I would verify the agentic benchmark numbers with my own harness and look past the conceded scientific-reasoning gap before treating the 375B model as a general-purpose replacement rather than an agentic workhorse.
