It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows. The first three authors are Xuehang Guo, Haoyu Wang, and Haifeng Chen, working at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign.

The setting is multi-agent workflows, where several specialized LLM agents coordinate to solve a task too hard for any single model. Recent methods train the LLM that builds the workflow against the outcomes of executing it, so the workflow generator learns which decomposition to produce. The paper's objection is that these methods optimize only the workflow generator. The agents that actually build and execute the workflow stay fixed. The hard part of improving them is credit assignment. The agents are coupled, so they cannot be trained independently, because a change in one role shifts the inputs every other role sees, and a workflow's outcome is a single sparse score that does not say which agent caused a failure. You get one number for the whole pipeline and no signal about where the problem was. That is the gap the paper is trying to close, and it frames the workflow itself as the missing training harness, the structure that lets you decompose a single outcome into per-role signals.

Their method, FloWright, uses the workflow itself as a harness for optimization. Instead of a single outcome score, it introduces a hierarchical, structure-aware reward paradigm that decomposes the final result into the workflow's own structure, so reward can be assigned to the individual roles and the way they connect rather than only to the terminal answer. The hierarchy follows the workflow's DAG. Intermediate artifacts get their own rewards, so a role that produces a bad intermediate gets penalized even if a later role partially papers over the mistake, and a role that recovers from a bad input gets credit for that recovery. This lets the system optimize the workflow and its roles together. It supports both single-role self-evolution, where one role improves while the others are held, and multi-role co-evolution, where several roles improve at the same time, and it does all of this without additional models, labels, or extra executions, which is a real practical constraint. The co-evolution case is the harder one, because the roles are moving targets for each other, so the credit-assignment signal has to stay meaningful as the roles it is attributing reward to are themselves changing. One detail that makes the co-evolution result non-trivial is the credit-assignment window. In single-role self-evolution, the reward is a clean function of one role's output, so the gradient, or the RL update, has an unambiguous target. In co-evolution, a final answer can be right because one role fixed a mistake another role made, or it can be wrong even though every individual role did its best. The structure-aware reward has to decide, in the second case, whether the failure belongs to the role that produced the bad intermediate or the role that failed to recover, and that decision is exactly the kind of attribution that a sparse outcome reward cannot make. The fact that co-evolution outperforms single-role optimization by more than double the margin is the evidence that the attribution is at least sometimes correct, because if it were not, the roles would be pulling in conflicting directions and the co-evolved workflow would underperform the best single role, not beat it by 2.2 points.

The second contribution addresses a data problem. Workflows are typically trained and evaluated on data a single agent can already handle, which means the training signal never actually requires the workflow. Their companion method, DataWright, is an adaptive data hardening approach that converts existing datasets into workflow-level tasks with increased difficulty, so the training data genuinely demands multi-agent coordination. The hardening is adaptive in the sense that it measures how much a task a single agent can already do and pushes the difficulty to the regime where the single agent fails but a coordinated workflow can succeed. That matters because a workflow trained only on easy data has no gradient pressure to actually distribute work, so it learns to route everything through its strongest role and the multi-agent structure becomes decoration. By contrast, once the data is hard enough that no single role carries the task, the structure-aware reward has real work to do, and the credit assignment over the roles is what determines whether the system learns to divide labor or to collapse back into one dominant agent.

The results span document, slide, chart, code, math, and finance tasks, and the models are small open models, which is the point, showing that the gain comes from the training method rather than from a large backbone. Small open models trained with FloWright improve by up to 7.41 percent over the baseline. The co-evolution result is the more interesting one. Co-evolving the roles, which adds 5.03 percent, gains more than optimizing a single role in isolation, which adds 2.83 percent. So the coupling they are trying to exploit, the fact that the roles are jointly responsible for the outcome, is where the extra gain lives, and it is only recoverable because the structure-aware reward can attribute credit across the coupled roles. The task breakdown matters for interpretation. The document, slide, and chart tasks are the ones where a workflow has the most obvious structure, a draft stage, a formatting stage, a verification stage, so they are also the ones where a structure-aware reward has the most to decompose. The code and math tasks are harder for the method in a different sense, because the intermediate artifacts are less cleanly separable, so a reward on an intermediate is less clearly a reward on a role. The finance tasks sit in between. That the improvement is positive across all six rather than concentrated in the document and slide tasks is the reason the authors can claim the method is not tuned to one workflow shape, though it is worth checking how much of the 7.41 percent comes from the document-heavy tasks specifically. One more practical detail is that the method works without additional models, labels, or extra executions, which constrains how you could replicate the result. The structure-aware reward has to be computed from the intermediate artifacts the workflow already produces, not from a separate judge model that scores each role, and the data hardening has to operate on datasets you already have rather than requiring a fresh, harder corpus. That makes the method cheaper to deploy than the alternatives, but it also means the quality of the intermediate artifacts is load-bearing: if a workflow's intermediate outputs are not well-formed or not separable by role, the reward decomposition has less to work with, and the credit assignment gets noisier. The method is most powerful exactly where the workflow's internal structure is clean, and that is a condition that not every multi-agent system satisfies.

Skeptically, the upvote count was zero at fetch time, and the absolute gains, while consistent, are measured on a fixed task suite with small open models, so the question is whether the co-evolution advantage holds as models get larger, where a single strong agent may absorb the coordination benefit. The reward decomposition is the load-bearing piece, and its quality determines how well credit is actually assigned, but the paper's own framing of the sparse-outcome problem is what makes that attribution nontrivial. The data hardening is also a double-edged tool, because if DataWright makes the tasks harder in ways that favor the particular workflow structure the system is trying to learn, the benchmark and the method are partly aligned. The honest read is a reasonable and well-motivated take on a real credit-assignment gap in workflow training, with a concrete mechanism and a clean co-evolution result, but the evidence is on small models and a fixed task set, and the reward design is where the method either stands or falls.
