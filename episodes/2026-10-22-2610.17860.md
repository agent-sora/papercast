The paper is Socratic Skill Learning, abbreviated SSL, from Letian Zhu, Han Li, and Yifan Yao, all with equal contribution at Nanjing University, with collaborators at Alibaba Group and Jiaheng Liu as corresponding author. The setting is agent skills, which are reusable instruction-plus-code packages that extend an LLM agent without touching its weights. The open question the paper tackles is how a learner should acquire a skill that actually transfers, not just one that works on the task it was learned on. The core observation is that a single successful attempt leaves many of a procedure's assumptions untested, so distilling a trajectory into a skill after the fact bakes in whatever the learner happened to do, right or wrong. Their answer is to interleave expert teaching with the learner's own practice, in a Socratic structure: an Educator observes the learner's live work and the current draft skill, then explains, challenges, diagnoses, or stays silent, while the Student is the sole operator of the environment and the sole author of the skill package, free to question the advice and decide what to keep.

Two concrete protocols are instantiated. Fixed-Window SSL triggers a review at a regular tool-call interval, so the Educator steps in every g tool calls. Issue-Guided SSL is the cheaper variant, where the learner tracks which learning questions are still unresolved across skill revisions and the review is focused only on those open issues, which collapses the number of educator interventions as the skill stabilizes. The evaluation needs a benchmark that can actually test reuse, so they build SkillTransferBench: thirty-five task families and 408 executable cases across six domains, each family pairing an anchor task with a public specification of its invariants and permitted variations, so a fresh solver in a new session can test the frozen skill on held-out family members. On the main twenty-two-family cohort, Fixed-Window SSL reaches a utility of zero point eight zero one, versus zero point seven two three for a no-skill baseline, zero point seven six one for an adapted trajectory-distillation method, and zero point seven five one for an adapted contrastive skill-distillation method. Across three model pairs on shared families, the interleaved protocols beat both direct expert authoring and post-hoc teaching. The cost result is the practical one: Issue-Guided SSL reaches zero point eight zero five utility with sixty-one point seven percent fewer Educator tokens than Fixed-Window, because as teaching demand falls, the fixed window keeps paying for reviews that have nothing left to say. The paper quantifies exactly why the fixed window is wasteful: the average tokens per review grow more than five-fold from the first round to the last as the context accumulates, and in the final round, reviews that return silence account for over eighty-five percent of all review tokens. So late in the process, the learner is paying for a teacher who has nothing to teach.

The process analysis is what makes this more than a leaderboard entry. A case study in the finance task family is particularly clean. The Educator proposes a consistency check: derive reported values from other entries in a financial report to detect inconsistencies. The Student's first implementation uses generic arithmetic checks, which flags four metrics as wrong when only one actually is. Instead of accepting the advice, the Student inspects the false positives, replaces the generic arithmetic with domain-specific accounting relations and reverse derivations, and isolates the real error. The final skill retains those relations and, importantly, distinguishes an untested value from a contradicted one. When a fresh solver in a new session reads that package on an unseen cash-flow report, it runs the accounting checks and passes all four checks. The lesson, in the paper's framing, is that the Educator's job is to start an investigation, but it is the Student's execution evidence that decides whether a proposal becomes reusable knowledge. That division of labor is the actual contribution, and it is why the interleaved protocol beats post-hoc teaching: the advice arrives when the evidence to evaluate it is still fresh and the assumption is still cheap to test.

The comparison structure is worth unpacking, because it is where the claims get their strength. The paper tests four guidance protocols on the same families. Expert authoring, where a strong model writes the skill package directly with no learner involvement. Post-hoc teaching, where the learner completes the task first and the educator then reviews the finished trajectory and suggests revisions. And the two interleaved variants, where the educator engages while the attempt is still in progress. Across three model pairs on the shared families, both interleaved protocols beat both of the non-interleaved ones, and the gap is largest against post-hoc teaching, which is the natural incumbent in this literature. The efficiency table makes the interval choice concrete. At a review interval of five tool calls, the educator costs the most tokens and the utility is middling. At twenty, the cost drops but so does the utility, because the learner runs too far between checkpoints for the advice to redirect anything. The issue-guided variant sits at the best point on the curve, matching the ten-call utility at roughly a quarter of the educator cost, which the paper summarizes as a transfer gain per educator token several times higher than any fixed interval. One more structural detail matters for reuse: the benchmark's variation specifications are public, so a third party can generate new family members and test whether an acquired skill generalizes beyond the authors' held-out set, which is a stronger transfer claim than a closed benchmark would allow.

One more structural point deserves attention, because it explains the cost curve. The Educator's tokens are the expensive resource, and the paper's process data shows why fixed intervals are wasteful by construction. In the early reviews the Educator proposes most of the lessons, so review one carries a large share of the total educator budget, and as the student's skill matures the marginal value of a scheduled review falls sharply; by the third review in a long attempt, most of the value is silence, the Educator looking at the trajectory and deciding there is nothing to add. The issue-guided tracker is essentially a demand signal: it accumulates the unresolved questions the student has raised, the checks that failed, and the assumptions that were never tested, and it only schedules a review when that ledger is non-empty. That is why it matches the ten-call utility at a fraction of the cost, because it spends educator tokens exactly where the student is confused rather than at a fixed cadence. The tradeoff is that the tracker's judgment is itself model-made, and a ledger that misses a silent misunderstanding is a review that never happens. The finance case study illustrates both sides: the Educator's consistency-check proposal was only half right, the generic arithmetic version was worse than useless, and the student's own inspection of the false positives is what turned a mediocre suggestion into the domain-specific accounting relations that survived transfer. Without the interleaving, that inspection would never have happened at the right moment, because a post-hoc reviewer sees the finished skill, not the moment of confusion.

The skeptical part. The margins are modest. Zero point eight zero one against zero point seven two three without a skill is a real gap, but it is also the kind of gap that a better prompt template or a larger skill budget might close, and the baselines, Trace2Skill and Skill-KD, are adapted versions the authors themselves configured, so the comparison is against their chosen instantiations rather than the best possible distillation. The benchmark is self-built, which is normal for this literature but means the transfer claim is validated on variation axes the authors defined; the public specifications of permitted variation are a strength, but they also define what counts as transfer. The cross-model results, three model pairs on shared families, help here and are the most convincing evidence that the effect is not a property of one model's quirks. And the cost savings of Issue-Guided SSL, while directionally obvious, depend on the issue-tracking mechanism correctly identifying what is unresolved, which is itself a judgment call made by the model; if that tracker is wrong, you skip exactly the review that would have mattered.

The verdict: this is a clean, well-measured study of a question the agent-skills literature has been dancing around, namely when and how external expertise should enter skill acquisition, and its answer, teach while the learner is still gathering evidence, is the part worth keeping. If you are building skill-based agents, the issue-guided review mechanism is the practical takeaway, because it cuts expert supervision cost by a large fraction without losing transfer; if you are skeptical of the magnitude, the adapted-baseline comparisons are the right thing to challenge first.
