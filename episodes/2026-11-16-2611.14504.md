The paper is Emergent Biological Capabilities in a Foundation Model for Molecular Interactions, a single-author technical report by Adi Mashiach of Output Labs, submitted to NeurIPS 2026. It introduces Bonbon, a foundation model for molecular interactions trained on roughly 3.2 trillion tokens of paired protein and ligand sequences, and it makes a specific, testable claim: that three capabilities the model was never explicitly supervised on emerge from a single frozen representation space, and that they appear in a sharp jump at the largest scale even though the pretraining loss decreases smoothly. This is a foundation-model release with an emergence study attached, so I will treat the numbers as claims to be scrutinized, not settled facts.

The setup. Foundation models are supposed to develop emergent capabilities: tasks beyond the training objective that appear with sufficient scale, the principle behind language and vision-language models. The authors extend that to molecular interactions. The data challenge is what makes this different from a vision-language foundation model. Paired protein-ligand data is sparse; a single image-caption pair carries dense signal, but the pattern that distinguishes, say, an agonist from an antagonist is distributed across millions of examples rather than concentrated in any one. And the relationships are many-to-many: one protein binds thousands of ligands and one ligand binds hundreds of proteins, whereas standard multimodal architectures assume a one-to-one pairing. So the architecture and the data regime both have to be matched to that structure. There is a second, subtler point in the motivation that is easy to miss: co-folding models, the current dominant approach to protein-ligand structure, are capability-bounded by construction. They predict 3D atomic coordinates, and everything beyond coordinates, affinity, mechanism, site localization, is bolted on as a supervised head. Independent benchmarks report that these models memorize their training complexes and generalize weakly to unseen ligands. So the question Bonbon poses is not can a sequence model match a co-folding model, but can a sequence-only representation contain the interaction information at all, without coordinates ever entering the training objective.

The model is a transformer with pre-layer normalization, rotary position embeddings, flash attention, and a 65,536-token context window, sized to hold the longest known protein sequences and multi-component inputs. It is trained by self-supervised learning on paired sequences alone, with no task-specific labels, no structural coordinates, and no binding geometries. The training data is proprietary screening data, enriched with synthetic interaction data generated to match the distribution of the real binding data; the generation method is deferred to a separate technical report. The authors are careful to state that every evaluation uses real experimental data from public databases and the literature, and no synthetic data appears in any evaluation set. They apply quality filters: PAINS-pattern and aggregator-prone compounds are removed, proteins with disproportionately broad ligand sets are removed, sequence-level redundancy is filtered on both sides, and any protein sharing more than 90 percent sequence identity with an evaluation target is excluded before training. That last filter is the basis of all their out-of-distribution claims, and it is the right thing to do.

The scaling regime is the second notable choice. The 1.6 billion parameter production model uses a 1 to 2,000 parameter-to-token ratio, which is two orders of magnitude more data-heavy than the Chinchilla-optimal ratio for natural language. They justify it as accommodating the information sparsity of paired biological data. And they run a controlled scaling experiment across six scales, from 42 million to 1.6 billion parameters, spanning about three orders of magnitude of compute, with everything held identical except parameter count.

Now the emergence claim, which is the heart of the paper. From the single frozen representation, they report three capabilities at three levels of resolution, all evaluated with parameter-free signatures applied unchanged across scales, so the evaluation itself is not the thing that changes with scale. At the functional level, zero-shot prediction of mechanism of action across six classes, including distinguishing agonist from antagonist on the same receptor. At the residue level, zero-shot binding-site localization, including on GPCRs and transporters that lack a well-defined pocket, plus a parameter-free signature that separates orthosteric from allosteric pockets on the same protein. At the bond level, classifying covalent versus non-covalent engagement from the dense representation and localizing the reactive residue from the per-residue representation. All three, they claim, generalize to out-of-distribution complexes.

The scaling result is the one that matters. Pretraining loss decreases smoothly across all six scales, as expected. But every capability metric stays flat or near baseline through roughly three orders of magnitude of compute and then jumps sharply at the 1.6 billion scale. The authors' conclusion is that loss-based scaling laws alone are not enough to predict when a zero-shot emergent capability becomes available, which is a direct rebuttal to the argument that apparent emergence is just an artifact of threshold-imposing metrics. I will note that this is their framing and it rests on the parameter-free signatures being a fair probe; if the signature has a sharp decision boundary, some of the jump could be the probe's. But the controls, especially the identical signature across scales, are reasonable.

The transfer and wet-lab results. The same frozen representations are applied to standard benchmarks. For binding prediction they report a state-of-the-art MF-PCBA AUPR of 0.081 and an LIT-PCBA-AF3 EF1 percent of 16.6. For affinity prediction they report matching physics-based free-energy perturbation, with a correlation of 0.74 on CASP16 and 0.78 on FEP+4, at roughly five orders of magnitude greater speed than co-folding methods. And the prospective wet-lab validation: across nine disease targets spanning seven protein families, a 61.5 percent dose-response hit rate on de novo compounds, yielding molecules not previously reported to inhibit these proteins, including for out-of-distribution targets.

The skeptical assessment, and there is a lot to check here because a single lab is making large claims. First, the data. The training corpus is proprietary and the synthetic data generation is in a separate report, so the exact training distribution cannot be independently verified. That is a real reproducibility gap for a foundation-model release. Second, the wet-lab hit rate of 61.5 percent is prospective, which is the good kind of validation, but it is nine targets from one lab, and the de novo compounds were presumably generated by this same group with full knowledge of the model's preferences, so there is a risk of selection on the generator's own in-distribution chemistry. The out-of-distribution targets are the part to look at most closely. Third, the five-orders-of-magnitude speed claim is a comparison to co-folding methods, and it is a compute comparison, not a quality comparison; the quality is the near-match on affinity, which is the honest number. Fourth, the emergence jump, while well-controlled, is a single family of probes; more diverse capability probes would be needed to conclude that the jump is a general property of the model rather than of these particular signatures. And the single-author, single-lab provenance means there is no independent replication yet.

What I would trust: the architecture and data-regime reasoning, the 1 to 2,000 data-heavy ratio for sparse paired data is a defensible and useful design principle. What I would trust: the frozen-representation transfer to binding and affinity at high speed, because those are standard benchmarks with published numbers. What I would re-verify before citing this as a paradigm result: the wet-lab hit rate on truly external targets, the synthetic-data generation method once published, and the emergence jump under a broader set of capability probes.

Two-sentence verdict: Bonbon is a substantial foundation-model release for molecular interactions whose cleanest contribution is a controlled scaling study showing that zero-shot capabilities can emerge in a sharp jump at the top scale even while loss falls smoothly. The prospective wet-lab hit rate and the five-orders-of-magnitude speed advantage are strong, but the proprietary data and single-lab provenance mean the headline emergence claim should be held as a well-controlled hypothesis pending external replication.
