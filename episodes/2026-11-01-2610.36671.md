Look Only When Needed, by Donghyeon Cho, Joowon Kim, and Seungho Shin, all at KAIST, with the corresponding author Eunho Yang also affiliated with AITRICS, is an inference-time efficiency paper for diffusion vision-language models, the masked-diffusion VLMs like LLaDA-V that unmask tokens in parallel over denoising steps. The central finding is a measurement: if you run the model with and without the image at every decoding step along the same trajectory, the image changes which token gets unmasked at only about 23 percent of steps on average. Most of the decoding compute in these models is spent re-reading an image the model does not need to see again. The paper turns that redundancy into a training-free decoding rule and reports keeping 99.6 percent of the baseline score on ten benchmarks at 0.35 times the decoding FLOPs.

The setup is worth stating precisely because it is what makes the redundancy possible. A diffusion vision-language model starts from a fully masked response and, over a fixed number of denoising steps, predicts and unmasks the most confident positions each time. The conditioning input is the visual tokens plus the prompt, and that visual input is fixed for the whole trajectory. Because the attention is bidirectional, you cannot use the exact key-value cache that autoregressive models use, so the standard implementation re-runs a full forward over the entire multimodal sequence at every step. On the benchmarks studied, visual tokens make up 89 to 97 percent of that input, and a forward without them costs only 3 to 10 percent as much. So the expensive part of every step is reprocessing an unchanged image. Prior work attacks this from three directions: prune visual tokens so each forward is cheaper, cache intermediate states so you reuse computation, or unmask more tokens per step so you take fewer steps. None of those asks the question this paper asks: does this step need the image at all?

The measurement that motivates the method is a paired comparison. Along LLaDA-V's standard decoding trajectory, the authors run two forwards per step: a full forward over the image, prompt, and current response state, and a text-only forward with the visual tokens removed but position IDs preserved. They record the top-one predictions from both. Two numbers come out of this. First, at 83.4 percent of the positions the model actually unmasks, the full and the text-only forward agree on the top-one token, so the prediction follows from the text alone and the image is doing nothing at that step. Second, 85.0 percent of final tokens had already been the top-one prediction at their position before the step that unmasked them, yet the baseline kept recomputing them with the image until unmasking. The redundancy is not uniform: the share of steps where the image changes at least one decision ranges from 7.9 percent on MMBench to 43.8 percent on DocVQA, averaging 22.8 percent, and where it does matter it tends to cluster in the early denoising steps. That last fact is the argument against a fixed schedule. You cannot say skip the image on steps 5 through 12, because which steps matter varies per input and per task.

The method, called LOWN, decides both of the two questions standard decoding answers with a single full forward: whether the image is needed, and how many tokens to unmask. Every time LOWN runs a full forward, it stores the top-one predictions and confidences on the still-masked positions and calls that pair the anchor. At each subsequent step it runs the cheap text-only forward, orders the masked positions by decreasing anchor confidence, and walks down that ordering. A position is certified when its current text-only prediction equals the anchor's prediction. The longest confidence-ordered prefix of certified positions is what the step accepts. If that prefix is at least k long, where k is the number of tokens the baseline unmasks per step, the step skips the full forward entirely and unmasks the entire prefix to its anchor predictions. Because it accepts the whole prefix rather than exactly k tokens, a cheap step can advance further than the baseline. If the certified prefix is shorter than k, the step runs a full forward, replaces the anchor, and unmasks k positions in disagreement-first order, meaning the positions where the text-only and full predictions differ come first, so image-dependent tokens are not deferred. A fresh full forward overturns between 2.2 and 4.7 percent of the tokens unmasked on text-only steps in their own runs, which is the honest error rate of the certificate. There is no learned threshold and no per-benchmark tuning; the acceptance length is whatever the current input and step certify.

The cost model is clean. Let f be the fraction of steps that run a full forward, sigma the relative number of steps, and rho the cost of a text-only forward relative to a full one. The relative FLOPs are f plus sigma times rho. LOWN lowers f, and accepting more than k tokens can lower sigma. Visual token pruning is orthogonal because it only scales the full-forward term: pruning makes each full forward cheaper, LOWN makes full forwards rarer, and the two compose without retraining. The paper combines LOWN with RedVTP at 50 percent retention, reaching a normalized average of 98.3 at 0.223 times the baseline FLOPs and scoring highest on all ten benchmarks, and with ZOO-Prune at 33 percent it cuts their FLOPs by a further 60.6 percent. It also composes with Fast-dLLM's approximate key-value cache: keeping the cache's block and refresh settings and combining its token selection with LOWN's acceptance rule, adding LOWN on top reduces mean generation time by 38.1 to 50.1 percent and FLOPs by 44.1 to 58.9 percent relative to Fast-dLLM alone, with combined scores within 2.0 points of the unmodified model, though the cache-plus-LOWN pair actually scores slightly below the cache alone on all three measured tasks, with a 5.0 point drop on LLaVA-Bench.

The main table, at matched FLOPs, is where the method earns its keep. Against accelerated competitors given at least LOWN's FLOPs budget, LOWN scores highest on eight of the ten benchmarks. The nearest competitor, KLASS, a confidence-plus-stability decoding schedule, reports 91.9 percent at its own matched FLOPs budget, while using benchmark-specific tuned thresholds, so LOWN's one adaptive rule wins on eight of the ten benchmarks. Four of the ten benchmarks actually score above baseline under LOWN, which is why the paper also reports a capped average of 98.6 percent with each benchmark clamped at 100, so gains do not offset losses. The rule transfers: on LaViDa it retains 99.7 percent of baseline at 0.174 times the FLOPs, and on the video model VidLaDA-8B it uses 0.255 times the FLOPs on EgoSchema at 60.2 versus 60.6 baseline, and 0.272 times on MLVU at 43.3 versus 41.8, so on the video benchmark it is both cheaper and better.

Now the skeptical read. First, everything is measured at greedy decoding, batch size 1, no key-value cache in the standalone setting, on one primary backbone, LLaDA-V, with two transfer checks. The 99.6 percent average is a mean over ten benchmarks with wide per-benchmark variance, and on the benchmarks that matter for document and chart work, DocVQA and ChartQA, the image-dependent step share is highest, 43.8 and 24.5 percent, which is exactly where the savings are smallest and the certificate is least reliable. The FLOPs accounting is analytical, not wall-clock, and the one place they report wall-clock time is the cache composition, where the combined method loses a little quality to the cache alone. Second, the certificate ages: the anchor was computed from an older response state, and the error grows with the age, which the paper handles by replacing the anchor on every full forward, but that also means a long run of successful text-only steps means the anchor is stale and the 2.2 to 4.7 percent overturn rate is the price you pay for skipping. Third, the method is tied to a decoding structure that has a fixed number of steps and a natural per-step budget k; how much of this transfers to diffusion LMs with different unmasking schedules, or to non-diffusion VLMs where the redundancy may look different, is not shown. The measurement protocol is cheap to reproduce, and that is the part I would do first: if the 83 percent agreement number does not hold on your workload, none of the rest does.

My verdict is that this is one of the cleanest inference-efficiency results of the year in this space: a single measurement, a single rule, no training, no thresholds, and it composes with every other optimization axis because it attacks how often the expensive forward runs rather than what the forward processes. The main caution is that the savings are workload-dependent, they are smallest exactly on the document-heavy benchmarks where diffusion VLMs are often deployed, and the quality accounting at the matched-FLOPs points is close enough to the competitors that the real question for adoption is wall-clock on your hardware.
