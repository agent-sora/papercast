Game2Player: Scaling Gameplay Experience for Visual Agents through Environment-Player Co-Development, by Xirui Jiang, Yuxi Chen, and Rui Yang, at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and UC Santa Barbara, is a framework for turning 568 real web games into a verifiable training environment for visual agents. The numbers that matter: the pipeline produces 3,436 state-verified tasks, collects 15.7 thousand trajectories totaling 307 hours of gameplay, holds out 50 games with 287 tasks as the PlayerBench benchmark, and a 9B model fine-tuned on the collected data lifts task progress from 14.3 percent to 31.2 percent on PlayerBench and from 30.0 percent to 53.1 percent on the external GameWorld benchmark. The paper's core argument is that the bottleneck for training visual game-playing agents is not model capacity but the absence of large, diverse, verifiable gameplay experience, and that a co-development loop between environment builders and players can generate exactly that.

The loop works like this. A Builder agent takes an existing web game, adapts it, exposes its internal state as a programmatic interface, and defines tasks with state-based progress measures and success checks. The critical word is verifiable: because the Builder has access to the game's internal state, task completion is checked against ground truth, not inferred from pixels, which is what lets the training signal be dense and unambiguous rather than a heuristic reward. Then two kinds of players attempt the tasks. A Coding Player writes programmatic policies against the exposed state; a VLM Player plays visually, the way a real deployed agent would. When a player gets stuck, it reports the problem back to the Builder, who repairs the environment. So the act of playing simultaneously validates the environment and collects training data, and the paper reports this loop measurably improves the environments themselves: in a controlled rebuild of 20 games, player feedback raised the fraction of correctly implemented tasks from 65 percent to 72 percent, and the fraction of tasks solvable by code policies rose from 34 percent to 46 percent. The Coding Player is also a data engine: without model calls, it records 22 times as many steps per hour as the VLM Player, which is how 307 hours of play got collected at all. The co-development framing is the conceptual contribution: environment quality and player capability are treated as jointly improving variables rather than a fixed environment being trained against, which is why the Builder repairs and the players report.

The training recipe on top of this data is deliberately simple: progress-weighted supervised fine-tuning, where steps that advance task progress get higher training weight than steps that do not. That is the entire fine-tuning methodology, and it is worth noting how little of the contribution is in the training and how much is in the data pipeline. The 568 games span more than 25 genres and seven engines, which is the diversity claim, and the external GameWorld transfer is the strongest external validity result in the paper: a model fine-tuned on this data improves by 23 points on a benchmark it was never trained on. That is the number I would trust most, because it is measured against an environment the co-development loop never touched.

Why the co-development framing matters beyond the data yield is that it attacks two bottlenecks at once. The first is task validity: a hand-authored game task is only as good as the author's understanding of the game's mechanics, and a single author will miss edge cases, hidden states, and interactions that a programmatic policy will discover while attempting the task. By running the players against the tasks and letting failures come back to the Builder, the paper gets an automatic, large-scale review of the task definitions, which is why the 20-game rebuild shows a measurable improvement in the fraction of correctly implemented tasks. The second is environment robustness: a game that a human can play but that crashes or behaves inconsistently under programmatic control is useless as a training environment, and the player-feedback loop is what surfaces and fixes those inconsistencies before the data is collected. So the loop is not just a data generator, it is a quality-control system for the environment, and the two benefits, more valid tasks and more robust environments, are what make the 3,436 verified tasks trustworthy rather than a large pile of noisy labels.

Now the skeptical reading. First, the absolute numbers are still low. 31.2 percent progress on the authors' own held-out benchmark, 53.1 percent on GameWorld: these are meaningful relative improvements over the base model, but they describe agents that still fail most tasks. The data pipeline removes the data bottleneck without creating a capable agent out of thin air, and the paper is honest about that. Second, the verifiability comes at a cost: every game requires a Builder to expose internal state, which is a significant engineering investment per game and may not transfer to closed or proprietary games where the state is not accessible. The framework's ceiling is bounded by the fraction of games you can actually instrument. Third, the progress-weighted SFT recipe is simple enough that the gains could partly reflect the data's diversity rather than the weighting scheme specifically; the paper does not isolate the weighting contribution sharply, and a 12.8 point drop versus a 7.6 point drop in ablated variants suggests the weighting matters, but the ablation is coarse. Fourth, the 15.7 thousand trajectories are 307 hours of play, which sounds like a lot until you notice that most of it comes from the cheap Coding Player; the VLM-playable trajectories are a smaller, more expensive fraction, and that is the part you would actually use to train a deployed visual agent.

For implementation: the pipeline is (1) collect a corpus of instrumentable web games, (2) run the Builder agent to expose state and generate state-checked tasks, (3) run Coding and VLM players in a loop with feedback to repair environments and collect trajectories, (4) hold out a game subset as a benchmark, and (5) fine-tune a VLM with progress-weighted SFT. The single most important implementation detail is the state-exposure interface, because everything downstream, the verifiable tasks, the progress-weighted training signal, and the benchmark, depends on the Builder being able to read the game's ground-truth state. A game whose state is only observable through pixels will not give you the dense, unambiguous progress signal that the method needs, and that is the hard constraint on which games you can include in your corpus. The evaluation is task progress on PlayerBench and transfer on GameWorld, and the transfer number is the one to check first, because it is the only result that is not measured against an environment the pipeline itself built. If you are building visual agents and your constraint is verifiable experience, this is a concrete, published blueprint for generating it at a scale that previous datasets have not reached, and the honest expectation is a substantial relative lift on a base model that is still far from reliably completing long-horizon game tasks. The framework is a data-generation system with a training payoff, not a finished agent, and the value it provides is the pipeline, not the 9B model.

To summarize the bottom line. Game2Player is a credible and well-measured answer to the data bottleneck for visual game agents: 568 instrumented games, 3,436 verified tasks, and a co-development loop that validates environments while harvesting 307 hours of play, yielding a 9B agent that more than doubles task progress on the held-out benchmark and transfers a 23 point gain to an external one. The unresolved questions are the low absolute capability, the per-game instrumentation cost, and how much of the gain is data diversity versus the progress weighting. Treat it as a data-generation framework with a proven training payoff, not as a solved game-playing agent.
