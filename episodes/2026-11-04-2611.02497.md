RoboColosseum: Evaluating Physical Intelligence at Scale In-The-Wild, by Tan Sang Nguyen, Yi Ru Wang, and Shailesh Shailesh, from the National University of Singapore, the University of Washington, Stanford University, the Allen Institute for AI, and Hugging Face, among a wider roster that includes the University of Oxford, BitRobot, and the Singapore Embodied AI Centre, is a distributed evaluation framework for robot policies that runs on a fleet of locally operated robots in real, uncontrolled environments. The problem it targets is a real one. General-purpose vision-language-action policies are supposed to work across many robots, tasks, and settings, but the physical trials needed to test them are expensive to reproduce. Every lab builds its own benchmark, its own hardware, its own tasks, and the numbers do not transfer. RoboColosseum's answer is to run a network of robots, each operated locally by a different site, all serving the same standardized policy and the same task protocol, and to aggregate the results into pairwise comparisons.

The setup is concrete. The fleet spans more than a hundred robots across the United States, the United Kingdom, and Singapore, and the initial study covers four embodiments: the SO-101 arm, the Franka arm, a bimanual YAM setup, and the G1 humanoid. It evaluates five vision-language-action policies, including MolmoAct2, pi-zero-point-5, LingBot-VLA version 2, and GR00T N1.7, alongside three frontier AI agents. Policy inference runs locally on a laptop with an RTX 5090, which is a meaningful detail because it means the results reflect a realistic deployment budget, not a datacenter server, and it keeps the framework reproducible for a lab that cannot rent a GPU cluster. There are two tracks. The open track runs models without any task-specific adaptation, in blinded pairwise trials under matched local conditions, where the evaluator does not know which system produced which trial. The fine-tune track adapts the vision-language-action policies to twenty tasks with a standard budget of one hundred demonstrations per task, and evaluates with twenty-five trials per model and task pair.

The scoring is worth describing because it is how a messy fleet of physical trials becomes a comparable number. Each trial is judged on two things, full-task success and a normalized progress score that credits partial completion. They fit two separate Bradley-Terry style models. One is a difficulty-conditioned comparison model, an Elo-style rating, that decides which system wins a head-to-head trial given the task's human-rated difficulty. The other is a progress-based model, fitted within each manipulation category, where the system with the higher normalized progress wins and equal progress is a tie. They then report each system's expected comparison score, which is the probability it wins a random matchup, so the numbers are calibrated probabilities rather than raw success counts. That framing matters because it lets them compare systems that were never run on the exact same task.

The open track is the headline. It comprises 5,699 pairwise comparisons across 5,699 tasks, 124 sites, and the four embodiments. The overall finding is blunt: frontier AI agents rival specialized vision-language-action policies on some comparisons, with an average agent advantage of about 5.3 percentage points, but all systems struggle to complete open-world tasks reliably. Across 901 paired progress evaluations, the frontier agent makes more progress in 394 of the comparisons, the VLA makes more in a comparable share, and the rest are ties, which is why the average edge is small. On the difficulty-conditioned ratings, GPT-6 Astra posts the highest point estimate on the DROID and Franka combination. MolmoAct2 posts the highest progress-based estimate among the five VLA policies in all six manipulation categories, with expected comparison scores ranging from 58 to 77 percent. Observed full-task success rates range from only 6 to 13 percent, and no evaluated system exceeds 13 percent success in any category. Success also collapses with task difficulty. On tasks rated 1 to 4 on a difficulty scale, full-task success is 30.7 percent, but on tasks rated 9 to 10 it falls to 0.4 percent, and mean normalized progress drops from 56.6 percent to 3.5 percent. That is the sobering core of the paper: even the best systems cannot reliably complete hard open-world manipulation.

The fine-tune track has a sharper, and more useful, finding. After fine-tuning, the policy that leads depends on the embodiment. GR00T N1.7 leads on the SO-101, while pi-zero-point-5 leads on both the Franka and the bimanual YAM. And the rankings barely agree across embodiments. They report Kendall's W, a measure of rank agreement, of 0.24 across the embodiment-specific task suites, which is weak. More striking, the open-track rankings poorly predict relative performance after fine-tuning, with substantial rank reversals on all three embodiments shared between the tracks. On the DROID and Franka combination, one policy, LingBot-VLA version 2, achieves 100 percent success on the open-drawer task and 0 percent on the marker-in-holder task, while MolmoAct2 is the exact opposite, 0 percent and 88 percent. So a single open-track number is not a reliable proxy for where a policy will land after fine-tuning.

There are a couple of structural results worth keeping. One is that the G1 humanoid is essentially unsolved: no policy completes any of the five humanoid tasks, yet average progress ranges from 13.3 to 23.2 percent across policies, meaning the systems make partial progress but never finish, which is the clearest single indicator in the paper of how far open-world manipulation is from being solved on a humanoid body. The other is a statistical-efficiency result: open-track rankings stabilize as trials accumulate, and an active matchmaking procedure, which is their analogue of an adaptive bracket, achieves comparable ranking agreement with substantially fewer physical trials. That matters because physical trials are the expensive resource, and a method that reaches a stable ranking with fewer trials is a direct cost saving for any lab running a fleet. The framework is open, the code and the model checkpoints are published on Hugging Face, and the protocol is designed so that new sites can join the fleet and contribute trials without re-deriving the whole evaluation.

The skeptical read. This is an evaluation infrastructure paper, so the contribution is the protocol and the findings, not a model. The fleet is geographically diverse, which is good for robustness, but it also means local conditions, lighting, and hardware wear are confounds that standardized protocols can only partially control. The 25-trial budget per model-task pair is modest, so individual cell estimates are noisy, and the authors lean on aggregate comparisons rather than point estimates to avoid overstating precision. The 5.3 point average agent advantage is a point estimate over 5,699 comparisons, but the sign and size of that advantage will depend on the task mix, and a different mix could flip it. The weak rank agreement, Kendall's W of 0.24, is presented as a finding, but it is also a limitation: if rankings do not transfer across embodiments, then a leaderboard is a harder object to maintain than the paper's framing implies. That said, the scale is real, the protocol is reproducible, the hardware budget is honest, and the negative result, that nobody reliably completes hard open-world tasks, is exactly the kind of calibrated baseline the embodied field needs.

Bottom line: RoboColosseum is a credible, large-scale, distributed benchmark that produces two durable findings, that open-world manipulation is far from solved, and that single-number leaderboards do not transfer across embodiments or across fine-tuning. The 5,699 comparisons across 124 sites and four embodiments is a real scale that a single lab cannot match, and the honest hardware budget makes the numbers defensible. If you evaluate robot policies, the distributed pairwise protocol, the dual Bradley-Terry scoring that credits partial progress, and the active matchmaking for trial efficiency are the parts to adopt. The 13 percent ceiling on open-world success is the number to keep in mind when reading any embodied capability claim.
