The paper is Pack Light, CarryOn, from Yuchen Lu, Julian McAuley, and Lei Zhang, all at the University of California San Diego. It addresses a specific and, I think, underappreciated problem in long-horizon agents: once a task has made progress, that progress lives in persistent task state, but the agent keeps re-reading a long interaction history that merely describes how the progress was achieved. The central question is whether the context can be reorganized around the reusable progress itself rather than the history that produced it. The proposed system, called CarryOn, is a context runtime that does exactly that, and it reports gains on two long-horizon benchmarks while cutting total token usage roughly in half.

The motivation is concrete. Think of a slide-editing task, where images have already been dropped into a presentation, or a report where measurements have already been entered. The resulting task state already captures that completed work; later steps can inspect and modify it directly. Yet as the agent runs on, it repeatedly processes an account of how results that are already available were produced. The prior art manages this growth in two broad families. One summarizes, retrieves, or prunes past interactions. The other translates the history into explicit progress records or structured task states. CarryOn's objection to both is that when they substitute for the reusable task results, they make continuation depend on a redundant, selective account, and that account can drift away from the state the agent can directly inspect and modify.

So the design reorganizes the context into three complementary pieces. The first is continuation carriers, which bind reusable content or object references to their conditions of use, so the agent can recover relevant information by directly inspecting and using these objects instead of by re-reading prose about them. The paper gives a concrete illustration from a slide-editing task: the working presentation and the exported image files carry the completed editing progress, while the remaining constraints, like which slides still need the background applied and whether the save was verified, stay explicit. The second is execution context, which preserves the active goals, the applicable constraints, and the unresolved issues that the carriers do not capture. The third is a live tail, which retains the interactions since the most recent successful fold, so the agent is not left without any recent raw history to work from.

A useful way to think about the split is by what each piece is for. The carriers answer the question of what has already been produced and can be reused. The execution context answers the question of what still needs to happen and under which constraints. The live tail answers the question of what has just happened that the other two have not yet absorbed. The carriers are the objects, the execution context is the plan, and the live tail is the recent conversation. That decomposition is what lets the system discard the raw history without losing the information the history carried, because every piece of information that matters for continuation has been routed into one of the three slots.

The hard problem, and the one the paper is really about, is keeping this reusable basis valid after the surrounding history has been retired. When a fold happens, the covered history is dropped, but the carriers that referenced it have to stay true. CarryOn's answer is to maintain the carrier records across folds, revising only the records that are affected, checking them against bound evidence and applicable environment checks, and carrying forward the records that are unaffected, while jointly updating the execution context. Once the updates are validated they are committed through a fold, which replaces the covered history. The key engineering property is that the system does not reconstruct the full representation at every fold; it only touches what changed.

The experiments use two long-horizon benchmarks, OSWorld 2.0 for computer use and BrowseComp-Plus for long-horizon browsing, with a Claude Opus host model. On OSWorld 2.0 the long-horizon task score improves from about forty-four to about fifty percent, a gain of roughly five and a half to six points, and the total token usage drops by about fifty-two percent, with total cost down about thirty-six percent. On BrowseComp-Plus the score moves from about forty-nine to about fifty-six percent, a gain of seven points, while token usage drops about twenty-three percent. The token reduction is the number I find more impressive than the accuracy, because the accuracy gains are modest and the context savings are large, and the whole point of the paper is that the history is largely redundant.

What I would trust is the observation and the cost result. The redundancy claim, that a long-horizon agent re-reads accounts of progress that its own task state already holds, is well-motivated and general, and it explains why the token savings are so large. The three-way split of the context into carriers, execution context, and live tail is a clean decomposition, and the incremental fold, only revising the affected records against evidence, is the right way to keep the representation honest without a full rebuild. The object-oriented framing is a useful lens even if you do not adopt the full runtime, because it gives you a vocabulary for asking, of any piece of your agent's context, whether it is a reusable result, an open obligation, or just recent noise, and each of those three has a different right way to manage it over time.

The design also has a property that I think matters for implementation and that is easy to understate: the carriers carry conditions of use. A record that says an image was exported is only reusable if the record also says under which assumptions that export is still valid. Binding the content to its conditions is what lets the fold discard the history safely, because when the environment changes, the affected conditions fail their checks and only the affected records get revised. Without that binding, a summary of progress is just a claim that can go stale silently, which is the exact failure mode the paper is trying to avoid by moving away from reconstructed accounts.

What I would want to verify is how much of the accuracy gain is real versus how much is a byproduct of a cleaner context. The accuracy improvements are in the low single digits to about seven points, and I would want to see whether the gain holds across more than the two benchmarks and more than one host model, because a context-management technique can easily overfit to the structure of a particular benchmark's tasks. The report does report both binary and partial scores on OSWorld, with a binary gain of about three point seven points and a partial gain of about five point eight, and the fact that the partial credit gain is larger is the right signature: it means the agents are further along the task before the budget runs out, which is consistent with the context staying usable longer rather than with a lucky pass. I would also want to understand the cost of the validation step. Checking affected carriers against bound evidence and environment checks is not free, and if those checks require the agent to re-inspect state or re-run tools, then some of the token savings may be offset by verification cost that is not fully accounted for in the headline number. And the BrowseComp-Plus cost reduction is much smaller than the OSWorld one, at about seven percent, which suggests the savings are task-dependent and that the fifty-two percent figure is the favorable case.

Two-sentence verdict: CarryOn is a sensible, well-motivated reorganization of long-horizon context around reusable task objects, and the large token reduction with modest accuracy gains is a credible and useful result. The main open questions are whether the accuracy gain generalizes beyond the two benchmarks and whether the validation cost of keeping carriers honest is fully reflected in the reported savings.
