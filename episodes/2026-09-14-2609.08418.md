Good evening, and our final paper tonight is Feyospace-v1: How the Cyber Mercury Seven Trained Frontier Cyber Models, from the Feyospace team. This is a training-report paper, and I want to be straight with you up front about how I am reading it. The title has a certain swagger to it, the Cyber Mercury Seven, and the team is not naming individual authors in the way a normal paper would. That anonymity is itself a data point, and I will come back to it. What the paper is actually about, stripped of the branding, is a data pipeline and a post-training recipe for open-weight cyber and security reasoning models. Let me tell you what it does, because the substance is where the interesting and the questionable both live.

The setting is security task reasoning. The target capabilities are the ones that matter in real offensive and defensive security work. Vulnerability discovery, exploit development, CTF-style challenge solving, patch generation, and reasoning over real codebases. The team's core claim is that they built a pipeline that turns a large corpus of real-world security tasks into executable, verifiable training environments, and that post-training open-weight checkpoints on those environments produces models that are competitive on security benchmarks. The three checkpoints they train are Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen3.8-27B, and a third open-weight base. So this is not a new foundation model. It is a post-training, supervised fine-tuning effort on top of existing open-weight bases, and I am flagging that because it shapes how much weight the results carry.

The data pipeline is the actual contribution, and it is worth your attention. They start from a large set of repositories and issue-resolving tasks, and the whole point of the pipeline is verification. They process a corpus where, across 1,170 repositories, 79.0 percent of the processed issues are verified as resolvable. They identify a set of fixes judged security-relevant, and out of 38,519 such fixes, 12,993 are verified. So the first principle of the pipeline is that you do not train on a task until you can execute it and confirm the fix actually works. That is a real methodological point, and it is the part of the paper I would respect, because most of the security-reasoning data out there is generated without an execution check, and that is exactly what produces the ungrounded, plausible-looking training signal you see everywhere else.

They categorize the tasks, and the labeling is detailed. They map cases to the Common Weakness Enumeration, CWE, classes, which is the standard catalog of software weaknesses. The classes that show up most, each appearing in more than 1.5 percent of the cases and together accounting for 57.0 percent of the observed labels, are out-of-bounds writes at 8.42 percent, cross-site scripting at 7.29 percent, out-of-bounds reads at 5.11 percent, path traversal at 4.95 percent, null pointer dereference at 4.65 percent, SQL injection at 3.81 percent, input validation errors at 3.55 percent, and use-after-free at 3.36 percent. That distribution is a reasonable fingerprint of real-world vulnerability work, and it suggests the data is grounded in actual code rather than synthetic toy examples.

The final supervised fine-tuning mixture contains 164,269 retained traces, and they come from multiple sources, with 28,177 from the basic set and the rest from the deeper security categories. They also maintain a set of benign baseline inputs that continue to run without regression, which is a small but telling detail. It means they are checking that the security fine-tuning is not breaking the model's general behavior, which is the failure mode you have to guard against when you specialize a model. And there is a reasoning-audit step in the pipeline where they map the verified reasoning steps to a capability taxonomy, so the traces are not just execution-verified, they are also classified by what capability the reasoning is exercising. That dual gate, execution verification plus capability mapping, is the methodological core, and it is the part that separates this from a paper that just fine-tunes on a bag of exploit snippets.

Now the headline numbers. The three checkpoints improve over their starting models by an average of 23.76 percent on the full CyberGym suite and 10.49 percent across the pooled CTF suites. And as of September 1, 2026, one of the checkpoints, which they call Feyospace-s1, achieves a verified success rate of 63.24 percent and ranks 10th on the official CyberGym leaderboard. I want you to sit with that number for a second, because it is the number the whole paper rests on, and it is the number I would interrogate hardest.

Here is my skeptical read, and I am being direct. The first thing to notice is that the 63.24 percent is a verified success rate on CyberGym, and the 23.76 and 10.49 percent figures are average improvements over the starting models. Those are relative improvements, and relative improvements over a weak base can look much more impressive than the absolute capability. A 23.76 percent relative gain on a suite where the base model scores low is a real gain, but it is not the same as a 23.76 point gain from a strong base. I would want the absolute scores per benchmark, the base model scores and the fine-tuned scores side by side, before I could say how good these models actually are, because the relative framing is doing a lot of the work in the abstract.

The second thing, and this is the one I would push back on most, is the leaderboard position. 10th on CyberGym, with a verified success rate of 63.24 percent. That is a respectable result for a post-training effort on open-weight bases, and I am not dismissing it. But the title, frontier cyber models, is a much stronger claim than the 10th place leaderboard position supports. The frontier of cyber reasoning, as of this year, is dominated by the large proprietary systems, and a 10th place open-weight post-training model is a solid entry, not the frontier. The branding is outpacing the evidence, and that is the gap between what the title promises and what the leaderboard shows. I would read the results as a competent, well-executed post-training recipe that produces a competitive open-weight security model, and I would not read it as the team claiming it is at the frontier.

The third thing I would verify is the reproducibility, and the anonymity cuts against you here. The pipeline is described, the verification gates are described, and the trace counts are given, but the team is not naming individual authors, and the data provenance, where the 1,170 repositories and the 38,519 security-relevant fixes came from, is not something I could independently audit from the paper. For a security dataset, provenance matters a lot, because you want to know the legal and ethical status of the exploit data, and you want to know whether the verified environments are representative of the tasks you would actually face. The verification is the strong part, and the provenance and the naming are the weak part.

And I will flag the double-edged nature of this work plainly, because it is a paper that trains models to find and exploit vulnerabilities, and the team has clearly thought about the benign-regression check, which is good, but the broader concern about dual-use security capability is the context this paper lives in, and a responsible read includes that.

Here is the bottom line. Feyospace-v1 is a well-executed post-training report whose real contribution is a verification-gated data pipeline that turns real security tasks into executable training environments, and the 63.24 percent verified CyberGym success rate with a 10th place leaderboard finish is a credible result for an open-weight model. I would not buy the frontier framing in the title, and I would want the absolute per-benchmark scores and the data provenance before I trusted the relative-improvement headline.
