Kandinsky 6.0 Video is a foundation model release from the Kandinsky Lab, with a long contributor list that includes Julia Agafonova, Bulat Akhmatov, and Mikhail Aksyutin. It is a family of diffusion models for synchronized text-to-audio-video generation, released with code, model checkpoints, and a diffusers integration under the MIT license. The family has two members: a 3B parameter Lite model and a 29B parameter Pro model, both generating 5-second video clips with synchronized 44 kilohertz audio, including lip-sync.

The architectural core is a dual-stream CrossDiT. One stream is a pretrained video diffusion model carried over from Kandinsky 5.0, and the other is a newly trained audio stream. The two streams are connected through bidirectional cross-attention for temporal synchronization, so each stream can condition on the other's latent content as generation proceeds. The bidirectionality is the important word here: it is not a one-way conditioning where the audio follows the video, but a mutual coupling where the video stream can react to what the audio stream is producing and vice versa, which is what makes the lip-sync and the sound design coherent rather than layered. The text prompt is encoded by both a CLIP text encoder and a vision-language model, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, with the VLM providing the richer semantic signal. Both models generate clips at standard-definition resolution in a latent space of 512 by 768 with 31 latent frames, which corresponds to 121 frames at 24 frames per second, and a built-in super-resolution model ups the output to Full-HD, 1920 by 1080. The two release sizes share this architecture and differ in the dimension of each stream: the video stream runs at 1,792 dimensions for Lite and 4,096 for Pro, the audio stream at 896 and 2,048, with 32 visual blocks for Lite and 60 for Pro.

The training data is the part worth dwelling on. The audio side is built on 40 million audio tracks between 5 and 20 seconds long, extracted from the Kandinsky dataset. The video-audio mixture is trained in two modes: text-to-audio-video for 75 percent of steps and image-to-audio-video for 25 percent. The audio branch is trained in stages, and after 5,000 steps there are 11 domain-specific checkpoints that get blended together. The final training loss is a weighted sum: the video loss at 0.85, the audio loss at 0.15, and a new face loss at weight 1.0 that restricts the video loss to the facial region to push lip-sync accuracy. Roughly 25 percent of the text-to-audio-video clips contain no visible faces, because clips without faces do not require lip-sync supervision.

The evaluation story has several pieces. For lip-sync, the headline metric is word error rate between the generated speech and the reference transcript. After the face-loss post-training stage, the Pro model's WER drops from 0.235 to 0.124, a relative reduction of 47 percent, and Lite drops from 0.179 to 0.127, a 29 percent reduction, both with paired Wilcoxon tests significant at p less than 10 to the minus 3. On a harder set of 100 Harvard sentences, the Pro WER drops from 0.251 to 0.180 at p of 0.005. That is the cleanest number in the paper: the dedicated face-region loss does the work.

For overall quality, they run human preference comparisons against leading audio-video generation models, and the report says the models are competitive with the leaders, particularly on speech-heavy prompts. The Pro model has a 2x inference path with approximately 0.763 billion parameters in that branch, which is how they make 29B parameters practical to run. For prompt conditioning, the fine-tuning stage adapts only the VLM's final layers through LoRA, 199 million parameters for Pro, 0.69 percent of the model, and 47 million for Lite, 1.57 percent, trained from scratch without warm-starting.

The release format matters as much as the model. MIT license, diffusers integration, and checkpoints for both sizes means anyone can build on the joint audio-video generation stack immediately. The prompt detail mixture in training is also documented: long captions at 0.7 weight, medium at 0.15, short at 0.1, shortest at 0.05, so the models are conditioned to work across prompt verbosity levels rather than only detailed prompts.

The infrastructure behind the release is worth a paragraph on its own. The super-resolution stage is trained on a combined dataset of 150,000 unique clips: 100,000 diverse 4K video clips used to construct paired high-quality and low-quality examples, plus roughly 35,000 clips from the Kandinsky 5.0 text-to-video collection selected through artifact filtering and Q-Align-based quality scoring. The two source datasets are filtered by different quality checks, and the 4K set additionally runs a ripple detector on top of Q-Align scores. There is also a Russian Cultural Code dataset, 1.2 million images and 530,000 video scenes, curated to represent Russian cultural context and injected through images during joint training with English and Russian captions.

The distillation pipeline is a second notable contribution. Kandinsky 6.0 Video uses a two-stage distillation carried over from Kandinsky 5.0: first, trajectory distillation, where the teacher's sampling trajectory is compressed into a few-step student; second, quality improvement, where the perceptual fidelity lost during aggressive compression is recovered with real data and adversarial training. The paper reports evaluating a wide range of trajectory distillation methods, including DMD, DMD2, SID, phased consistency, CFG distillation, pi-Flow, and parallel decoding distillation, and notes that distribution-matching methods were prone to mode collapse and mode dropping, while CFG and consistency methods altered the teacher's motion dynamics. The final choice is a combination that balances few-step speed against fidelity. The few-step student is then what ships, and that is the number that matters for the release: the paper reports the distillation as what makes the 29B model practical to run, since a multi-step video diffusion model at that parameter count is otherwise prohibitive for most teams, and the two-stage recovery of perceptual quality is what keeps the few-step samples from showing the artifacts that aggressive trajectory compression is known to introduce.

Where the skepticism sits. First, 5-second clips at standard definition before upscaling is a real constraint, and the super-resolution step is a separate model in the pipeline, which adds latency and a second failure mode. Second, the human evaluation is self-reported and the paper does not publish the full preference tables in the main text with enough detail to audit the margins. Third, the 40 million audio tracks come from the Kandinsky dataset, and the paper does not fully break out licensing provenance for the video-audio pairs in a way an enterprise would need to verify. Fourth, the 29B model's quality claims are strongest on speech and lip-sync, which is exactly what the face loss targets; the question of how the audio stream generalizes to non-speech sound design is less well documented. Fifth, the WER metric uses Whisper-large-v3, which is also one of the reward models used in training, so part of the measured improvement is the model aligning with the very system measuring it; the Harvard sentences result partially mitigates this, but it is a 100-prompt check.

A note on scope: the dual-stream design inherits the video stream from Kandinsky 5.0, so the video quality ceiling is bounded by what 5.0 already achieved, and the genuinely new capability is the audio stream and the synchronization between them. That is a reasonable and efficient way to ship a joint model, but it means the release is less a from-scratch foundation model and more a well-engineered extension, which the title slightly overstates.

The verdict: this is the most complete open release of a joint audio-video foundation model to date, with the MIT license and diffusers integration making it immediately usable, and the lip-sync numbers are the strongest in the open literature. If the 5-second length limit and the separate upscaler are acceptable for your pipeline, Kandinsky 6.0 Video is the default starting point for synchronized audio-video generation that anyone can run.
