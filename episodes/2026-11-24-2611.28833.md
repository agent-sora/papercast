Self-WSP, by Guoming Ling, Shanshan Zhong, and Kaixuan Zhang, at Carnegie Mellon University and MilkThink, asks the most literal version of the recursive self-improvement question: can a language model read its own weights, propose an edit to them, and continue operating with the edited weights, getting better along the way? The setup is a single 0.8-billion-parameter model, Qwen3.5-0.8B, which plays two roles. As the editor, it reads a catalog of its own 318 tensors, inspects up to 64 values, means, standard deviations, and row and column norms, and proposes edits as grammar-constrained JSON. As the solver, it answers math questions with those weights. A fixed outer loop, with no model in it, decides which edits survive: an edit is applied to a copy of the weights, checked on two disjoint batches of practice questions with reference answers, and kept only if it solves more questions without doing worse on either batch. The headline numbers are real: self-edits raise GSM8K accuracy from 11.8 to 62.2 percent, a fivefold gain, and MATH500 from 7.0 to 32.2 percent, sometimes by changing as few as 16 parameters.

The model itself is an interesting choice. Qwen3.5-0.8B is 24 layers deep, with three of every four layers using linear attention, the kind of layer that stores what it has read in a fixed-size state and forgets old information at a rate set by its weights, and one in four using standard attention. The editor proposes with thinking turned off; the solver answers with thinking on. The edit vocabulary is small: each proposal is one or two edits, each selecting a consecutive block of entries in a tensor and applying one of seven operations, set to zero, multiply by a number, add a number, fill with a number, redraw randomly from the tensor's own mean and standard deviation, remove a direction, or apply a short NumPy expression. Direction removal is the operation that matters: the editor names one column of a matrix as a direction, and every selected column has its component along that direction subtracted out, with a coefficient of 1 removing it entirely. That is the same family of operation that a frontier model used, in the prior WSP work from which this paper inherits its setup, to lift a small model's MATH500 from 7.9 to 48.1 percent by removing a single direction from 0.016 percent of the weights.

The paper runs the loop under two interfaces that differ in how the tensor catalog is ordered. The shuffled interface lists tensors in random order, uses 32 practice questions per batch, keeps an edit for a single-answer gain, and runs three rounds with budgets of 4, 6, and 12 valid edits. The ordered interface lists tensors in layer order, uses 128 questions per batch, requires a gain of at least three answers, and spends 8 valid edits per round. After every round, the current model is scored on validation questions that are never shown to the editor, and the round with the largest validation gain defines the run's best model. The reported test numbers come from the top three runs of each setting, which is best-case by construction, and the paper says so. The best ordered-interface model hits 61.0 percent on GSM8K and 24.2 on MATH500, and continued runs, where the best shuffled models keep editing under the ordered interface, push the top model to 62.2 and 31.8.

The section I would read most carefully is the interpretability one, because it is where the paper is honest in a way that most self-improvement results are not. The editor edits whichever tensors are listed first. It shows a strong preference for layer 0, and the most plausible mechanical explanation, that the first layer's forget rate in the linear-attention state is a reasonable target to repair, is undercut by a simpler one: the catalog happens to start at layer 0. Between 9 and 28 percent of the values in valid edits are statistics the editor just read, a mean, a standard deviation, or a norm. The mean of a layer-0 tensor, minus 1.979, appears as a scale or a shift in four of the selected models. The explanations the editor attaches to kept edits are often hallucinated: empty, describing a different block than the one edited, or irrelevant. The most extreme example, quoted in full in the paper, is the sentence, the cost of one chair is 11 dollars, copied word for word from the end of a solver response in the same call's practice feedback. And kept edits often hurt: because the outer loop decides from small practice batches alone, it keeps edits that pass by luck, and under the shuffled interface more than 40 percent of rounds that keep an edit lower validation accuracy.

The control experiment that frames everything is the random search. The editors are replaced by a random sampler that draws tensors, blocks, operations, and values uniformly, under the same budget. On GSM8K, random search matches self-editing, 32.8 to 31.9 percent for the best models, and the two run distributions look alike. On MATH500, with the same budget, random search finds nothing while self-editing reaches 17.3 percent; only with about 70 percent more budget does random search find a 13.6 percent model. The single-edit screening is the cleanest result: 352 random edits are applied one at a time to the original model and screened on 128 held-out questions, and most fall inside the band of pure sampling noise, which the paper measures explicitly by re-answering with new seeds. The best single random edit reaches 22.3 percent on GSM8K by scaling 4096 weights by minus 2. And here is the number that reframes the prior work: one of the frontier model's two published edits, zeroing 256 columns of an attention output matrix, scores 22.0 on GSM8K and 13.8 on MATH500 in this pipeline, as good as the best single random edits, and in the screening it merely ranks among the better random draws. The frontier model's simpler edit was, in effect, a very lucky random edit.

Where self-editing does beat chance is modest but measurable. In the same screening, the best self-edited model gains more than all 352 random edits on both datasets, and the continued models gain up to 17.2 points on MATH500, more than twice the best random edit. And a frozen editor, where only the solver weights are edited while the editor always uses the original weights, behaves much like the full loop, so letting the editor edit itself brings no clear benefit yet. The typical-run numbers are the honest ones: spot-check runs that are never selected gain an average of 24.7 points on GSM8K and 7.7 on MATH500, far less than the selected best cases.

What I would verify before believing the big picture. This is one 0.8-billion model, two math benchmarks, and the outer loop is supervised: it uses reference answers to keep edits, so the improvement is search with a reward, not the model's own judgment of its progress. The ordered interface was designed after seeing the shuffled results, which is disclosed but matters for the 62.2 percent headline. The gains concentrate in a few lucky rounds, eleven of 24 GSM8K runs gain more than 23 points and thirteen gain under 10, and the model's own stated weakness, repetitive thinking, is unusually easy to repair with a few direction removals. The paper's own framing is the right one: the successes come mostly from search and selection rather than from understanding.

Two-sentence verdict: Self-WSP shows that a sub-billion model can genuinely improve its own math weights through self-proposed edits, but the interpretability analysis, position bias, copied statistics, hallucinated explanations, and random-search parity on GSM8K, shows that the improvement engine is the outer loop's selection, not the model's understanding. It is a real data point on the path to recursive self-improvement, and it is also a caution about how much of recursive self-improvement is just stochastic search with a verifier.
