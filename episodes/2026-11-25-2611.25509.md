LingSante MedGuard Technical Report, from the LingSante MedGuard team at LingSante. The claim: you can build a single medical guardrail model, available at 4 billion, 9 billion, and 27 billion parameters, that checks a candidate medical response for safety, compliance, and ethical risk by distilling a guardrail teacher and six specialized capability experts into one student, and it reaches mean F1 of 87.4 percent on the team's 11-subset benchmark at the 27 billion scale.

The risk taxonomy has three domains with different failure physics, and the paper is careful to keep them separate. Safety is about clinical harm: a dosing error, a contraindicated recommendation, a diagnosis that misses the actual finding. Compliance is about professional scope: the response may be medically true but overstep the authorized role of the system, give a definitive diagnosis where the system is only triaging, or cite guidance whose applicability conditions are not met. Ethics is about who sees what: disclosing a patient's identity, condition, or context to parties who should not have it. The reason the three need one model rather than three filters is that they interact: a response that is safe and in-scope can still leak protected context, and a response that is in-scope and private can still be clinically wrong, so the joint judgment has to be made over the full response at once, with shared medical knowledge doing cross-domain reasoning rather than three independent classifiers each seeing a slice. The paper's point is that clinical correctness establishes neither compliance with professional responsibilities nor adequate protection of patient privacy, so a single safety score is insufficient.

The architecture of the supervision is the core contribution. There is a medical guardrail teacher model that supervises risk decisions and explanations across the three domains, and six capability expert models, each covering one supporting skill: medical rule understanding, numerical reasoning, multimodal hallucination detection, agentic safety verification, risk localization, and multimodal entity understanding. Two of these are called out as the evidence-grounded ones. Rule understanding applies explicit requirements to the response. Agentic safety verification acquires and appraises external medical evidence, so the guardrail can check a clinical claim against retrieved sources rather than relying on parametric knowledge. The examples in the paper are concrete: a lab report where the response attributes an LDH value of 204.50 to creatine kinase, whose actual reported value is 39.00, and a CT case where the response claims contrast and free air outside the bowel when the paired reference shows contrast remains within the lumen.

The training recipe has two stages, and the second one is the part that separates this from ordinary fine-tuning. First, offline supervised fine-tuning on teacher-generated demonstrations, so the student learns the joint output format: a risk decision, an explanation, and the capability-level justifications, all in one structured response. Second, multi-teacher on-policy distillation, where the student generates its own responses and the teachers score those rollouts, so the student is corrected on the mistakes it actually makes rather than only being shown ideal answers. That distinction matters for a guardrail, because the failure distribution of a model is not the same as the answer distribution of a teacher: on-policy feedback targets the student's own blind spots. The end state is a single deployable model; at inference there is no teacher ensemble, no retrieval over six expert calls, which is the practical point, because a production guardrail sits in the response path and cannot afford an ensemble. The capability experts are supervision sources only, training-time infrastructure that gets distilled away.

The risk categories are more concrete than the summary suggests. The paper walks through a statin recommendation where the response cites USPSTF guidance for a patient with an LDL-C of 195 milligrams per deciliter, even though the recommendation excludes LDL-C levels above 190: that is an evidence-applicability error, and it is the kind of compliance risk that a general content-safety classifier is not built to catch. Another example is a recalculation case where recomputing the MELD score exposes an arithmetic error, and checking the cited guideline exposes an applicability mismatch in the same response. These are the failure modes the six capability experts exist for: numerical reasoning catches the MELD error, rule understanding and agentic verification catch the guideline mismatch, and the guardrail teacher assembles the overall risk decision across all three domains in one pass.

The benchmark, LingSante MedGuardBench, has 11 risk-detection subsets spanning safety, compliance, and ethics, plus eight representative capability-evaluation tasks that probe the six specialized capabilities directly, so the suite measures both the cross-domain risk judgment and the supporting operations underneath it. The report also ablates training strategy and supervision source, which is the analysis that tells you how much the two-stage recipe and the six experts each contribute to the final score, rather than just reporting the endpoint. Results at the three scales: mean F1 of 82.3 percent at 4 billion, 82.4 at 9 billion, and 87.4 at 27 billion, with the 27 billion model highest among all evaluated models on the full risk-detection track. The near-flat step from 4 to 9 billion is notable in its own right: it suggests the 4 billion student absorbs most of what the supervision provides, and the remaining five points come from capacity rather than from seeing more of the same signal. The 4 billion model also reports 73.9 percent mean accuracy on MedHallu and MedHALT-NOTA under retrieval-enabled evaluation, which is the number that matters if you intend to run the small model with a retrieval backend in production. The capability-level analyses report improvements in evidence-supported consistency, source traceability, and rule-guided rationale quality as scale grows. The code and benchmark are released under inclusionAI on GitHub and Hugging Face, which is the right move for a benchmark whose main value is as a shared evaluation surface: the artifacts, the weights, and the eval data are all one repository away from reproduction.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? First, this is a technical report from the team that built the benchmark, and the headline numbers are self-reported on the self-built benchmark. The benchmark is released, which lets anyone reproduce, but the comparison set, which external guardrails are the 27 billion model compared against, matters a great deal and the abstract does not make it easy to find. I would want the full comparison table, including general-purpose guardrails and medical-specific ones, before taking the state-of-the-art claim seriously. Second, the 4 billion model is the interesting deployment target, and the gap between 82.3 and 87.4 F1 is the gap between something you can run on a workstation and something you cannot; the report should say more about where the 4 billion model specifically fails, because a 5 point F1 gap in a safety system is not a small gap. Third, the agentic verification component, retrieval-based evidence checking, is where the real risk reduction lives, and retrieval quality will dominate in production, so the 73.9 percent retrieval-enabled number is the number to stress-test: what happens when the retrieval index is incomplete or the evidence is contradictory. And fourth, the six-expert decomposition is a design choice, and the ablations on supervision sources are the section that tells you whether the capability experts earn their keep or whether the guardrail teacher alone gets you most of the way.

Bottom line: LingSante MedGuard is a solid, well-scoped guardrail report: one model, three scales, a released 11-subset benchmark, and a two-stage teacher-distillation recipe that replaces an expert ensemble with a single deployable student. The near-flat 4 to 9 billion curve is the quietly important result, because it says the small model is where the supervision's value actually lives, and that is the curve a deployment decision should key on. The self-reported benchmark is the main asterisk, and the 4 billion model's failure profile is the question that matters for actual deployment.
