Confidence Comes from Experience: Experiential Confidence Estimation from Reasoning to Agents. The first three authors are Caiqi Zhang, Xiaochen Zhu, and Chengzu Li, and the work comes out of the University of Cambridge, with one author at Google DeepMind.

The motivation is a failure mode you have probably seen in an agent log. A model proposes a fix, runs the tests, sees them pass, and then reports high stated confidence, "tests passed, so I'm confident the fix is correct." But a green test is not the same thing as a correct fix, and the model's verbal confidence is not calibrated to whether it is actually right. The paper's premise is that a model's own stated confidence is a weak signal, and that a much better signal lives in the model's past experience: if you keep a record of similar tasks, with what the model's initial confidence was and whether it ultimately succeeded, you can recalibrate its present confidence against that history. That is the "experiential" part.

There is a deeper reason stated confidence is unreliable, and it is worth making explicit because it motivates the whole approach. A model's verbal confidence is produced by the same forward pass that produced the answer, so it is a readout of the model's internal state at the moment of answering, before it has any information about whether the answer was right. It is essentially a prior over the question, conditioned on the question text, not on the outcome. The experiential estimate, by contrast, is a posterior: it is the observed success rate over a set of past episodes that looked like this one, which is a quantity that is actually grounded in realized outcomes rather than in the model's own introspection. That prior-versus-posterior gap is the entire conceptual content of the paper, and it is why the method does not try to make the model better at stating confidence; it sidesteps the stated confidence almost entirely and replaces it with an empirical estimate from the bank, using the stated confidence only as a key for retrieving the right neighborhood.

The method has three pieces. First, the model solves a task and emits a stated confidence, the usual self-assessment. Second, before committing, it retrieves from an experience bank the set of similar tasks that had similar initial confidence, so it is comparing the present situation against a neighborhood of its own past. Third, it recalibrates the confidence using the success rate of that retrieved neighborhood. The bank is the accumulating artifact. As the agent does more tasks, it logs episodes, the task, the stated confidence, and the realized outcome, so the retrieval gets richer and the recalibration gets better. The paper reports that calibration improves as the bank grows, holding everything else fixed and growing the bank from a small size upward.

The scope of the paper is also worth stating, because it is broader than the title suggests. The authors frame this as a bridge from reasoning to agents. The same recalibration mechanism applies to a pure reasoning model that answers a question and states a confidence, and to a tool-using agent that takes a sequence of actions and states a confidence in the final answer. That is why the paper's title says "from reasoning to agents," and why the experiments span both a set of reasoning benchmarks and agentic settings. The claim is not that one of those domains is the point; the claim is that the experiential signal transfers across the line between single-shot reasoning and multi-step agency, which is a stronger statement than it first looks like and a harder one to make true.

One implementation detail matters for the agent side. In an agentic trajectory the final state is often mechanically close to the label. If the task ends with the model having written out the numeric reward or the pass-or-fail result, then a confidence estimator that reads the tail of the trajectory is, in effect, reading the answer. The paper calls this out directly and treats the honest operating point as the pre-hoc one, the confidence the agent commits before it has seen the terminal evidence. That distinction, between a pre-hoc estimate you can actually act on and a post-hoc score that is tautological, is the kind of thing that separates a deployable calibration method from a benchmark artifact, and it is why the paper reports the pre-hoc numbers as the ones that count.

The paper is unusually careful about what it claims and what it controls for, which is the part I would point a reviewer to. They check that the estimate is not just a difficulty predictor. Random neighbors score at chance, a mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of about 0.494 over the reasoning cells, so the retrieval is doing real work and not accidentally sorting by which questions are hard. They also control for difficulty explicitly: they match on difficulty so that the comparison holds the base success rate constant, and they show the estimate survives shrinking the actor, repeating the experiment at 397 billion, 27 billion, 9 billion, and 4 billion parameter models, where task accuracy falls as the model shrinks but the experiential estimate still carries signal. There is also an honesty note about a self-improving actor being non-stationary: as the model improves, older episodes in the bank gradually become "another model's," so the calibration memory has to be treated as tied to the actor that produced it, not as a timeless resource.

On the operating side, the practical payoff is abstention. If you rank by the estimated confidence and drop the lowest 10 percent of episodes, the delivered accuracy of the remaining set rises by about 4.8 points averaged across the reasoning sets. That is the concrete number to hold them to. There is also a second-call variant, where a reflection step that reads the retrieved cards improves calibration, with a calibration error 1.4 to 2.6 times lower than the baseline. The paper leans on the Brier score for a specific methodological reason: it is immune to the score-flattening that makes a near-zero expected calibration error achievable in a degenerate way, because a calibrator that just huddles all of its scores near the base rate will report a small ECE without actually discriminating. That is a genuine methodological point, because ECE alone can be gamed by a degenerate near-constant predictor.

A skeptical read. What I trust is the rigor of the controls, not the headline. The difficulty-matching, the random-neighbor baseline at chance, and the cross-size robustness are the right things to check, and the refusal to lean on ECE is a sign the authors have been burned by degenerate calibration before. The abstention result, dropping the lowest decile for a 4.8 point accuracy gain, is the number I would actually use, because it is an operating point you can deploy: if you are running agents in production and you can afford to abstain on the low-confidence tail, this gives you a mechanism to do it with a calibrated threshold rather than a raw verbal probability.

What I would re-verify before adopting. First, the non-stationarity problem is underweighted in the framing. If your actor keeps improving, the bank is quietly going stale, and the paper acknowledges this but the fix is not developed into a principled forgetting or re-weighting rule. In a long-running self-improving system that is the thing that would actually bite you. Second, retrieval quality is load-bearing. The whole method is only as good as the neighborhood you retrieve, and "similar tasks with similar initial confidence" is a similarity that has to be defined, and the paper's results across four reasoning sets suggest it is sensitive to how you embed the tasks. The bottom line is that this is a careful, well-controlled paper that makes a modest but real claim: past experience is a better confidence signal than present self-assessment, and it can be turned into a calibrated abstention threshold. It is not a new model or a big benchmark; it is a clean evaluation-and-recalibration recipe, and the controls are the part worth remembering.
