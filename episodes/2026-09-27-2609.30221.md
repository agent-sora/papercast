WanPE, Towards Cinematic Prompt Enhancement for Modern Text-to-Video Generation, by Yubo Zhu, Yawen Shao, and Ziyun Dai, from Nanjing University, the Wan Team at Alibaba Group, and the University of Science and Technology of China. The Wan Team authors are the dominant group on the paper, with the project led from there, and the academic co-authors cover Nanjing, USTC, Fudan, and Tsinghua.

The problem this paper addresses follows from where text-to-video generation has arrived. Modern generators now scale to 30-second outputs and follow complex conditions faithfully, which means the textual prompt has become the director. It has to plan how actions, camera trajectories, lighting, and sound unfold across multi-shot sequences. A raw user prompt like a one-line description is not a screenplay, and the quality of the video is increasingly capped by the quality of the text going in. The failure mode this is fixing is specific: when you give a modern generator a one-line brief, it produces a coherent single shot, but when you ask for a 30-second sequence with multiple scenes, the generator has no plan and the result is a single stretched moment rather than a sequence, because the model was never told which shots to cut between, in what order, with what camera moves. So the paper proposes a prompt enhancement model: a large language model that rewrites user prompts into shot-level cinematic plans before the video generator ever sees them, and the enhancer is the only thing that changes between the two arms of the evaluation.

The model is WanPE, at 397 billion parameters, which is an unusually large number for a prompt rewriter and a deliberate signal about how much structure the authors want in the plan. It is trained on 1.05 million real-world videos. Two design decisions carry most of the method. The first is video-grounded reverse construction: instead of rewriting the user prompt forward into a fancier prompt, the training data is built by starting from real videos and constructing the shot-level plan that would produce them, then learning to invert that process. The second is a reinforcement learning objective they call SC-GRPO, Semantic-Consistency GRPO, which is a group-relative policy optimization variant whose reward is computed by a judge model, Qwen3.7-Max, scoring whether the rewritten plan preserves the user's specified constraints. The judge scores on a scale from 0 to 100 across nine dimensions covering style, subject, and the other axes of a cinematic plan. The point of the semantic-consistency reward is straightforward: a 397B model rewriting your prompt will happily add shots, characters, and camera moves you never asked for, and the reward is there to keep it honest to the original intent. The judge's scoring is specific about what it checks. It looks for omitted, weakened, altered, or contradictory user requirements; incorrect bindings between subjects and their attributes, or between speakers and their dialogue; and action or camera moves that were never specified. The training signal is a text-only reward on a scale from 0 to 100, and the RL stage runs on roughly 15,000 prompts, which is a small number by RL standards and a sign that the authors are treating the RL phase as a targeted refinement of a strong supervised base rather than a from-scratch optimization.

The benchmark is WanPEval, a human-annotated testbed covering clip durations from 5 to 30 seconds across varying levels of intent granularity, meaning the same scene is prompted with a one-line description, a medium spec, or a fully detailed brief, and the enhancer has to do proportionally more or less work. The evaluation is supported by about 11,000 blind pairwise assessments, which is a real human study rather than a VLM judge. The headline result is measured as human preference for the video generated from the enhanced prompt versus the raw user prompt, with Wan3.0, the in-house generator, held constant as the video model in both arms. At 5 to 15 second clips, WanPE-397B lifts preference by between 10.66 and 18.84 points. At 30 seconds the gain jumps to 50.86 points. The paper's interpretation is that long multi-shot generations are where raw prompts break down hardest, so that is where a plan-first enhancer pays off most. The evaluation also covers the intermediate regime where the enhancer has to add the most structure: the granularity axis is what lets the paper measure how much value the enhancer creates when the user gives it almost nothing to work with, versus the case where the user has already written a detailed brief.

The ablations are where the method earns its keep. First, reverse construction beats forward rewriting: the data built by inverting real videos into plans produces a meaningfully better enhancer than data built by rewriting prompts forward. Second, SC-GRPO is what closes the semantic-fidelity gap. In the paper's consistency table, the 397B model after plain supervised fine-tuning scores 75.5 on semantic consistency, with 36.9 percent of rewrites counted as outright failures of the user's constraints. Adding SC-GRPO raises consistency to 97.6 and drops the failure rate to 2.8 percent. That is a large and specific win: the reinforcement stage is not making the plans fancier, it is making them obey the brief. And the gain carries to the downstream video: under the same Wan3.0 generator, expert preference for the generated video moves from 42.70 to 49.69 after SC-GRPO, with improvements across all seven content categories and the largest gains in knowledge, singing and dance, and speech content.

For context on the competitive landscape, the paper notes that modern commercial text-to-video systems all already ship proprietary prompt enhancers: HappyHorse 1.1, Kling 3.0, MiniMax-H3, and the Seedance family all have native enhancement components whose designs are undisclosed, with MiniMax-H3 being the notable exception in exposing its enhancer as a separate accessible model. WanPE is, in effect, Alibaba open-sourcing the equivalent of its own in-house component, which is why the paper can evaluate head-to-head against the commercial field. The bottom line of that comparison: at 5 to 15 seconds WanPE leads all evaluated commercial offerings, and at 30 seconds it remains competitive with Seedance 2.5 rather than clearly ahead.

Skeptical assessment. What I would trust: the evaluation design. Blind human pairwise preference with the video generator held fixed isolates the prompt enhancer's contribution cleanly, 11,000 human judgments is a proper sample size, and the semantic-consistency failure-rate metric (2.8 percent after RL) is the single most useful number for an engineer deciding whether to adopt an enhancer, because it directly bounds how often the model will betray the user's brief. The ablation attributing that drop specifically to SC-GRPO is also the kind of result that tells you which piece to keep if you are going to build your own smaller version.

What I would re-verify: the 30-second number. A 50.86 point preference gain is large enough to be suspicious, and it is the number the abstract leads with. The plausible explanation is that raw prompts are simply incoherent as 30-second multi-shot direction, so the baseline is weak, but I would want to see the per-category breakdown and the rater variance before quoting it externally. The 397B parameter count is also a cost question: prompt enhancement is a pre-step that runs on every generation request, and a model that big in the serving path is a real infrastructure decision, not a free quality win. The paper's own scale study shows SC-GRPO preserves semantic fidelity across model scales, which implies a smaller model plus the same RL recipe is a viable path, but the full 397B system is what was benchmarked.

Bottom line: WanPE is a serious, well-evaluated open release of a production-grade prompt enhancer for long video generation, and its core technical contribution is the SC-GRPO recipe that cuts constraint-failure rates from about 37 percent to under 3 percent. If you are generating multi-shot video at scale, the enhancer-plus-RL pattern is worth adopting; if you are, the paper gives you the recipe, the benchmark, and the commercial comparison in one place.
