Ensemble Routing for Prompt-Adaptive Cost-Aware LLM Inference, by Saul Urso, Emanuele Carlini, and Patrizio Dazzi, from CNR's Institute of Information Science and Technologies and CNR's Institute of Informatics and Telematics in Italy, with a co-author at the University of Pisa, is a routing paper that goes past the usual pick-one-model-per-prompt setup and lets the router choose an entire ensemble, deciding both which models to call and how many of them, under a cost-quality tradeoff you can dial at inference time without retraining.

The framing is standard cost-aware routing, generalized. You have a fixed pool of candidate models, each with an unknown prompt-dependent probability of being correct and a normalized inference cost. Single-model routing picks the model that maximizes a utility that is alpha times the predicted accuracy plus one minus alpha times the normalized cost, where alpha is the cost-quality preference. Ensemble routing generalizes the action space from one model to any non-empty subset of models, all queried in parallel, with their outputs combined by a response resolver. The two settings differ only in the action space: single routing picks one element of the pool, ensemble routing picks a subset. The hard part is that you cannot estimate the ensemble's success by training a separate model for every possible combination, so the paper's core contribution is a surrogate for ensemble success built from the individual capability estimates.

The single-model method, PISA, works like this. Prompts are embedded, the fit-set embeddings are clustered with X-means, which picks the number of clusters as well as the centroids, and each query is represented by a soft membership vector over the centroids, computed from cosine distances with a concentration parameter, so a query draws information from several neighboring clusters rather than one hard assignment. On top of those soft memberships, PISA trains one independent logistic-regression head per candidate model, predicting that model's probability of a correct answer on this prompt, with the regularization strength chosen per model by five-fold cross-validation over ten log-spaced values. The router then picks the model with the highest cost-aware utility for the given alpha. Cost comes from each model's average observed cost on the training set, normalized by the most expensive model's average cost.

The extension, PISA-E, reuses exactly those individual capability estimates and does not train anything ensemble-specific. The quality term for a candidate ensemble is a strict-majority success surrogate: the probability that more than half of the selected models are correct, computed under the assumption that the models' correctness is conditionally independent given the prompt, using the standard Poisson-binomial dynamic program. That is a deliberate surrogate, not a claim that the ensemble actually succeeds whenever a strict majority does; the final answer also depends on the response resolver, but the surrogate is what lets you rank candidate subsets before you have any responses in hand. The cost term is the sum of the members' average costs, normalized by the sum of the top k-max average costs, which is constant across candidates, so ensembles of different sizes are comparable.

Candidate selection is greedy. Models are ranked by their single-model utility under the same cost normalization, and the candidate ensembles are the odd-cardinality prefixes of that ranking, up to k-max. Only odd cardinalities are considered, and the paper gives a clean argument for why this does not lose anything under the surrogate: removing one member from any even-cardinality ensemble both lowers the required majority count and lowers cost, so the odd subset is never worse on the utility term. That turns the search over subsets, which would be exponential, into a linear scan over prefixes, and the whole ensemble decision reduces to comparing at most k-max candidates that all share the same capability estimates. The selected ensemble is queried in parallel, so the wall-clock cost is the latency of the slowest member rather than the sum, which matters when you are choosing a four-model ensemble over a single expensive one. The responses are combined with a self-weighted BERTScore resolver, a capability-aware variant of minimum Bayes risk decoding where each response is weighted by its model's estimated capability relative to the other selected models, and the response with the highest weighted similarity to the rest wins. The capability weighting is doing real work: it down-weights a cheap model's response in favor of a stronger one when the two agree loosely, so the resolver is not a flat majority vote.

The evaluation uses four LLM routing benchmarks under in-distribution and out-of-distribution conditions, covering tasks like MMLU-Pro, SimpleQA, ArenaHard, and tau-bench among others, and compares against existing single-model routers and ensemble approaches. The out-of-distribution condition is the interesting one, because a router's capability predictors are learned from historical outcomes on the fit set, and any deployment sees prompts that drift from that distribution; a router that only works in-distribution is mostly a lookup table. The headline results: PISA achieves the highest macro-average cost-performance hypervolume among the single-model routers in both settings, ranking first in six of the eight benchmark-setting combinations. PISA-E achieves the highest macro-average task performance among the ensemble approaches when quality is prioritized, beating the strongest competitor by 2.09 points in-distribution and 2.87 out-of-distribution. And at selected lower-cost operating points, it matches or beats the strongest ensemble competitors' task performance while cutting average inference cost by about 36 percent in-distribution and 25 percent out-of-distribution. Comparisons against fixed-cardinality variants show that adapting the ensemble size per prompt is worth something on its own.

There are two properties of the design worth calling out, because they are what make this practically different from a lot of the routing literature. First, the cost-quality preference alpha is a pure inference-time knob: you move it along the cost-quality frontier without retraining or re-optimizing hyperparameters, which is exactly what a service with different SLAs per customer tier would want, and it means the same trained router can serve a latency-sensitive endpoint and a batch endpoint with different cost tolerances from a single model. Second, the ensemble success is estimated without ever observing ensemble behavior, so adding a new model to the pool only requires collecting that model's per-prompt correctness and cost, not re-estimating every combination it participates in.

Now the skeptical pass. The strict-majority surrogate assumes conditional independence of model correctness given the prompt, which is a strong assumption for models that share training data and failure modes; if two models in the ensemble fail together on the same prompts, the Poisson-binomial overstates the ensemble's reliability, and the whole ranking inherits that bias. The paper compares against exhaustive surrogate optimization in an appendix, which addresses the greedy-prefix question, but not the independence question. The BERTScore resolver is a semantic-similarity pick among candidate responses, not a verification, so on hard prompts where every response is wrong it will confidently pick the most similar wrong answer, and the capability weighting does not fix that. The reported deltas over the strongest ensemble competitors are 2 or 3 points, which is real but within the range where benchmark choice and OOD split construction matter a lot, so I would want the per-benchmark tables before taking the aggregate claims at face value. And the cost model is a flat per-model average, not token-metered, so in a deployment where a cheap model's cost varies wildly with output length, the normalized cost term is only a rough proxy.

Bottom line: this is a clean, well-motivated extension of cost-aware routing to prompt-adaptive ensembles, and the two genuinely useful properties are the inference-time cost-quality dial and the fact that ensemble success is estimated from per-model predictors without combination-specific training. The independence assumption behind the majority surrogate is the main theoretical weakness, and the margins over existing ensemble methods are modest enough that the practical case rests on the flexibility of the operating points more than on the raw accuracy wins. For a team already running a pool of models with heterogeneous price and quality profiles, this is the kind of router worth benchmarking against your own traffic before committing.
