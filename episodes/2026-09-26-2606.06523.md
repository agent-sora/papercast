Lean4Agent: Formal Modeling and Verification for Agent Workflow and
Trajectory, by Ruida Wang, Jerry Huang, Pengcheng Wang, and co-authors Xuanqing
Liu, Luyang Kong, and Tong Zhang, from the University of Illinois at Urbana-
Champaign, with two of the co-authors working as independent researchers.

This is a paper about what happens when you take the machinery that formal
methods people have spent decades building for operating systems and
compilers, and point it at the new kind of program that is an agent loop.
The starting observation is a familiar one stated bluntly: large language
models can now execute multi-step workflows, but the systems that run them
have almost none of the formal apparatus that traditional software has. There
is no specification language you can write down what the agent is supposed to
do. There is no verifier you can check the workflow against before it runs.
And when a trajectory goes wrong, there is no systematic way to say which
step violated which requirement. The paper frames this as mirroring a
long-standing problem in mathematics, where the ambiguity of natural language
has motivated the development of formal languages, and the argument is that
agent systems need the same kind of formal specification and verification
machinery that mathematics eventually adopted.

The proposal, Lean4Agent, is what the authors describe as, to their knowledge,
the first framework that uses Lean 4, a dependently typed formal language, to
model and verify agent behavior. The design has two layers, and the
distinction between them is the core of the contribution. The first layer is
the workflow model: before anything runs, the agent's intended behavior is
written down in Lean 4 as a formal specification, including the assumptions
under which the workflow is supposed to hold. This is the part that formal
methods people would recognize immediately, a state machine or a set of
invariants expressed in a type theory, where a specification that does not
type-check is simply rejected rather than silently interpreted. The second
layer is the trajectory verification: after the agent runs and produces a
trajectory, a sequence of actions and observations, the framework checks that
trajectory against the workflow model. The interesting property they build is
failure localization, which means that when a trajectory violates the
specification, the framework does not just say the run failed; it identifies
which step in the trajectory is the point of violation, under which
assumptions, so the debugging problem becomes a matter of inspecting a
localized region rather than reading an entire transcript.

The extensible library, FormalAgentLib, is the concrete artifact that carries
this, and it is worth describing what extensible means in this context. The
library provides the Lean 4 definitions for the agent concepts: an action, an
observation, a step, a trajectory, a workflow property, and the
relationship between a trajectory and the property it is supposed to satisfy.
Because these are definitions in a general-purpose proof assistant rather than
hard-coded checks in a framework, a new agent design can be specified by
writing new Lean 4 definitions against the library, and the verification
machinery carries over. The paper frames the semantic-consistency property
explicitly: the workflow's formal model and the actual execution are checked
for agreement under explicit assumptions, and the assumptions are part of the
object of study rather than being hidden in the code. That last point is
methodologically important, because much of the time a trajectory violation is
really an assumption violation, the environment behaved outside the range the
specification was written for, and making the assumptions explicit is what
lets the framework distinguish those two cases.

The evaluation in the paper is a demonstration of the framework on a set of
agent workflows, where the authors write Lean 4 specifications for the
workflows, run agents that both satisfy and violate the specifications, and
show that the framework correctly accepts the satisfying trajectories and
localizes the violations in the failing ones. The significance of the result
is not the number of workflows, which is modest, but the fact that the whole
pipeline is machine-checked end to end: the specification is verified to be
well-formed Lean, the trajectory is encoded, and the satisfaction or
violation judgment is a theorem, or a counterexample with a location,
produced by the proof assistant rather than by a judge model. This distinction
between an assumption violation and a specification violation is where the
formal approach earns its keep over a black-box trace inspector, because a
model-based tracer can tell you that the run went wrong but cannot, in
general, tell you whether the fault lies in the agent's logic or in the
environment having behaved outside the range the specification was written
for, and the Lean 4 formulation makes that a checkable question with an
explicit answer rather than a judgment call. This echoes the distinction formal
methods have long drawn in the operating-systems and compiler literature
between a program that is buggy and a program whose preconditions were not
met, and the move here is to carry that separation over to agent
trajectories, where the precondition is the environmental assumption and the
bug is the workflow step that does not hold under it.

Now the skeptical read. The first question is scope. The workflows verified
in the paper are necessarily simplified versions of real agent behavior,
because writing a faithful Lean 4 specification of a complex, open-ended agent
loop is a major undertaking in itself, and the framework's value at scale
depends on how much of that specification effort is amortized across runs
versus paid once per agent design. The paper's own framing of why formal
methods matter here is worth pressing, because it draws a parallel to a
long-standing problem in mathematics, where the ambiguity of natural language
has motivated the development of formal languages, and the argument is that
agent workflows face the same ambiguity problem, just with the added
complication that the "specification" is supposed to describe a process that
interacts with an external world rather than a static mathematical object.
That is a reasonable position for a research contribution, but it does mean
that the practical case for the framework depends on agent systems getting
complex enough for the informal approach to fail, which may not happen for
simple agents that work well enough. The second question is the cost of the
encoding step. A trajectory has to be translated into the library's formal
language before it can be checked, and if that translation is itself done by a
language model, then the verification is only as good as the encoding, and a
faithful encoding of a rich trajectory may be as hard as the original
verification problem. The paper's framing of the trajectories suggests the
encoding is structured rather than free-form, which is the right design, but
the generality of the encoding relative to arbitrary agent outputs is the
thing to press. The third question is what the localization buys in practice.
If the developer has to read the Lean 4 specification to interpret the
localized violation, the practical debugging gain depends on the developer's
formal methods fluency, and the framework is most useful in exactly the teams
that already have that fluency, which is a real adoption constraint. And the
competitive landscape is moving: there is active work on process reward
models, trajectory verification by language models, and agent tracing tools,
so the question for this framework is not whether verification is needed,
which nobody disputes, but whether the formal approach gives enough precision
over the model-based approaches to justify its specification cost.

Bottom line: this is a clean and genuinely first-of-its-kind application of a
dependently typed proof assistant to agent workflow and trajectory
verification, with a two-layer design that separates the specification from
the execution and a localization mechanism that makes failures inspectable.
The open question is the economics of the approach, whether the one-time cost
of writing Lean 4 specifications amortizes well enough, and how far the
trajectory encoding generalizes beyond the structured workflows the paper
demonstrates on.
