Statistical Learning Theory in Lean 4, by Yuanhe Zhang from the Department
of Statistics at the University of Warwick, Jason Lee from the Department
of Electrical Engineering and Computer Sciences at the University of
California, Berkeley, and Fanghui Liu from the School of Mathematical
Sciences at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, which is a detail worth
holding onto, because this is a collaboration between a statistician, a
machine learning theorist, and a combinatorialist who works on discrete
mathematics, and that combination is exactly what a formalization of
statistical learning theory needs, because the subject sits at the
intersection of probability, analysis, and optimization, and each of those
pieces has a different formal tradition.

The question the paper is answering is a coverage question, which part of
statistical learning theory is actually formalized in the Lean 4
standard library, and the honest answer is almost none of the hard part.
The Lean 4 library, Mathlib, has a great deal of analysis, a great deal
of probability, and a great deal of linear algebra, and it has the
building blocks, but the statistical learning theory that a practitioner
actually uses, the concentration inequalities, the empirical process
theory, the generalization bounds, is not there, and the paper's
contribution is to build it, and the framing of the contribution is
specific, it is the first comprehensive Lean 4 formalization of
statistical learning theory grounded in empirical process theory. The
empirical-process grounding is the part that matters, because empirical
process theory is the modern language of statistical learning, it is the
framework that connects the concentration of the empirical average to the
complexity of the function class, and it is the framework that the
generalization bounds are stated in, so a formalization that is grounded
in empirical processes is a formalization that is in the right language,
not a formalization that is a bag of inequalities. The end-to-end formal
infrastructure implements the missing contents in the latest Mathlib, and
the paper is specific about what the missing contents are, and the list
is the contribution. The first is a complete development of Gaussian
Lipschitz concentration, which is the concentration inequality for the
supremum of a Gaussian process indexed by a Lipschitz function class, and
it is the workhorse of the Dudley integral, and the second is the first
formalization of Dudley's entropy integral theorem for sub-Gaussian
processes, which is the theorem that bounds the expected supremum of a
sub-Gaussian process in terms of the metric entropy of the index set, and
it is the theorem that turns a concentration statement into a
generalization bound, and the third is an application to least-squares
sparse regression with a sharp rate, which is the part that shows the
machinery is not just a formalization exercise, it produces a result, and
the sharp-rate property is the part that matters, because a formalized
rate that is not sharp is a statement that is true but weak, and the
paper's contribution is that the formalized result is at the minimax
level, which is the strongest rate the problem admits.

The workflow the paper uses is the part a reader should hold onto, because
it is the part that is reproducible and that a reader can imitate, and it
is described as a human-AI collaborative workflow in which humans design
proof strategies and AI agents execute tactical proof construction. The
division of labor is specific, the human decides what to prove and in what
order, which lemmas to state, which reductions to make, which estimates
to track, and the AI agent does the tactical work, which is the work of
filling in the Lean proof state, of finding the right lemma to apply, of
managing the dependent types and the coercions and the measure-theoretic
machinery, and the human verifies the result, the paper describes the
output as a human-verified Lean 4 toolbox for statistical learning theory,
and the human-verified property is the part that distinguishes this from
an AI-generated formalization that has not been checked. The paper also
reports a property of the process that is worth noting, that the
formalization process exposes and resolves implicit assumptions and missing
details in standard statistical learning theory textbooks, and that is the
part that is a contribution beyond the Lean code, because a formalization
that forces you to state the hypotheses you have been assuming is a
formalization that is doing mathematical work, and the line-by-line
understanding that the paper says it enforces is the part that is a
benefit to the mathematicians who did it, not just to the Lean library.

Now the skeptical read. The first question is the scope, because the
paper says comprehensive, and the honest version of the question is how
comprehensive, because comprehensive in a Lean 4 formalization means
comprehensive in the sense that the main theorems are there and the
proofs go through, and the question is which theorems, and the three the
paper names are the concentration, the entropy integral, and the
regression application, which is a solid core, but a reader should check
what is not there, because a statistical learning theory toolbox that
does not have the VC dimension machinery, or the Rademacher complexity, or
the uniform convergence theorems, is a toolbox that is a starting point,
and the value of the paper is as a foundation, and a reader should check
the repository to see the surface of the library, not just the three
results in the abstract. The second question is the sharp rate, because
the paper says sharp, and the honest version of the question is sharp
relative to what, because the paper says up to minimax-level, and
minimax-level is the right phrase, it means the rate matches the minimax
rate up to constants, and a reader should check whether the formalized
rate matches the published informal rate, because a formalization that
proves a rate that is weaker than the published rate is a formalization
that has lost something in translation, and the sharp-rate claim depends
on the formalized statement being the same as the informal one. The third
question is the workflow, because the human-AI collaborative workflow is
the part that is reproducible, and the honest version of the question is
how much of the work the AI did, because the paper says the AI executes
tactical proof construction, and the question is what fraction of the
proof state the AI closed versus what fraction the human had to do by
hand, and the paper should be read as a demonstration that the workflow
works, not as a measurement of how much of the work the AI did, and a
reader who wants to imitate the workflow should check the repository for
the actual Lean code and the actual proof strategy, because the workflow
is only reproducible if the strategy is written down. And the practical
question is the library dependency, because the formalization is in the
latest Mathlib, and the question for a reader who wants to use it is
whether it is in Mathlib proper or in a separate package, and the answer
matters, because a result that is in Mathlib is a result that is
maintained by the Mathlib community, and a result that is in a separate
package is a result that is maintained by its authors, and the
maintainability of a formalization is a function of where it lives, and a
reader should check where this toolbox lives, because the value of a
formalization is a function of its maintenance, not just its correctness.

Bottom line: this is a substantial and well-motivated formalization, the
first comprehensive Lean 4 development of statistical learning theory
grounded in empirical process theory, with a complete Gaussian Lipschitz
concentration, the first formalization of Dudley's entropy integral
theorem for sub-Gaussian processes, and a sharp-rate application to least-
squares sparse regression, produced through a human-AI collaborative
workflow in which humans design the proof strategy and AI agents do the
tactical construction, and the process exposed implicit assumptions in the
standard textbooks. The open question is how much of the statistical
learning theory surface the library covers beyond the three named results,
whether the formalized sharp rate matches the published informal rate, and
where the toolbox lives, in Mathlib proper or in a separate package,
because the maintainability of a formalization is a function of where it
lives.
