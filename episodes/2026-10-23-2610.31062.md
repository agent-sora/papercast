The paper is SCOPE: Compiling Natural-Language Policies into Neurosymbolic Predicate Programs, from Arnon Mazza and Elad Levi at Plurai, the two-author AI-safety company. This is a neurosymbolic paper with a very concrete product problem underneath it: policy enforcement for large language model agents. The problem has shifted from single-turn content moderation, where you classify one response, to compound, application-specific policies evaluated over multi-turn transcripts. The policy is natural language, the input is a whole conversation, and the judgment is a verdict about whether the conversation violated the policy. The two obvious approaches both fail in characteristic ways, and the paper is built around that failure pair. Small models are efficient, but they degrade on compound policies, the kind that say the agent must check X, then only if X is true check Y, and refuse if either branch fires. Large-model judges get higher accuracy but cost a lot at inference and produce unstable predictions, the same input flipping verdicts run to run. SCOPE's answer is to compile the policy into an executable program: a set of neural predicates, each answered by a small model, plus deterministic extractors and Python code that composes their outputs into the final verdict.

The compilation loop is the method's core, and it has four parts that run until the program stabilizes. The input is a natural-language policy and a small seed pool of samples. The optimizer decomposes the policy into a program of predicates: each predicate is a narrow question that a small model can answer reliably, the extractors pull out the facts the predicates need, like which tools the agent called or what the user asked for, and the code composes the predicate answers into the verdict. The adversarial generator is the part that makes it a loop rather than a one-shot decomposition: it searches for counterexamples, inputs where the program's verdict deviates from the policy, and it does this at two levels. A local counterexample breaks a single predicate, so the generator finds an input where one small model's answer is wrong and adds it to that predicate's training pool. A systemic counterexample breaks the composition as a whole, an input where every predicate is individually right but the code that composes them gets the verdict wrong, and those go into a pool that retrains the composition. The program and its training data are optimized jointly, and the loop repeats until the counterexample rate drops. That is the neurosymbolic claim made operational: the neural part is small and narrow, the symbolic part is explicit code you can read, and the adversarial loop is what keeps the two honest.

The predicate program as an object deserves a second look, because it is what makes the system auditable in a way the baselines are not. A compiled policy is a short Python file: a list of extractors that pull facts out of the transcript, a set of predicate calls to the small models, each returning a structured answer rather than prose, and a composition function that maps those answers to a verdict. Because the composition is code, a human reviewer can read the policy as it is actually enforced, find the branch where two clauses interact, and see exactly which predicate supplies the input to which decision. The prompt-optimized judge has no such surface; its policy lives in a prompt and a latent judgment, and the workflow-generation methods produce pipelines, but those pipelines are generated artifacts whose logic is only as legible as the generator's output. There is a second structural property worth noting: the predicates are narrow on purpose. Each one answers a single question a small model can handle, like did the agent call a tool the policy restricts, or did the user provide consent in the last turn, and the complexity of the policy lives entirely in the composition. That is the neurosymbolic division of labor, neural for perception, symbolic for logic, and it is the reason the system's failures are localizable, a wrong verdict can be traced to a single predicate or a single composition branch rather than dissolved in a large model's output.

The evaluation is on a benchmark of real-world policy tasks, and the comparison set is the right one for the problem: prompt optimization, where you tune the judge's instructions; task decomposition, where you split the policy into steps for the judge to follow; and workflow generation, where a model builds a fixed pipeline. SCOPE reports the highest average accuracy across tasks and models, which is the headline, but the more interesting result is the transfer one. Programs optimized with the weakest executor, the smallest model, transfer to other models without further optimization, and they can be deployed in production on inexpensive models. That is a strong practical claim, because it means the expensive part of the system, the compilation loop, runs once with strong models, and the cheap part, executing the compiled program with small models, is what runs in production. The paper is thirty-three pages, and the predicate programs themselves are part of the artifact: because the decomposition is explicit code, an auditor can read the policy as enforced, which neither the prompt-optimized judge nor the generated workflow gives you.

The transfer result deserves the last word on the evidence, because it is what separates this from another accuracy table. The paper's claim is that programs compiled with the weakest executor, the smallest model in their test set, transfer to other, stronger models without any further optimization of the program, and that the production deployment then runs on inexpensive models. The logic of the claim is that the program's value is in its structure, the decomposition into narrow predicates and the composition logic, and the executor model only supplies the narrow answers, so a stronger executor improves the predicate answers without invalidating the composition. The direction that matters for deployment is the one they test: compile weak, execute anywhere. The reverse direction, compile with a strong model and execute with a weak one, is not claimed, and I would not read it into the paper, because a strong model may discover a decomposition that presupposes predicate answers a weak model cannot produce reliably. The production framing is what a safety team would actually use: run the expensive compilation once, offline, with the strongest available models and the adversarial generator, and then run the compiled program in the hot path with small models, getting a legible, low-cost, stable verdict. The auditability argument, which I have already made, is the durable reason to prefer this shape of system even when the accuracy gap over a tuned large judge is small.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the framing, because the small-model-versus-large-judge tradeoff is real and the neurosymbolic middle, narrow neural predicates under explicit composition, is the right shape of solution for auditable policy enforcement. I would re-verify two things. First, the benchmark is the authors' own set of real-world policy tasks, and the counterexample-driven training means the method is optimized to close the gaps it knows how to find; I would want to see the same compiled programs tested on policy families they were not adversarially trained against, because the adversarial generator's coverage is a property of the training data, not of the method. Second, the transfer claim is the strongest and the one I would press hardest on: a program compiled with the weakest executor transferring to stronger models is plausible, and the reverse direction, a program compiled with a strong model degrading when executed by a weaker one in production, is where compiled neurosymbolic systems tend to break, and the paper's deployment story should be read in the direction it actually demonstrates. The auditability argument is real and durable, and it is the reason to prefer this family of methods over a tuned large judge even when the accuracy gap is modest.

Bottom line: SCOPE is a well-motivated neurosymbolic system for agent policy enforcement that compiles natural-language policies into readable programs of small-model predicates, and the adversarial counterexample loop with its local-versus-systemic split is the genuinely useful idea. The accuracy results beat the right baselines, and the compiled programs transfer to cheaper executors, but before putting it in a production safety role I would test against unseen policy families and verify the transfer direction actually used in deployment, because the adversarial training is only as broad as the counterexamples it was allowed to find.
