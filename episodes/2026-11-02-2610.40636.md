Probing Spatial State Representations in Visual Foundation Models, by Baiqiao Yin, Zhongang Cai, and Ruisi Wang, from New York University, Nanyang Technological University, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, and the Chinese University of Hong Kong, is an interpretability study that asks whether the internal representations of pretrained visual models actually contain a persistent spatial state, the kind of thing that lets an observer keep track of what is beyond the current view as it moves through a scene. The setup is deliberately controlled: they use frozen-feature probes, so nothing about the models is fine-tuned, and they probe ten different representations, a mix of image and video foundation models, under streaming egocentric observations of static indoor environments. The word frozen is the whole method, and it is the right choice for the question, because if you fine-tune the model you are asking it to learn to do spatial tracking rather than measuring whether it already represents it, and a frozen probe isolates what is recoverable from the existing representations from what the model would need to be trained to produce.

They organize the analysis into four levels of spatial capability, which is the structure of the paper and the thing worth understanding. Level one is spatial structure, can you read out where things are in the current view, the easiest. Level two is object persistence, can you track an object after it has left the view, which requires a memory that persists under self-motion. Level three is predictive spatial update, can you predict where a feature will be after the camera moves, which is forward modeling. Level four is actionability, can the representation actually drive a useful action, the level that connects perception to control. The central finding is that these four capabilities do not come together: a strong readout of the visible scene, level one, does not ensure accurate object persistence, level two, or future-feature retrieval, level three. That is the result that matters, and it is a negative result in the sense that it breaks the assumption that a model that can describe what it sees can also track what it has seen and predict what it will see. The matched controls are the methodological strength here, they separate readout accuracy from reliance on visual history and from reliance on motion, so a high score on a spatial task cannot be explained away by the probe just copying the current frame or the motion input.

The numbers are specific and I will give you a few. On the streaming readout, among the model-layer-prefix cells, the best observed endpoint prediction is around 29.4 degrees, and longer visual prefixes do not uniformly improve spatial readout, which is a genuinely surprising result, you would expect more history to help, and instead among the endpoint pairs a large majority favor the shorter prefix, with three of the models, DINOv2, Qwen2.5-VL, and VideoMAE, actually getting worse with a longer prefix beyond their confidence intervals. On the future-feature retrieval, the models diverge in a way that tells you something about their training: for V-JEPA2, simply copying the last observed feature beats using the predicted one, nineteen point nine percent versus thirteen point zero four percent, which means the model's own prediction is worse than doing nothing, while for Wan the prediction is better, fifteen point two two percent versus eleven point nine six percent. And on the motion condition, correct motion improves retrieval over matched wrong motion by eighteen point eight four, ten point five one, and four point three five percentage points for V-JEPA2, VideoMAE, and CogVideoX, with Wan's two point nine points spanning zero. The cross-model spread is the finding: these models were trained differently, V-JEPA2 with a prediction objective, the others with different ones, and the probing reveals that the training objective shows up directly in whether the internal representation can predict the future or just describe the present.

The actionability level is the most sobering. They test goal-dependent candidate selection, and the result is that it does not establish reliable continuous action utility, the predicted actions do respond to goal changes, so the representation is not frozen to a single policy, but supplying the correct goal does not reliably improve progress toward it. That is a precise and deflating result, the representation has some goal sensitivity, but it is not actionable in the way a control system needs, and the gap between having a spatial representation and being able to act on it is large. The dependency structure is the cleanest takeaway, and it is where the cross-model correlations do the work. Camera-pose readout is the root ingredient: the cross-model rank correlation between camera-pose readout and object persistence is a strong 0.76, which is the paper saying the two are nearly the same thing measured differently, whereas the correlation between depth or view-consistency readout and persistence is weak, under 0.1 in absolute value, so visible-scene readout does not buy you persistence. The four representations that are best at both camera-pose and persistence, DINOv2, Qwen2.5-VL, V-JEPA2, and VideoMAE, are, after the dedicated reconstruction model Fast3R, also the four best on depth and view consistency, which is the consistency check that the capability profiles are not noise. Fast3R is the interesting outlier: it has the strongest camera-pose and persistence readouts of all ten, because it was trained explicitly on multi-view reconstruction and camera estimation, so it carries a persistent state by design, and on the candidate-selection test the probe picks between two candidate camera motions by their progress toward the goal, reaching 75.9 to 79.9 percent accuracy against a 67.8 percent action-only baseline, with shuffling the goals dropping accuracy by 12 to 21 points, which is the evidence that the readout genuinely uses the goal rather than ignoring it. The paper's summary of the dependency structure is the cleanest takeaway: object persistence and actionability build on camera-pose readout, while feature prediction builds on visible-scene readout and is unrelated to persistence, so the capabilities are not a flat stack but a specific dependency graph, and the edges that are missing are the ones that matter for building an embodied agent on top of these models.

A few things I would want to verify. The ten representations are all frozen and the probes are linear or near-linear readouts, so a high readout is an upper bound on what a simple probe can recover, and a low readout does not prove the information is absent, only that it is not linearly accessible, which is the standard limitation of probing and the paper should be read with it. The static indoor environment is a narrow slice of the world, and the results may not transfer to dynamic scenes, which are exactly the ones that would stress object persistence. And the actionability test is a candidate-selection setup, a specific way of asking whether this representation is usable for control, so the negative result is about that test, and a different action formulation might extract more, though the fact that goal changes are registered and correct goals do not help is the strongest version of the negative. One more detail is worth flagging because it qualifies a headline: the layer analysis shows the designated extraction layers do not uniformly win, VideoMAE leads by about nine degrees at the designated layers while the best single cell, at twenty nine point four degrees of endpoint error, comes from a different extraction setting, which is the paper being honest that there is no universal best layer and the layer-by-prefix sweep is the real object of study, not any single reported number.

My verdict is that this is a careful and honest interpretability study, and its real contribution is the four-level decomposition with the matched controls, which cleanly separates readout from history from motion and shows that the three do not travel together. The finding that strong scene readout does not imply object persistence or future prediction, and that the training objective shows up directly in whether the representation can predict, is the result to take away, and the actionability gap is the result that should caution anyone planning to build an embodied agent on a pretrained visual model, because the spatial state these models have is a description, not yet a control signal.
