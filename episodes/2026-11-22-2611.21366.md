ALIVE, by Jinzhao Li, Yuan Xu, and Jinxuan Wu, at Tsinghua University, Peking University, and ByteDance, is an interactive streaming video model. It turns a video LLM that passively watches a stream into one that maintains a list of user tasks over time, updates those tasks when the user changes their mind, and asks for more visual evidence when what it can see is not enough. The paper reports that the resulting model achieves the highest overall scores among the compared models on four public streaming benchmarks plus a new benchmark the authors call FSI-Bench, and it preserves the base model's ordinary streaming understanding while doing so. Code and a project page are released, and the authors position the work as a step toward always-on multimodal assistants in the JARVIS mold.

The gap the paper targets is real, and the framing is honest. Today's streaming video models can process incoming video continuously, reason about past observations, describe ongoing activity, and monitor for events as they happen. But they handle user requests in isolation. You ask one question, it answers, and the next request is treated as a fresh situation. There is no persistent notion of an active task, no mechanism for "instead, watch for something else," and no behavior where the model says, in effect, I cannot read that yet, please hold the map closer. The authors argue this missing interactivity is what keeps streaming models from being a foundation for multimodal interactive AI. Their formulation decomposes interactive streaming video understanding into three core capabilities: proactive monitoring, where the model watches for events the user asked about and speaks up when they happen; streaming task coordination, where concurrent tasks are maintained, modified, or canceled as the stream unfolds; and feedback-seeking interaction, where the model detects a missing prerequisite and asks the user to do something about it.

The data story is the first concrete contribution. The authors curate two datasets for two-stage post-training: ALIVE-SFT-288K, with 288 thousand supervised examples, and ALIVE-RL-3K, with 3 thousand reinforcement learning examples. In their comparison table, prior streaming datasets cover at most one or two of the three capabilities. The largest prior SFT datasets, like the 617 thousand entry VideoChat3-OL617K or the 465 thousand entry Streamo-Instruct, support proactive monitoring but not task coordination or feedback seeking. The prior RL datasets are all small, in the 1.9 to 11 thousand range, and again cover only monitoring. ALIVE-SFT-288K is the first dataset in the table that supports all three capabilities with multi-turn interactions, and ALIVE-RL-3K is the only RL dataset in the table that covers all three. That table is worth sitting with, because it says the interactive behaviors did not exist in training data for anyone before this paper. The models were not failing to learn them; there was nothing in the data to learn them from.

The training framework is two stages. Stage one is contrastive supervised fine-tuning. The idea is that the same observation can call for different behaviors depending on the interaction condition, so the data deliberately contains controlled variations of the same scene with different task states, and the supervision targets two things: strategy selection, meaning which of the capabilities should fire, and response gating, meaning whether to speak or stay silent at all. Plain SFT teaches what to say; contrastive SFT teaches when to say it and when to hold back, by putting near-identical situations side by side where the correct behavior differs. Stage two is reinforcement learning with something the authors call Behavior-Aligned Credit Assignment, or BACA. The problem with multi-turn credit assignment is that a trajectory-level reward, good or bad at the end of a long interaction, is a very blunt signal for any individual decision. BACA combines a trajectory-level advantage with local advantages computed over behavioral units: the decision to select a strategy, the decision to respond to a task, and the decision to stay critically silent. In other words, the model gets graded both on whether the whole interaction went well and on each of the three kinds of decisions it made along the way. The paper notes this is in the same family as GiGPO, which does episode-level and step-level advantages, but applied to the streaming interaction setting.

The second concrete contribution is FSI-Bench, a benchmark for feedback-seeking interaction and the task completion that follows it. The name is an acronym for feedback-seeking interaction, and it is designed to test exactly the behavior no prior dataset covered: the model notices it cannot proceed, asks the user for something, and then completes the task once the missing information arrives. The paper reports that ALIVE achieves the highest overall scores on FSI-Bench and on four public benchmarks, which are OVO-Bench, IPIBench, StreamingBench, and Proactive VideoQA, against compared models including JoyAI-VL-Interaction, AURA, and VideoChat3. The headline claim is that an offline video LLM can indeed be transformed through post-training into a capable interactive streaming video model, and that the transformation does not come at the cost of the original streaming understanding.

Now the skeptical part, because there are several load-bearing claims here that deserve scrutiny. First, the headline "highest overall scores" is a relative claim within a specific set of compared models, and the paper's own Figure 1 shows the comparisons as bar charts where the margins on the public benchmarks look modest next to the gap ALIVE opens on FSI-Bench, which is a benchmark the authors built. When the authors introduce the evaluation for the one capability no one else has trained, they also get to define what the score measures, so the biggest margin is partly self-referential. That does not make it fake, but it means the most impressive number is the one the authors have the most control over. Second, the BACA mechanism is described at a fairly high level in the parts of the paper I could verify: the combination of trajectory and local advantages is sound and in the mainstream, but the paper does not give a dramatic ablation showing how much of the gain comes from the local advantages versus plain GRPO with trajectory rewards. The dataset comparison table is strong; the credit-assignment ablation is weaker. Third, the scale of the RL set is small, 3 thousand examples, which is normal for this kind of post-training, but it means the RL stage is refining a narrow behavior space, and the ceiling is set largely by the SFT stage and its 288 thousand examples, whose construction is where most of the real engineering lived.

Still, the structural point of the paper is the durable one. The capabilities were missing from the training data, so no model had them; building the data and the two-stage training is a reusable recipe, not a one-off. The contrastive SFT design, supervising the gate and the strategy separately from the content, is a reasonable answer to the timing problem that plagues all streaming assistants: the model that always speaks is noisy, and the model that never speaks is useless. The feedback-seeking loop, where the model can resolve a missing prerequisite by asking the user, is also the piece that most resembles how a real assistant would need to behave in the physical world, and it is the least explored.

The data construction details matter for anyone who wants to reproduce the recipe. The contrastive SFT data is built around controlled variations of interaction conditions: the same scene under different task states, so the model sees that a given observation can correctly trigger a proactive alert in one state, a task update in another, and silence in a third. The response-gating supervision is what makes the model learn critical silence as a first-class behavior rather than an absence of output, and the RL stage's local advantages over behavioral units are what let the model be credited or penalized for the silence decision itself, not just for the content of the turns where it did speak. The paper positions BACA in the same family as multi-turn credit-assignment methods like GiGPO, which combine episode-level and step-level advantages, but applies the decomposition to the streaming interaction setting where the units are strategy selection, task response, and silence.

My verdict: ALIVE is a competent and well-scoped systems paper that fills a genuine gap in streaming video models with a reusable data-plus-training recipe, and its interactive behaviors are real. The main caveat is that its largest result sits on a self-built benchmark, and the credit-assignment contribution is not yet demonstrated as decisively as the data contribution. Worth reading if you work on streaming or interactive multimodal systems.
