A-RSI: Adaptive Task Refresh for Zero-Data Multimodal Self-Improvement, from Yu Liu, Youxing Li, and Yuedong Tan, spanning the Institute of Information Engineering of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, the University of Würzburg, and a long tail of industry and academic collaborators, is a paper about the under-discussed control problem in recursive self-improvement. Everyone in this space works on what to generate next, harder problems, tasks near the competence frontier, and so on. Almost nobody works on when the current training data has stopped being useful. This paper makes the when question a first-class citizen and answers it with signals that come from the training logs themselves, not from any evaluation run.

The setup is zero-data multimodal reinforcement learning. The model, a solver, is trained with group relative policy optimization on a pool of tasks, and the tasks and their reference answers are produced by a fixed library of programs that the base model wrote once, before training started. Each program samples parameters, draws an image, composes a question, and computes the reference answer from the same underlying configuration. The paper's example is a bar chart. The program samples two values, 18 and 11, draws the bars, asks how much larger one bar is than the other, and computes the answer, 7, from the stored values. The learner only sees the image and the question, never the latent numbers, and its response is scored against the program-computed answer. The point of this construction is that answer correctness does not depend on the model voting on its own output. No self-consistency, no majority vote. The reference answer comes from arithmetic on the configuration, so the supervision is independently checkable even though the whole loop uses no external training examples and no human-written labels.

The refresh problem. Training proceeds in windows, and in the main experiment each window is 100 group relative policy optimization updates on an active pool of 500 tasks. The question the controller answers after each window is whether the pool has saturated, meaning it is no longer producing useful learning signal, and if so, whether to swap in a fresh pool of 400 unused tasks. The key design constraint, and the one that distinguishes this from curriculum-learning work, is that the controller reads only training-side signals. It does not run a validation set, it does not look at test scores, and it does not estimate the model's competence frontier. That is a real constraint, because in a self-improvement loop the evaluation you would reach for is often the same model or a closely related one, which makes the evaluation part of the thing you are trying to improve.

The signal itself is simple and comes from a fact about group-based reinforcement learning. In group relative policy optimization, a prompt only contributes a nonzero advantage if the sampled responses within its group receive different rewards. If every rollout gets the same reward, the group mean is the reward and every advantage is zero, so the prompt teaches nothing. A-RSI counts, across a window, how many distinct prompts retain this within-group reward variation, and it also tracks logged answer accuracy. When the count of informative prompts declines and the accuracy signal indicates the pool is being solved, the detector fires and the next prepared replacement pool is activated. The paper is careful to call this an operational indicator of declining learning signal, not a proof that the pool has been mastered, and that distinction matters for how much you should trust the timing.

The replacement, when it happens, is deliberately dumb, and the dumbness is a finding. The new tasks are drawn without replacement under fixed family quotas across geometry, charts, tables, and counting, in a fixed pseudo-random order that does not depend on the model's answers or on any difficulty estimate. The paper then tests the obvious smarter alternatives. Frontier selection uses held-out synthetic accuracy to find the easiest difficulty level the model does not yet solve reliably and draws from there. There is also a periodic schedule and a continuous every-window refresh with adaptive difficulty. The result, which the paper states plainly, is that none of the difficulty-aware or competence-aware selection strategies beat random sampling under the fixed family shares. This is limited to their generators, their model, and the tested rules, but it is a genuinely useful negative result for anyone building self-improvement pipelines, because it says the selection rule is not where the value is in this regime.

The numbers. On the primary eleven-benchmark suite, the Qwen model, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, improves from 57.77 at the post-initialization checkpoint to 61.05 after eight continuation windows using 800 fresh tasks. A MiMo model improves from 59.08 to 60.71 from its public base. The efficiency comparison is the more interesting one. Refreshing every single window, the continuous baseline, uses 3,200 fresh tasks to reach 60.64, while A-RSI reaches 61.05 with 800. At the matched sixth continuation window, A-RSI scores 60.40 against 58.35 for a control arm that kept training on the old pool. So the timing rule does more work than the data budget it saves, and the paper frames the contribution around exactly this. The when decision, driven by cheap log signals, is worth more than spending four times the fresh-task budget on a fixed schedule.

There is a section of the paper I want to flag because it is unusual in this literature. The authors audit their own gains, and they distinguish two ways a score can go up. One is completion. More answers finish within the output budget, so fewer responses are truncated, and the average rises even though no actually-completed answer got better. The other is correction. Already-completed answers change from wrong to right. They report these separately, and they check whether the gain persists under a longer generation budget and format-corrected scoring, including when they evaluate the released weights of MM-Zero, a prior zero-data multimodal method, under the same protocol. That audit is the paper's strongest move. A lot of reported gains in self-improvement work are completion artifacts, and doing the decomposition, and doing it against a third party's released weights, is the kind of rigor the field needs more of.

The limits are also stated, and I think they are the right ones to weight. The task families are narrow, geometry, charts, tables, counting, so the transferability of the refresh rule to open-ended multimodal work is untested. The random-selection result is scoped to these generators and this model. The detector is a heuristic on log statistics, and the paper does not prove it detects the true saturation point, only that it tracks a declining learning signal well enough to save budget. And the whole loop still depends on the initial program library being good. If the base model writes bad templates, no refresh policy fixes the supervision.

The practical takeaway for someone implementing this. If you are running a zero-data or self-generated-data reinforcement learning loop, the cheapest possible improvement is probably not a better curriculum. It is a trigger that watches how many of your prompts still have within-group reward variation, and a prepared set of fresh tasks that you swap in when that number decays. The answer side should come from programs that compute the reference, not from the model voting. And before you claim a gain, split it into completion and correction, because the completion part is the part you can accidentally manufacture.

My verdict is that this is one of the more honest papers in the self-improvement space, and its central result is a control insight rather than a model. The adaptive refresh rule saves a large fraction of the fresh-task budget at matched or better performance, the negative result on difficulty-aware selection is worth more than the positive one, and the output-level audit sets a bar the rest of the field should be held to.
