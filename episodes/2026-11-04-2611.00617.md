Words Caught in the Heart: Simulating Person-Specific Expression for Self-Opaque Clients in Psychotherapy, by Mengxi Xiao, Jingjing Chen, and Ziyan Kuang, from Wuhan University, Huazhong Agricultural University, the University of Manchester, and Yale University, with corresponding authors at Wuhan and Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence, is a paper about building a realistic simulator of a psychotherapy client. The problem is specific. A real client often feels distress before they can describe it coherently, and the way they express it changes with their emotional state, how safe they feel, and how much they trust the therapist. Existing large-model client simulators tend to be fed a clinically organized case description, and that has a side effect the authors call out directly: it encourages the simulated client to explain their difficulties more clearly than their current level of understanding warrants. The simulator becomes too articulate, too self-aware, which defeats the point of simulating a self-opaque client, one who does not yet have the words for what they are feeling.

Their proposed system is called Pandora, and the core idea is to separate the psychological content of a response from its linguistic realization. Pandora first maintains a psychological state, which is a set of twelve numerical variables, including distress, emotional arousal, perceived safety, disclosure readiness, self-awareness, and insight, among others, and it updates these states in response to the therapist's interventions using rule-based updates rather than calling a separate model to re-infer the state. It then constructs subjective appraisals and attributions, which is where the client interprets what happened and who is responsible. It plans the content and strategy of the response, deciding what to disclose and what to hold back, and it organizes the sequence of disclosures so that supporting disclosures precede deeper ones. And then a separate component, which they call the expression planner, takes those decisions and combines them with language patterns distilled from the client's own prior utterances, packaged into a role-specific language package, to guide how the response is actually phrased. That final component is the contribution. The paper's argument is that expression is a distinct layer that most simulators collapse into the content generation, and that collapsing it is exactly what makes the simulated client sound like a competent explainer rather than a struggling person.

The evaluation is modest in scale but carefully matched. They evaluate Pandora against five baselines on 115 cases drawn from four counseling and motivational interviewing datasets, and the five baselines include the prior interactive-agents counseling simulator and a consistent motivational interviewing system. The headline metric is the rate at which human evaluators judge a generated client turn to sound AI-like, and they run a four-evaluator study on 45 matched disclosure items, meaning the same content is expressed by different systems so the comparison isolates expression rather than substance. Pandora receives AI-like judgments in 7.2 percent of the ratings, which is 13 AI-like votes out of 180. The baselines range from 43.3 to 67.2 percent, a 36.1 point reduction relative to the best baseline. That is a large gap, and it is the central result. The content-side results go the same direction: on disclosure alignment, measured at three levels of granularity, Pandora scores 0.843, 0.854, and 0.866, exceeding every baseline and the ablation at each level, and it has the lowest error in disclosure bundle size. They also run the critical ablation, where they remove the expression planner while keeping the rest of the system, and the AI-like rate jumps to 35.6 percent, a paired difference of 28.3 points with a case-bootstrap confidence interval from 20.6 to 36.1 points, and the expression similarity metric drops. So the expression planner is doing most of the work, not the content planning.

Two secondary findings are worth keeping. First, the effect holds across evaluator groups, and the split is informative. Among the four evaluators, the counselors judge the generated systems as AI-like at 10.0 percent for Pandora, while the synthetic-data researchers judge them at 4.4 percent. The researchers are the better discriminators here, which makes sense, because they have more exposure to model output. The fact that both groups land far below the baselines is the robustness signal, and the ablation shows the same split, where the expression-planning-free variant reads as AI-like at 40.0 percent to the counselors and 31.1 percent to the researchers, so the gap between the full system and the ablated one is large in both populations. Second, they report the evaluator agreement is middling, with a 62.9 percent overall agreement and a 53.7 percent positive agreement on the AI-like judgments specifically, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 49.4 to 58.1. That is a caution. The AI-like judgment is subjective, the evaluators only weakly agree with each other, and a 7.2 percent rate measured against a noisy judge is a weaker claim than it first reads. They are honest about this, which is more than most papers in this space are.

There is also a methodological detail that matters for anyone reproducing this. They deliberately do not reproduce a trained authorship verifier or an aggregate consistency score from the prior Eval4Sim work. Instead they rely on direct human judgment of individual turns, matched on content. That is a defensible choice for a study about expression, because a whole-document consistency metric would conflate the expression question with a content question, but it means the result is a perception result, not a capability result. The system is better at sounding human, in the narrow sense of not triggering an AI-likeness judgment, and that is a real but bounded claim. The authors themselves flag the boundary clearly: the psychological state variables are modeling constructs, not clinical findings about real individuals, and the measures they report, expression fidelity, disclosure alignment, and perceived AI-like expression, do not by themselves establish clinical validation. Any deployment would need to make it explicit that the interactions are simulated, and that the simulator is a research instrument rather than a therapeutic one.

The skeptical read. The scale is small, 115 cases, 45 matched items, four evaluators, and the confidence intervals are wide. The baselines are a specific set of five, and if the strongest baseline had a better expression model the gap would shrink. The datasets are counseling and motivational interviewing, which are structured conversational settings, so the result may not transfer to a more open or more adversarial clinical dialogue. And the whole enterprise has an open question about validity that the paper does not fully settle, which is whether a simulated client that sounds less AI-like is actually more useful for training or researching a therapist, or merely more pleasing to a human judge. Those are different properties, and the evaluation only measures the second one. That said, the ablation is clean, the matched-design is the right choice for the question asked, and the explicit separation of content planning from expression planning is a genuinely useful architectural idea that generalizes beyond psychotherapy to any task where you want a language agent to sound like a specific person rather than like a model.

Bottom line: this is a small but methodologically honest study that isolates one real variable, the linguistic expression layer, and shows it dominates the AI-likeness of a simulated client. The 7.2 percent against a 43 to 67 percent baseline range is a strong effect, but it is a perception result on a narrow task with noisy human judgment, so treat it as evidence that expression planning matters, not as a solved benchmark. The code and case material are released, and the twelve-variable psychological state plus the distilled language package are the pieces worth reusing. If you build user simulators for any domain, not just therapy, the content-versus-expression split is the part to steal.
