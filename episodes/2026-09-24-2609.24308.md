HappyWorld-Bench. The first three authors, in alphabetical order, are Zhiqi Bai, Junai Cai, and Yixin Chen, from Alibaba's Token Hub, the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence, and Tsinghua University, with the full roster of thirty-seven spanning Peking University and Nanjing University as well.

The premise is simple and the current state of the art is uncomfortable: a world model is only as useful as its predictions are under interaction. Photorealistic frames are the easy part. The hard requirement is that the world stays geometrically consistent when the camera moves, physically plausible when you touch it, persistent over long rollouts and revisits, and responsive when you intervene, change a rule, or apply a force. The complaint this paper is aimed at is fragmentation. Video world model benchmarks, spatial scene benchmarks, and embodied interaction benchmarks have each developed their own protocols, and none of them share a capability vocabulary, so you cannot tell whether a model that tops one leaderboard is actually a better world model or just a better video generator. HappyWorld-Bench's answer is a shared hierarchy, six levels they call W1 through W6, from the Perceptual World, coherent generation from a condition, through Interactive and Persistent World, where actions produce state transitions and state survives occlusion and revisits, up to Programmable World, where natural-language interventions must change only what they target, and Scalable and Universal World, shared multi-agent environments and full real-world replication. The levels are a ladder of what a world model owes the agent, and the benchmark instantiates that ladder across three separate tracks, each matched to a model family.

The scale is the point. One thousand one hundred thirty-eight video prompts, three hundred spatial scenes, and two hundred fifty-four embodied test cases, sixteen hundred ninety-two instances total, spanning nine application domains from robotics and driving to game worlds and fantasy, rollouts up to sixty seconds, and first frames ranging from below one megapixel to above eight. For scoring, they built and operated HappyWorld-Arena, a human A/B comparison system that produces model-level Elo ratings within each track, and they pair the human ratings with new automated metrics aimed at behavioral correctness, because Elo tells you what people prefer and the automated metrics tell you whether the world did the thing it was supposed to do. They evaluate fourteen video world models, nine spatial systems, and eight embodied candidates, and the results are, in the authors' own framing, sobering.

In the video track, the two strongest systems land at Arena Elo ratings of one thousand two hundred sixty-three and one thousand two hundred six, and the capability tables show a clear split between perceptual quality and world consistency. A model can score well on visual quality, human preference, and instruction following, and still fall apart on temporal and state consistency. The Dyn, or dynamics, column in their perceptual table is the tell: a handful of models post high fifties to high nineties there while most of the field sits in the single or double digits, which is to say that generating plausible motion is still a minority skill. The interactive track separates action execution from state transition correctness: high scores on action tracking mean the model followed the command, not that the resulting world state is coherent, and the causal-consistency columns show wide spread among models that track actions equally well. The spatial track is where the headline numbers live: placement accuracy tops out at seventy point one four percent and edit execution at seventy-three point three three percent across all nine systems, which is to say that even the best spatial world models get roughly one in four placements wrong and one in four edits wrong. The embodied track found models that struggle to preserve state across multi-step actions and to respond precisely when action conditions or physical rules change.

The skeptical read. The capability hierarchy is the real contribution, and it is a good one: a shared W1 through W6 vocabulary that video, spatial, and embodied communities can all map onto is exactly the missing piece, and the split between human Elo and automated behavioral metrics is the right epistemology, because preference and correctness are not the same thing and both matter. What I would push back on is the composition of the leaderboard. The video track is a who's who of commercial systems, several of them from the same ecosystem that runs the benchmark, and Arena Elo at this sample size is a popularity contest with a confidence interval the paper should report and does not. The spatial and embodied tracks have eight to nine candidates each, which is thin, and the best-in-class numbers, seventy point one four and seventy-three point three three percent, are from a field that is months, not years, old, so the benchmark's main value is as a ratchet, a fixed yardstick you can re-run as models improve, rather than a final ranking. If you work on world models of any kind, this is worth reading for the W-hierarchy and the automated metric designs even if you discount the leaderboards. Verdict: the most systematic attempt yet to put all three world-model families on one capability ladder, with a sobering snapshot of where every current system falls short, but the video-track leaderboard is crowded with same-ecosystem entrants and the smaller tracks need more candidates before the rankings mean anything.

The metric design is where a lot of the value is, and it is worth going a level deeper than the leaderboard. Each track pairs the human Arena Elo, which summarizes overall preference, with a set of automated metrics that decompose the W-hierarchy into measurable capabilities, so you can see not just that a model won a human comparison but which capability it won on. In the video track, the perception metrics, visual quality, human preference score, and instruction following, sit alongside consistency metrics that separate geometric and state coherence from appearance, which is the whole point of the W1 result, that a model can look great and still not be a coherent world. The interactive track adds causality and action-execution columns, and the paper is careful to distinguish tracking the action from getting the state transition right, because a model can execute the requested motion and still produce an incoherent result, and those are scored separately. The spatial track's placement accuracy and edit execution numbers, the seventy point one four and seventy-three point three three percent ceilings, are single-task numbers from controlled operations, put an object here, edit that region, which makes them interpretable in a way that a holistic preference score is not, and it is precisely that interpretability that the video track's Elo rating cannot provide.

The three-track structure also has a methodological consequence the paper leans on: because all three tracks share the same W1 through W6 taxonomy, a model family that only does video, only does spatial, or only does embodied can be compared on the subset of the hierarchy it actually covers, and the shared vocabulary means progress in one track is stated in the units the other tracks use. That is the antidote to the fragmentation problem, and it is the part I would try to replicate in my own evaluation, even if I never ran the full benchmark, because defining what your model owes the agent in terms of a shared capability ladder is more valuable than the specific instances. The data statistics, sixteen hundred ninety-two instances across nine domains, rollouts to sixty seconds, and first-frame resolutions from under one to over eight megapixels, are broad enough that a model tuned to one domain or one resolution will not sweep the board by accident, which is a real defense against benchmark overfitting. The application-domain spread, robotics, urban and indoor, nature, transport, game worlds, daily life, industry, fantasy, and materials, also matters for the spatial and embodied tracks, where most prior benchmarks live in a single environment family.

The sobering result, across all three tracks, is consistent in shape, which is the most interesting finding. It is not that one family of world model fails; it is that every family fails in the same direction, generative quality is ahead of state reliability. Video models look good and then drift under long rollouts and revisits. Spatial models generate complete scenes but get a meaningful fraction of placements and edits wrong. Embodied models produce plausible action-conditioned video but do not preserve state across multi-step actions or respond precisely when the rules change. The implication is that the field is optimizing the easy half of world modeling, making it look right, while the hard half, making it stay right, is where every current system leaves most of the distance. A benchmark that makes that gap visible is doing real scientific work, and that is the case for HappyWorld-Bench regardless of where any particular model lands on its leaderboards.
