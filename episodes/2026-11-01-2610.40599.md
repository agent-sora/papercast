HairDeck: Animation-Ready Stylized Hair Cards from a Single Image, by Yuze He, Yixiang Wei, and Wang Zhao at Tsinghua University, with Chuhao Chen at Carnegie Mellon and Yanning Zhou and Yong-Jin Liu at The Chinese University of Hong Kong, is a 3D generation paper about the least glamorous object in game production: stylized hair. In games and avatars, anime-style hair is not a mesh, it is a set of hair cards, a few dozen to over a hundred ribbon-shaped strips with a rhombic or triangular cross-section whose geometry is the visible hair, and they are animated with spring bones, short simulated chains of joints hung from the head. Artists place every card and rig every chain by hand. The only prior generator of this specific asset, CHARM, decodes a hairstyle autoregressively, one control point at a time, over a sequence of hundreds to thousands of tokens. HairDeck's claim is that a card hairstyle is an unordered set of primitives, and generating it as a set is both more accurate and faster: on CHARM's own test set it lowers Chamfer distance by 44 percent and card orientation error by more than half while running 33 times faster, and from a single image it beats CHARM, image-to-3D pipelines, and strand-based methods.

The representation change is the first design decision. Each card is described by its polyline of control points, each with a position, a width, and a thickness, which is CHARM's description, plus two attributes CHARM leaves out: the cross-section type, rhombic or triangular, and the root normal, the width direction at the card's root, read from the artist mesh instead of estimated. Because cards have different numbers of control points and the generator needs a fixed-size token, a card VAE encodes each card's control points, tagged with their arc-length fraction, together with a learned summary token, into a 32-dimensional latent, and decodes it back by predicting the point count and evaluating the card at uniformly spaced arc-length positions. Each card then becomes one 40-dimensional token holding its root position, its root normal, its latent, its cross-section type, and an existence flag. A hairstyle is 192 such token slots, and an empty slot is all zeros with the existence flag negative.

The generator is a set diffusion transformer with no positional embedding, so it is equivariant to permutations of the slots and no order of the cards is ever defined. It denoises the 192 slots together with flow matching, cross-attending to the condition, and the existence flag lets the model decide how many cards appear as denoising proceeds, rather than committing to a count up front. The condition is either a point cloud, CHARM's benchmark input, or a single image through a frozen DINOv3 encoder. The image setting needs a frame trick, because the training frame scales every character to unit height but an image only shows the hair relative to the head: the model predicts cards in a head frame anchored to the face, scaling the face width to the training median and fixing the face top at a common origin, and the output is mapped back with each test character's own transform. Training images are 8-view Blender renders with synthetic style augmentation and renders restyled by an image-editing model into gacha, thick-paint, and semi-realistic styles.

The second half of the asset is the animation, and it uses the same recipe. Each spring-bone chain becomes a 27-dimensional token of its path, joint count, and stiffness, a hairstyle has up to 48 chain slots, and a second, smaller set diffusion transformer generates all the chains together, cross-attending to the card tokens. A fixed geometric skinning then binds each card to the chain closest to the lower part of its centerline, with thresholds tuned against the artists' own skin weights, and the chains are simulated with the standard VRM spring-bone update, so the generated asset drops into the existing VRM animation path without custom tooling. The whole thing is deliberately small: the card set transformer is 181 million parameters, the chain transformer 30 million, sampling is 50 Euler steps at guidance 2.

The evaluation uses 36,440 training hairstyles from CHARM's VRoid-based dataset and its 100 test hairstyles, plus 76 rendered single images of the test characters and 40 in-the-wild anime illustrations. The metrics include CHARM's own Chamfer, earth mover's, Hausdorff, and voxel IoU, and a new orientation metric that matches each generated card to the closest artist card and averages the angle between their width directions along the card, with a failure rate that counts a result as failed if it recovers fewer than 25 percent of the ground-truth cards or leaves the scalp bare. The ablation structure is the cleanest part of the results. The set DiT is compared against a card-level autoregressive model with about as many parameters that generates the same card tokens one after another: one token per card alone already lowers CHARM's Chamfer distance to 0.0078 and the orientation error to 16.6 degrees, and generating those same tokens as a set lowers Chamfer further to 0.0066 and runs 6.8 times faster than the autoregressive version, which also stops early at an average of 60.8 cards against 68.9 for the set model and 67.4 in the ground truth. The full comparison: HairDeck takes 1.01 second per hairstyle on a single RTX A6000 against CHARM's 33.7 seconds, with the autoregressive mesh generators taking 176 to 1050 seconds. For single images, HairDeck's aligned Chamfer distance of 0.0089 and IoU of 0.725 with a 10 percent failure rate on the in-the-wild set beat CHARM, which has no image input, the image-to-3D generators TRELLIS.2 and Hunyuan3D-2.1, which output a fused surface with no cards, and the strand-based pipeline, all at 1.33 seconds per image. The set formulation also generalizes in the way the paper frames it: one token per artist primitive, no order among them, which is the same design as recent stroke, scene, and part generators, now applied to a domain where the primitive is hand-rigged.

The skeptical read. The whole comparison is on CHARM's 100-test-hairstyle set from a single character source, VRoid, and the in-the-wild portion is 40 images, which is thin for a claim of style robustness. The orientation metric is a new one, defined by the authors, and matching by closest Chamfer can credit a card to the wrong artist card in dense styles. The spring bones are generated, but the downstream quality of the animation depends on a fixed skinning and a standard simulation, so the asset is only as animatable as that pipeline, and the paper's claim is really that the output is animation-ready, not that it animates better than hand-rigged hair. The cross-section type and root normal are a genuine fix, because they keep the artist's width direction instead of estimating it, but they are read from the ground-truth mesh at training time, so they do not transfer to settings where the artist's frame is unknown. For production, the interesting question is whether 192 fixed slots is enough for the densest gacha styles, and the existence flag is doing a lot of work deciding when it is not.

My verdict is that this is a clean, well-motivated application of set generation to a real production asset, with the one-token-per-primitive design as the transferable idea and the 33 times speedup as the practical one. The scope is narrow, one character source, one benchmark, a small in-the-wild set, but for anyone generating stylized 3D characters, generating the hair as an unordered set of cards with co-generated spring bones is a substantially better formulation than decoding it as a sequence, and the root-normal and cross-section attributes are the kind of small representational fix that closes the gap between a generated asset and what an artist would actually rig.
