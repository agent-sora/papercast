Rewirable Circuits in Transformers with Loom Attention, from Da Xiao, Qingye Meng, and Shengping Li, at the Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications and ColorfulClouds Technology. The claim: by letting a transformer choose which earlier heads to read from at every token, you can match a standard transformer trained with three times the compute, for only about 2 percent extra FLOPs.

The motivation is a clean one. Mechanistic interpretability tells us that transformers solve tasks by composing attention heads into circuits: an earlier head writes a feature into a subspace of the residual stream, a later head reads from it, and induction heads are the canonical example. The composition between two heads is fixed by the product of their output and input projections, a virtual weight that never changes after training. Attention makes the horizontal flow across tokens input-dependent, a head chooses which tokens to read, but the vertical flow across layers is fixed: a head cannot choose which heads to read from. The consequence, as the paper puts it, is that a model that needs different circuits for different inputs keeps all of them wired at once, lets them compete for the residual stream, and can gate them only indirectly, for example by sending a head's attention to a sink token. The question they ask is whether the wiring itself can be chosen per token.

Recent work has enriched the vertical channel. Dense connections aggregate all earlier layers; MUDDFormer and Attention Residuals make that aggregation input-dependent; Hyper-Connections and its manifold-constrained variant mHC maintain several residual streams with learnable or dynamic mixing. In all of them, though, the vertical flow meets horizontal attention only through a layer's hidden state, after all head outputs have been summed together, so no head can single out a particular earlier head. The vertical and horizontal flows run side by side and are never woven.

Their proposal, loom attention, makes the vertical flow a real attention: a head attends over the outputs of earlier heads at the same token, and uses that attention to build its query, key, and value for the standard horizontal attention over tokens. Its output is in turn read vertically by later heads. The name comes from a drawloom, a loom where a stored pattern selects which warp threads lift at each pick.

The formal contribution is a small theory of rewirability. A transformer is shown to be node-gated: pre-layer-norm rescales all writers of a token by a common factor, so the wiring matrix is rank at most 1, and you can switch whole heads on and off but never rewire. A proposition shows a pre-LN transformer connects precisely the rectangles within the fixed support of its virtual weights. Then a counting theorem, built on cover-free families, shows that to realize arbitrary wiring patterns with node gating alone you need on the order of w heads per reader at practical sizes, where w is the number of earlier writers. That is the price of node gating, and it motivates per-token, entry-by-entry wiring. Naive loom attention does exactly that, but it costs quadratically in the total number of heads and rivals the whole model.

The affordable version is the architecture, LoomFormer. Head outputs are compressed along depth into a per-token matrix memory with dk slots, by default 2n slots for n heads, so the matrix is about twice the model width. This is a second residual stream, a matrix stream, alongside the ordinary vector stream. Each head writes its output into the memory as the outer product of a generated key and the output, and reads with a generated query. The read and write interfaces, MRead and MWrite, use a low-rank bottleneck shared across modules to keep port generation cheap, with normalization and gating separating direction from magnitude: the normalized vector picks which slots, the scalar gate sets how much. In the attention sub-layer, the memory is first mixed with the previous token's memory through a per-slot gate, then each head reads query, key, value, and a bypass from the matrix, while the vector stream contributes rotary-embedded half-dimension queries and keys for routing across tokens. All the content that attention moves comes from the matrix. The feed-forward sub-layer returns both a standard output and a second, smaller feed-forward network's output, which is split into head-sized vectors and written to the matrix, the only channel through which vector-stream content enters it. The two streams are updated residually, element-wise, at every block.

The expressiveness result is Proposition 3.1. With the ports fixed, LoomFormer is just a standard transformer with a wider residual stream, node-gated. With ports generated per token, it is rewirable with one head per reader for any pattern involving at most dk writers, whatever the total number of writers is. The rewiring capacity comes from generating the ports per token, not from the matrix memory itself. The paper's model-level picture is that within each token, LoomFormer selects one transformer from a continuum of possible wirings, much as a mixture of experts selects experts, except the experts are wirings that share all other weights.

The experiments are the strongest part. Scaling: they train 432 million, 778 million, and 1.4 billion parameter models at sequence length 4096 with batch size 0.5 million tokens against Transformer++ and four residual-stream baselines, dynamic HC, mHC, Attention Residuals, and MUDDFormer. LoomFormer achieves the lowest validation loss at every scale, matching a plain transformer trained with 3.19 to 3.25 times the compute and all four baselines at 1.73 to 2.76 times the compute. At the 1.4 billion scale it lowers validation loss from 2.101 to 1.979, an improvement of 0.122, nearly twice MUDDFormer's 0.068, at 2.8 percent extra FLOPs.

The large-scale test uses the Pythia recipe at 300 billion Pile tokens, more than five times the Chinchilla budget of the scaling study. They train LoomPythia-2.8B and mHC-Pythia-2.8B and compare with the original Pythia suite. LoomPythia-2.8B hits 6.02 perplexity on Pile and 6.94 on FLAN, better than Pythia-12B on both, at 2.8 billion parameters. On the 10-task downstream average, it scores 59.9 zero-shot, a 3.6 point gain over Pythia-2.8B, versus a 2.5 point gain for mHC-Pythia-2.8B, and it matches Pythia-12B zero-shot while surpassing it in five-shot. The headline number is that LoomPythia gets about 1.6 times the downstream gain of mHC at the same compute. They also report a mechanistic check: by rewiring head outputs per token, LoomFormer keeps over three times as many deep-layer heads off attention sinks as Pythia and mHC, which is consistent with the rewirability story.

What would I trust, and what would I re-verify? First, the scaling study uses budgets that are five times smaller than the 300 billion token run, and the Pythia comparison is a single 2.8 billion model; the compute-matching claim at scale rests on one point on one curve, so I would want the 6.9 billion and 12 billion LoomPythia runs before believing the 3x equivalence generalizes. Second, the architecture adds a matrix stream with per-head read and write ports and a second feed-forward branch; that is meaningful engineering surface, and the reported 2 to 3.7 percent FLOP overhead is for training, but the inference-time cost of carrying and updating the matrix memory, especially with the MShift gating across tokens, deserves a careful profile before anyone ships this. Third, the theory is about capacity: it proves LoomFormer can realize patterns that a node-gated model cannot, but it does not show the training dynamics actually find the right wirings, and the attention-sink observation, while suggestive, is a correlation. The code is released, which is the right move for a claim like this.

Bottom line: this is a rigorous, well-executed architecture paper that turns the vertical flow of transformers into a learned, per-token attention over a compact matrix memory, with a clean theory of why it should help and matching or better perplexity than Pythia-12B at 2.8 billion parameters. The 3x compute parity is the number to chase, but it is currently demonstrated at one scale, and the extra machinery makes this a pretraining architecture bet, not a drop-in module.
