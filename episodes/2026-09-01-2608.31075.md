Scaling Large Reasoning Models beyond Human Supervision, by Zhiqin Yang, Jingwen Fu, and Yuhan Liu, from HKUST, the Zhongguancun Academy, and Xi'an Jiaotong University, with a large corresponding-author team spanning a dozen institutions. The question it organizes around: as large reasoning models keep improving, human supervision is gradually receding from the learning loop, so how does a model keep getting better once the person who wrote the answer, or the rubric, or the environment, is no longer in the room?

This is a survey and a position paper, not a new method, and I want to be clear about that from the start. Its value is as a map. It builds a taxonomy that the field has been using inconsistently, and it maintains a continuously updated GitHub repository that tracks the latest systems as they appear. The central organizing device is a five-level ladder, L0 through L4, that classifies where the supervision in a training loop is coming from, and which parts of the loop are still under human control.

The ladder is worth stating in full because it is the paper's main contribution. L0 is per-instance human supervision: every training example is paired with a human-provided answer, preference, or judgment that is its target. That is classic supervised learning and the human-in-the-loop reward modeling of early RLHF. L1 is human-grounded evaluation: the human criteria are encoded once into a reusable reward model, rubric, or language-model judge, so the same standard can be applied across many trajectories without a person looking at each one. L2 is reward beyond human evaluation: the reward now comes from model confidence, agreement across samples, available references, or observable outcomes in an environment, while the tasks and environments are still largely provided from outside. L3 is experience beyond human design: the training distribution itself becomes adaptive, with tasks, curricula, and environments generated, discovered, selected, or reorganized during learning, while humans set the broad goals, constraints, and initial conditions. L4 is autonomous co-evolution: feedback, experience, and policy improvement all adapt together in a persistent loop, and human involvement is concentrated at the level of intent, safety constraints, and independent oversight. The paper is explicit that this classifies supervision sources, not whole pipelines, so standard RLHF spans both L0 and L1, with human preferences providing the per-instance signal and the resulting reward model serving as the reusable evaluator.

The two axes behind the ladder are the reward axis, from per-instance human judgments to reusable verifiers and rewards that operate without any human feedback, and the experience axis, from human-curated tasks to self-generated curricula, constructed environments, and autonomous co-evolution. The paper places a large set of named methods on this ladder, from DeepSeek-R1 and the process reward model family, Math-Shepherd, PRIME, and xVerify, near the bottom, through RLAIF, Rubicon, and TTRL in the middle, up to self-challenging and co-evolution systems like R-Zero, AZR, Self-Challenging, and Agent-World at the top. That placement map is the practical artifact: it lets you look at any recent system and say which level of the ladder it actually operates at, and that is a distinction a lot of the recent literature blurs. The reason the blur matters is that a paper will often describe a self-generated curriculum, which sounds like L3, but the reward is still a fixed human-written rubric, which pins the loop at L1 or L2. Naming the actual source of both the reward and the experience, rather than gesturing at autonomy, is the discipline the ladder imposes, and it is a useful habit even for systems you do not plan to adopt.

The second major contribution is the risk taxonomy, which is the part I would take most seriously. As you climb the ladder, four failure modes get worse, and the paper names them precisely. Reward hacking, where the policy finds a way to score without doing the intended work. Feedback drift, where a learned or self-generated evaluator slowly diverges from the objective it was meant to approximate, especially as the policy outpaces the evaluator. Curriculum collapse, where a self-generated training distribution stops being diverse and the model trains on a shrinking, self-reinforcing slice of problems. And environment errors, where a constructed environment has bugs that the model learns to exploit or that silently corrupt the signal. The paper's argument is that these are not edge cases that get rarer as you scale; they are the defining engineering problems of the higher levels, and the systems that get to L3 and L4 are the ones that have had to build mitigations for all four.

The third contribution is an evaluation framework built around three complementary objects, which I think is the most under-appreciated part of the paper. Policy capability, which is the usual question, does the model get the task right. Feedback fidelity, which is a different question, does the reward or evaluator actually track the intended objective, and this is the one that catches reward hacking and feedback drift. And experience quality, which asks whether the training distribution the model is generating is still diverse and informative, and this is the one that catches curriculum collapse. The paper's point is that a system can look good on policy capability while quietly failing on the other two, and that is exactly the failure signature of an autonomous loop that has started to game itself. Evaluating all three, rather than just the final accuracy, is the methodological takeaway. The connection to the CogEvol paper on this same day is worth drawing, even though they are independent: CogEvol found a concrete instance of the feedback-fidelity failure mode, a screenshot judge that masked a thirty-six-point collapse in game playability, and the fix was to measure interactivity directly instead of judging it visually. That is exactly the kind of reward-fidelity problem the survey names in the abstract, and the CogEvol result is a worked example of what the survey is warning about.

Now the skeptical read, and for a survey the questions are different from for a methods paper. First, the ladder is clean, but the boundaries between levels are acknowledged by the authors to be fuzzy, and a lot of recent systems sit on two levels at once, so the taxonomy is a useful map rather than a crisp classifier, and I would not treat a system's L-level as a precise claim. Second, because this is a survey, the empirical content is limited to the placement of existing methods and the risk taxonomy, so the strongest test of it would be to take the three-object evaluation and actually run it on a real L3 system, which the paper sets up but does not itself execute. Third, the framing toward superintelligence is the paper's own, and I would be cautious about reading the L4 autonomous co-evolution level as anything close to a realized path; it is a description of where the research is heading and the failure modes that accompany it, not a claim that anyone is close. If you use this: the GitHub repository is the living part and the part worth bookmarking, because the survey's value decays as the field moves and the repo is how the authors keep it current. The ladder is a good vocabulary for writing your own position paper or for reviewing a new system's claims about how autonomous it really is. And the three-object evaluation, policy capability plus feedback fidelity plus experience quality, is a concrete checklist I would actually adopt when auditing any self-improving or self-generated-data training loop, because it forces you to look at the reward and the data, not just the final score.

Bottom line: this is a well-organized map of a fast-moving area, with a five-level ladder for where supervision is coming from, a named risk taxonomy for what breaks as autonomy increases, and a three-object evaluation framework that I find more useful than the ladder itself. Its limitation is that it is a survey, so it tells you the shape of the field and the failure modes to watch, but it does not itself advance the state of the art, and the superintelligence framing is aspirational rather than a demonstrated capability.
