The paper is Heartbeat Thinking, from Zhenyu Zhang, Kewei Fu, and Yongqi Tong at Ant International, with Chenzhi Zheng, Wei Wu, Jiang-Ming Yang, and the corresponding author Jianshe Li also at Ant International. The setting is a tool-using voice agent, the kind that listens to a user, calls tools, and speaks back in real time, and the specific problem is latency after the user stops talking. The observation is that users are often left waiting after their speech ends, even though actionable information arrived earlier in the request. The obvious fix, and the one prior speculative approaches use, is to start working while the user is still talking, but the paper points out the failure mode of that design: when you generate in chunks bounded by speech updates, or you interrupt and regenerate whenever the transcript updates, you can cut off a tool call before its arguments are complete. That couples the useful work you can do in advance to the cadence of the speech updates, and it caps how much preparation you can finish. Heartbeat Thinking is their alternative design for doing speculative work while listening, and the headline is that it cuts final response latency by more than a third.

The core idea is to decouple the input stream from the work. Instead of interrupting and restarting the reasoning every time the partial transcript updates, Heartbeat Thinking handles updated input by launching new Thinker invocations while letting the ones already running, and the tool calls they have already dispatched, finish. The Thinker is invoked on a fixed heartbeat interval, they use 500 milliseconds in their latency setup, and each invocation reads the latest partial transcript plus a shared record of the reasoning and tool results that have already completed. That shared record is the load-bearing piece: a later invocation can see that an earlier one already resolved some of the arguments, so it can fill in the missing pieces and initiate the dependent tool calls that were waiting on them. Preparation therefore advances across speech updates instead of resetting with each one, and because the requested tools execute asynchronously and their results land in the shared record, the agent can make progress through several reasoning and tool-use steps even while the user is still mid-sentence. The responder, the component that produces the final spoken answer, is triggered by the voice activity detection module when it detects the end of speech, and by then a lot of the tool work is already done.

The responder side is worth describing because it is where the speculative work either gets used or thrown away. When the voice activity detector marks the end of speech, the responder receives the final transcript, the history, the tool schemas, and a snapshot of the preparation that had completed by the time the context was assembled. The speculative outputs produced after that point are discarded. The responder then runs a ReAct-style loop: it checks the prepared work against the complete request, rejects any interpretation that the later speech contradicted, reuses the completed results that are still applicable, and executes any required calls that lack a result within its own process, so it does not block on preparation that is still running. That last point is the design that makes the latency win real, because the responder never waits for a speculative tool call that is still in flight; it either uses it or moves on.

The evaluation covers three benchmarks: function calling on SpeechFCEval, intent following under disfluency on FDBench v3, and knowledge-grounded answering on AudioCRAG. The baselines are the three production voice frameworks, Speech-to-Speech, Pipecat, and LiveKit, plus a SHANKS-style windowed speculative baseline, which is the chunk-bounded approach the paper argues against. The key metric, STC, is speculative tool coverage, the fraction of the required tool calls that were already prepared during the listening window and therefore reusable at answer time. On SpeechFCEval, with Qwen3.6 35B as the Thinker and DeepSeek-V4-Flash as the responder, the windowed baseline reaches a tool coverage of 33.13 while Heartbeat reaches 43.69, a ten point gain in coverage, and with the Qwen3.8 Flash Next responder the gap widens, with the windowed baseline at 44.05 and Heartbeat at 55.27. On FDBench v3 the latency result is the headline: Heartbeat increases tool coverage by 16 percentage points and reduces the mean final-response latency from the windowed baseline's 6.90 seconds to 4.39 seconds, a 36.4 percent reduction, which is also 45.7 percent below Pipecat, the fastest of the cascaded baselines. The paper is careful to note that the effect on final task quality is benchmark-dependent, so the latency and coverage gains do not come with a guaranteed accuracy gain on every benchmark.


One more result from the FDBench v3 latency breakdown is worth reading because it is stronger than the headline. The mean correct tool call latency, measured at the initiation of the first tool call that matches the final request, relative to the end of the user's speech, is negative 4.37 seconds for Heartbeat. That means the matching tool call, on average, has already been dispatched four and a half seconds before the user finished talking. The windowed baseline starts its first correct call 2.72 seconds after speech ends, and the cascaded baselines sit between 2.45 and 3.52 seconds after. So the speculative window is doing real work, not just marginal preparation. The corresponding final-response latency numbers are 4.39 seconds for Heartbeat, 6.90 for the windowed baseline, and between 8.08 and 8.52 for the three production cascaded frameworks. The speculative coverage gains across the three benchmarks, with the same Thinker model, are 10.56 percentage points on SpeechFCEval, 16.00 on FDBench, and 42.37 on AudioCRAG, where the metric instead measures the fraction of samples that reuse completed speculative retrieval results without additional retrieval. On FDBench itself, task quality also improves, rising from 72.0 to 77.0 percent and Pass at 1 from 57 to 65 percent, which is the counterweight to the benchmark-dependent accuracy caveat I raised earlier: on the disfluency benchmark the reuse actually helps, and the effect is largest, at over 42 points, on the retrieval benchmark.
So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the latency and coverage results, because the mechanism is clean and the measurement is direct: the final-response latency is measured to the onset of the spoken answer after the required tool calls, excluding earlier acknowledgments, and the 6.90 to 4.39 second reduction is exactly the quantity a voice-agent team cares about. I would trust the responder design, because the rule that the responder never blocks on in-flight speculative work and re-runs anything missing is the correct way to make speculative preparation safe, and the ReAct check that rejects interpretations contradicted by later speech addresses the real risk of starting work on a partial transcript. What I would re-verify is the accuracy side, which the paper flags as benchmark-dependent and does not uniformly improve, so the trade-off is real: you are buying latency by doing work during speech that is sometimes thrown away, and I would want the per-benchmark accuracy deltas and the cost of the discarded speculative tool calls, which the paper does not break out, before deciding the trade is worth it for my own agent. I would also want to know how the 500 millisecond heartbeat interval interacts with the tool dispatch budget, because the maximum concurrency of overlapping heartbeats is capped at two in their setup, which is a tuning knob I would expect to matter a lot across workloads.

Bottom line: Heartbeat Thinking is a clean fix for the latency problem in tool-using voice agents, and it buys a more than one-third reduction in final-response latency by preserving in-flight computation across speech updates instead of restarting on every transcript update. Treat the accuracy effect as benchmark-dependent and budget for the discarded speculative work before adopting the paradigm.
