s2n-bignum-bench, by Balaji Rao of the Stevens Institute of Technology,
John Harrison of Amazon Web Services, Soonho Kong, Juneyoung Lee, and Carlo
Lipizzi, a collaboration that is itself a point, because the benchmark
comes out of the people who built and verified the library it is about,
which is the difference between a benchmark that is assembled from public
artifacts and one that is grown out of a real verification effort.

The setting is a gap in how the field measures language models' ability to
do formal reasoning about code. The recent progress in neurosymbolic
theorem proving has been measured on mathematics-oriented benchmarks, on
competition-style problems, and the paper's argument is that doing well on
competition mathematics does not by itself demonstrate the ability to
construct proofs about real-world implementations. The distinction is
specific, and it is the whole motivation. Mathematical proving, even
formal mathematical proving, generally does not involve architectural
state, aliasing, or endianness, and those are exactly the things that
show up when you are proving a property about assembly code that runs on a
real processor, and competence in one does not transfer to the other in an
obvious way. So the benchmark is built to measure the second thing, the
ability to reason about low-level code, using a source that has the
property that the correct answer is already known, because the code it is
based on has already been formally verified.

The source is s2n-bignum, a library used at AWS to provide fast assembly
routines for cryptography, and its correctness has been established by
formal verification in HOL Light, a theorem prover. The paper describes
that verification as a significant achievement for the automated reasoning
group that did it, and it involved two tasks, precisely specifying the
correct behavior of the program as a mathematical property, and then
producing the proof that the assembly code satisfies that property. The
benchmark packages the outcome of that work into a corpus of two thousand
two hundred eighty-four proof obligations, each one taken from the
production-grade library and isolated into a self-contained context, so a
language model can be asked to produce the proof for a single obligation
without needing the whole library. Each problem consists of a HOL Light
boolean term, the statement to be proved, and a setup file that gives the
context, and the evaluation is a three-stage pipeline with a specific
verdict for each problem. Stage one is a syntax and type pre-check, where
the candidate proof is compiled in context as a HOL Light tactic
expression, and problems that fail this stage are excluded. Stage two is
proof execution, where the candidate is assembled into the evaluation
harness and run in HOL Light, with a per-problem timeout and a pre- and
post-axiom-count comparison, and that axiom-count check is the anti-cheating
mechanism, because a submission that tries to smuggle in an extra axiom,
the paper notes that HOL Light has a placeholder tactic analogous to sorry
in Lean, and the axiom-count comparison is what catches a submission that
changes the logical environment rather than producing a real proof. Stage
three is verdict collection, where each problem gets exactly one of five
verdicts, pass, fail, cheating, timeout, or error.

The baseline result is the part a reader would come back to, and it is
specific, and it is a low number. As a preliminary baseline, the authors
evaluate GPT-five point three Codex, run through a command-line harness,
and the model achieves a binary proof-completion rate of four point four
percent in a medium-effort configuration and five point three percent in a
high-effort configuration. The honest read of that number is that it is the
point of the benchmark, not a disappointment, because the number exists to
show that current state-of-the-art code models are far from being able to
do this kind of formal reasoning about real assembly code, and the gap
between a model that can write plausible-looking code and a model that can
produce a proof that a real cryptographic routine is correct is the thing
the benchmark is measuring, and the specific structure of the task makes
the difficulty concrete, because the model has to reason about symbolic
execution of assembly, tracking register state and program-counter
offsets through decode-and-execute semantics for each instruction, and
then simplify the resulting symbolic state terms, which is a very
different cognitive load from the symbolic algebra that competition
mathematics benchmarks require, and the paper argues that the math-centric
proving work has not generally had to deal with architectural state,
aliasing, or endianness, and that those omissions are exactly the gap
the benchmark is designed to expose. The paper positions the benchmark as a
complement to the existing verification benchmarks, and it is specific
about how it differs, because it is built from an industrial library with
shipped object-code artifacts and trusted instruction-set semantics, rather
than from a toy or a synthetic example, and that is what gives it the
property that the ground truth is known and the anti-cheating check is
meaningful.

Now the skeptical read. The first question is the baseline scope, because
the paper reports a preliminary pass-at-one experiment with a single model,
GPT-five point three Codex, and the four point four to five point three
percent range is a data point, not a leaderboard, and a reader should check
whether other models have been run, because the value of the benchmark is
in the trend, in whether the numbers go up as models improve, and a single
baseline is the starting point of that trend, not the measurement of it.
The second question is the transferability of the result, because the
benchmark is in HOL Light, a specific theorem prover with a specific
tactic language, and the question is whether a model that is weak on HOL
Light tactics is weak on the underlying reasoning or weak on the specific
syntax, and a reader who wants to use this benchmark should check how the
results change when the same reasoning is asked for in a different prover
or a different surface syntax, because the gap could be partly a
familiarity gap rather than a reasoning gap. The third question is the
scope of the obligations, because the two thousand two hundred eighty-four proof obligations come from one library, one domain,
cryptographic assembly, and the question is whether the benchmark measures
a general low-level code reasoning ability or a specific cryptographic-
assembly reasoning ability, and a reader should check the distribution of
the obligations, whether they are all arithmetic on machine words or
whether they include the more structural properties, to understand what
kind of competence a high score would represent. And the practical
question is the use, because the benchmark is under review as a workshop
paper, and the question for a reader who wants to use it is whether the
evaluation harness and the problem files are released in a form that can be
run independently, because a benchmark is only as useful as its
reproducibility, and a reader should check that the two thousand two
hundred eighty-four problems, the setup files, and the verdict pipeline
are all available, not just described.

Bottom line: this is a well-motivated benchmark that closes a real gap,
measuring language models' ability to do formal reasoning about real,
already-verified industrial assembly code rather than competition
mathematics, with a corpus of two thousand two hundred eighty-four
proof obligations, a three-stage evaluation with an axiom-count anti-
cheating check, and a preliminary baseline showing that a current state-
of-the-art code model solves only a small single-digit percentage of the
obligations. The open question is whether the single-model baseline is the
start of a trend or a one-off, whether the gap is in the reasoning or in
the prover-specific syntax, and whether the harness and problems are
released for independent reproduction.
