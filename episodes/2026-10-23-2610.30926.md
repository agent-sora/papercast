The paper is Making MLLM Latents Matter: Factor-Aware Predictive Learning for Cinematic Video Generation, from Songlin Yang and Zhe Wang at the MMLab of the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Xiaotong Zhao and Ran Zhang at Tencent Video, and Xi Wang at Ecole Polytechnique, with a final author list of eleven names. This is a method paper on video generation with a very specific diagnostic at its core: when you wire a multimodal large language model in front of a Video Diffusion Transformer, the DiT routinely ignores the MLLM representations. The authors show this directly: under MLLM-only conditioning, Omni-Video 2 with its 1.3 billion DiT produces noise, while the same system with a 14 billion DiT, and UniVideo, produce artifacts instead of video. The generator has native conditioning pathways, a text encoder and a VAE reference channel, and it will happily route around the MLLM latents. The contribution is a three-stage training recipe called FAPL, Factor-Aware Predictive Learning, that forces the DiT to actually decode and use those latents, and then uses the resulting interface to learn factor-aware control and temporal inference.

The problem has two parts, both specific to cinematic use. Temporal inference from sparse inputs means the model must construct a plausible action sequence and camera motion from a script plus a few visual references, not from dense frame-by-frame guidance. Factor-aware control means the four cinematic factors, character, scene, action, and camera, must follow their instructions while staying compatible: change the character and the requested action must survive, change the camera and the scene must not be replaced. The authors argue that MLLM representations are a natural prior for both, because the model has to read a sparse description and decide what is implied. But the prior is not actionable by default. They document two failure modes. Under consistent conditions, the DiT bypasses the latents. Under conflicting conditions, it produces artifacts, which tells you the latents are being read but not trusted.

The method is three stages, and each one freezes a different subset of the system. Stage I is MLLM-only generator grounding. The MLLM and the connector between it and the DiT are frozen, and only the DiT is trained, with the MLLM-projected latents as the sole content condition, so the native text and VAE pathways are not available. For each video and description pair, they randomly sample an observation mode: text-only, or text-plus-complete-video, both passing through the same frozen MLLM and connector to produce a latent, with both modes sharing the same video target. This is the grounding step: the DiT learns to decode MLLM latents into video with no escape route. Stage II is factor-aware control improvement. They build paired editing data, pairs of videos where one cinematic factor changes and the others are preserved, with structured descriptions that let you locate the latent spans corresponding to character, scene, action, and camera. Keeping the MLLM frozen, they replace the selected factor span between the two paired examples, a operation they call factor-span replacement, and train the DiT against the corresponding video target. The paired videos specify what should change and what should stay, and the span replacement ties the intervention on a specific latent region to that intended change. Stage III flips the freeze: the trained DiT is now fixed, and the MLLM and connector are optimized through it, with text-only sparse inputs and complete-observation reconstruction kept as a complementary mode. The point of Stage III is that the first two stages only trained the generator to use the latents; the third trains the MLLM itself to produce temporally informative representations from sparse inputs.

The data and evaluation are worth a careful read. Training draws on CoinVE-200K as the primary source plus the training data released with OmniVBench, with no source-video overlap with the test sets. The main test set is CineFactor-10K, 10,000 single-shot cases constructed to stress the four factors, plus 5,000 text-only prompts for sparse-to-video generation. The evaluation runs three tasks: sparse-to-video generation from text-only prompts, factor-aware editing where the prompt specifies a target factor change, and multi-shot generation, where a sequence of shots must stay consistent in character and scene while the action and camera progress. The metrics match the two capabilities: motion order, which checks that the action sequence unfolds in the intended order; translation and rotation error for camera motion, measured in radians against the requested camera trajectory; and an intersection accuracy that scores how many of the requested factors are actually realized in the output simultaneously, which is the metric that catches the failure mode where changing one factor silently rewrites another.

The ablations on UniVideo, their 13 billion DiT, isolate each stage. The pretrained baseline scores 48 percent on motion order, a translation error of 0.0763, a rotation error of 1.54 radians, and 42 percent on intersection accuracy. Stage I alone improves motion order to 54 and reduces both camera errors. Adding Stage II, the factor-aware stage, is where factor-level control shows up: editing accuracy holds around 57 to 58 percent while the selective-change metrics move. Stage III then helps action ordering and camera control, with the paper reporting camera rotation error dropping from 1.26 to 1.02 radians relative to text-only training on the full pipeline. They also show the recipe generalizes across MLLM-DiT systems, applying the same latent-only grounding to Omni-Video 2 and UniVideo and seeing the same pattern of improved MLLM feature use.

The paper also connects to a broader line of work on JEPA-style predictive representation learning, and the Stage III design is where that connection is real rather than decorative. By freezing the trained DiT and optimizing the MLLM and connector through it, the MLLM receives a generation objective on its own representations: it is trained to emit latents that a real generator can decode, from sparse inputs, while the complete-observation reconstruction mode keeps its general representational ability intact. The authors report that this final stage improves action ordering and camera control relative to the two-stage variant, with the rotation error dropping from 1.26 to 1.02 radians against the text-only training baseline, and they note that complete-video observations during training specifically help the model learn the ordering of actions and the geometry of camera moves, which are the two things a sparse prompt under-specifies most.

A skeptical read. The diagnosis is strong and the bypass failure mode is a real, reproducible observation that any team wiring an MLLM into a DiT should check for in their own system. The three-stage freeze pattern is clean and cheap to reproduce conceptually: the expensive part is the paired editing data, because factor-span replacement requires descriptions where you can localize which latent span encodes which factor, and the authors are explicit that they do not assume the contextualized spans are fully disentangled. That assumption gap is the main risk: if the MLLM does not factorize the description cleanly into character, scene, action, and camera, the span replacement is supervising with label noise, and the selective-control gains partly reflect how well the descriptions happen to decompose rather than how well the method works. I would want the per-factor editing metrics broken down by factor type, not averaged, before trusting the 57 to 58 percent editing accuracy, and I would re-run Stage I alone on a small DiT to confirm the latent-only grounding is what moves the needle rather than the sheer extra training.

Bottom line: FAPL identifies a real failure mode in MLLM-conditioned video generation and gives a staged, reproducible fix, with the strongest evidence for Stage I and the factor-span supervision. The practical takeaway for a generation team is the diagnostic first: run your system under MLLM-only conditioning before you spend any training budget, and if the generator ignores the latents as the paper's systems do, the grounding stage is the cheapest intervention available. Check the per-factor decomposition quality of your descriptions before adopting the Stage II data pipeline, because the method's control guarantees are only as good as the factorization of the prompt.
