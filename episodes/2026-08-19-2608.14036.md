Demystifying Agent Skills: Why They Work—Until They Don't. First authors Zhiyuan Jiang, Fangrui Huang, and Hanwen Xing, working across Princeton University, UC San Diego, Stanford University, the University of Southern California, and Johns Hopkins University. The paper asks a question most skill evaluations skip entirely: when do agent skills actually help, why do they work, and where do they fail. If you maintain SKILL.md-style packages in production agents, this is a measurement paper worth your attention.

Here is the gap being addressed. Skills are structured knowledge packages injected into an agent's environment at inference time. Existing evaluations report aggregate task success with and without skills, which tells you nothing about mechanism. A skill could win because it injects missing facts, because it stabilizes execution, or by accident. Those three explanations imply very different engineering investments, and aggregate success rates cannot distinguish them.

The study design is the strongest part of the paper. The authors compare three conditions built from identical source material: Raw, meaning no prior experience; Workflow Memory, meaning cleaned execution traces from prior runs handed to the agent directly; and Skill, meaning a standardized SKILL.md document distilled from exactly the same traces. Because Workflow Memory and Skill are constructed from the same underlying trajectories, any difference between them isolates the effect of representation rather than the effect of having seen past experience. They sweep a fixed-budget composition grid over trajectory mixtures, from five successes and zero failures down to zero successes and five failures, labeled mixtures like five-success-zero-failure through zero-success-five-failure, and they add a standard versus no-hint variant where success and failure labels are hidden from the skill creator during distillation while the trajectories and execution protocol stay identical.

Scale and tooling: they normalize 8,135 trial records across Terminal-Bench 2.0, SkillsBench, and Terminal-Bench-Pro, using two agent-model pairings, Codex with GPT-5.3-Codex and Gemini CLI with Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, with cross-framework transfer tested by moving Codex-produced artifacts into Gemini CLI. Retrieval studies use Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B for embedding-based ranking, with GPT-5.4 substituted for the unavailable GPT-5.3-Codex in that phase—a switch the authors flag so nobody compares RQ4 absolute numbers against the earlier phases. Downstream runs follow Harbor's standard evaluation workflow with five trials per task and parallelism of twenty unless noted, and retrieval-isolation experiments run one query per task-pool setting. Every task executes in a fixed Docker environment, and skills are placed in the execution environment as reusable procedural resources rather than stuffed into initial context. For the mechanism analysis, they open-coded 240 trajectories into 238 valid labels, consolidated into a taxonomy of three high-level categories—guided success, execution and verification, invocation and budget—and twelve fine-grained modes such as algorithmic logic error, output format mismatch, environment infrastructure failure, timeout exhaustion, and skill guidance misapplied. Human validation of the taxonomy showed strong agreement between independent coders, which matters because everything downstream rests on those labels. The evaluation unit is the trajectory: each executed run receives one dominant-behavior judgment, with a judge permitted to mark rare successful trajectories whose dominant behavior is still failure-recovery, and oracle-status success is read off those judgments so aggregate rates and mechanism labels describe the same runs.

Headline numbers first. Skill-augmented runs reach 61.9 percent oracle-status success, against 59.1 percent for raw execution and 55.9 percent for workflow memory. So skills beat raw by under three points, which is unremarkable. The meaningful comparison is skill against workflow memory: plus 6.06 percentage points from identical evidence, with a 95 percent bootstrap confidence interval running from 0.76 to 11.36 points. Note how thin that lower bound is. The direction is credible; the magnitude is not settled. The composition grid shows the effect across mixtures: at five successes and zero failures on Terminal-Bench-2, Codex reaches 44.5 percent success with workflow memory against 75.5 percent with skills distilled from the same five trajectories; SkillsBench moves from 52.5 to 72.5 percent; Terminal-Bench-Pro sits near 73 to 75 percent for skills. Raw-execution baselines on those benchmarks range from roughly 51 to 59 percent, so distillation from even success-only pools is doing real work beyond exposure to past runs.

Why do skills win? Mechanism labeling says procedural anchoring accounts for 65.7 percent of skill-arm cases, while explicit knowledge injection accounts for 4.5 percent. Skills stabilize action—which setup steps to run, which tool sequence to follow, which checks to perform—not supply missing facts. Consistent with that, execution-layer failures fall hard under skills: environment infrastructure failures drop from 5.3 percent of labels in raw runs to 0.2 percent with skills, output-format and schema mismatches fall from 7.4 to 3.2 percent, shell-command corruption declines, and background service lifecycle failures fall from 2.7 to 0.8 percent. Aggregated at category level, execution-and-verification modes account for 37.3 percent of raw-arm labels and 33.3 percent of workflow-arm labels but only 23.5 percent under skills, while skill-guided success makes up 61.6 percent of skill-arm cases against 54.5 percent workflow-guided success in its own arm—a symmetric picture of skills converting fragile executions into guided ones. On the twenty-six selected Terminal-Bench-2 tasks, lightweight controls land well below the real conditions: an instruction-derived short plan hits 47.7 percent, a test-first template 59.2 percent, workflow memory 62.3 percent, and skill injection 79.2 percent. It is not just any compact hint doing the work. The appendix also documents an effectiveness-versus-efficiency trade-off between skills and workflow memory worth reading before choosing a default.

Where skills fail is equally concrete. Algorithmic logic errors barely move: 8.3 percent of raw labels, 11.0 percent for workflow memory, 7.4 percent with skills. Static verification without runtime checks stays around 12 percent everywhere. Skills reduce operational fragility; they do not repair wrong algorithms or force honest validation. And the abstraction creates its own failure surface: skill guidance misapplied or ignored shows up in 10.0 percent of skill-arm cases versus 0.8 percent for raw execution. Workflow memory fails differently, by overload: timeout and budget exhaustion appears in 10.6 percent of workflow cases versus 4.4 percent with skills, because raw traces drag failed branches and process noise along.

Outcome annotation matters once failures enter the pool. With success-only sources, hiding outcome labels barely matters. Once failed trajectories appear, labeled construction wins clearly; for Gemini on Terminal-Bench-2 at a three-success-two-failure composition, 74.6 percent with hints versus 40.0 percent without, and the same ordering holds across every completed Gemini ratio on both benchmarks. If you distill skills from mixed pools, keep the labels visible to the distiller.

The retrieval result is the one I would take most seriously, because it contradicts intuition. The design uses three independent experiments: downstream execution with the full pool available and accessed skills parsed afterward, offline embedding retrieval ranking descriptions against task instructions, and offline agent selection without execution. On SkillsBench with controlled candidate pools, growing the pool from 5 to 100 collapses actual-use precision from 29.6 to 3.3 percent averaged across pairings—Codex falls from 42.3 to 5.9 percent, Gemini from 16.9 to 0.7 percent—while downstream task success stays essentially flat, moving from 36.4 to 39.3 percent. Recall at a pool of 100 remains 54.3 to 73.6 percent despite precision below 8 percent. Agents inspect multiple candidates, and related non-ground-truth skills still provide usable procedural support. Exact ground-truth invocation is neither sufficient nor necessary. Practical consequence: do not tune your retrieval pipeline against top-one selection accuracy; measure whether task success moves.

Limitations, stated plainly. Coverage is terminal and tool-use benchmarks only; no long-horizon web interaction or open-ended collaboration. Few agent-model configurations, so generalization across scaffolds is unverified. The taxonomy comes from a stratified sample covering roughly 3 percent of normalized records, so rare modes may be undercounted. And the confidence-interval issue mentioned earlier applies to every headline number here.

For adoption decisions: trust the matched-comparison logic—that part is clean experimental design. Re-verify the pool-size behavior on your own skill library before concluding retrieval does not matter, because the finding may reflect distractor similarity specific to SkillsBench. Engineering gotchas: serve skills as retrievable environment resources rather than context stuffing, keep outcome labels during distillation from mixed pools, and expect misapplication, not absence, to be your dominant failure mode. Budget for applicability judgment; a skill that cannot recognize when it does not apply is a liability.

Bottom line: this paper converts skill enthusiasm into a measurable lifecycle—representation, retrieval, invocation, adaptation—and shows the win comes from procedural anchoring, not knowledge injection. Read it for the methodology and the retrieval numbers; treat the 6-point headline as provisional until replicated outside Terminal-Bench.
