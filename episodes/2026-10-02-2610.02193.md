Hierarchical Continuous Diffusion Language Models, or HC-DLM. The first three authors are Hui Ren, Zihan Li, and Chang Liu, with the group at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and one co-author at Amazon.

This paper sits at the intersection of two families of non-autoregressive language models, and it attacks a real structural weakness in each. Discrete diffusion language models model the joint distribution over all tokens through iterative denoising, so they can condition on arbitrary subsets and decode several tokens in parallel. That helps with global constraint satisfaction and bidirectional reasoning. But parallel decoding has a bottleneck: when you unmask several positions in one step, each is sampled independently from its own marginal, so the joint over the simultaneously decoded tokens is a product of marginals. That fails exactly where tokens are tightly coupled by syntax, logic, or arithmetic. Continuous diffusion language models fix that by denoising a single shared continuous state, so tokens decoded in parallel all depend on the same latent rather than being sampled in isolation. But their weakness is the mirror image: the denoiser sees only the continuous state, and no term of the objective involves tokens along the trajectory, so nothing ties the latent to a valid token configuration until the very final decode. The two weaknesses are complementary, which is the whole motivation.

HC-DLM's answer is to make the continuous latent the only persistent generative state, and to treat the tokens as per-step readouts that feed back as a scaffold. The forward direction corrupts the two channels independently: the continuous latent with standard Gaussian diffusion, and the discrete tokens with a categorical forward kernel. The reverse direction couples them. At every reverse step, a token predictor reads token distributions out of the current latent, those tokens are re-noised through the discrete kernel, and the result is fed back as the conditioning for the next continuous latent update. So the token state is a genuine variable of the generative model with its own forward kernel, not a fed-back estimate like rounding or self-conditioning, where the estimate is neither a model variable nor a term of the objective. The authors call this the hierarchical coupling, and it determines both training and sampling.

The training objective is derived from a single variational bound over the two-level trajectory. Because the token state depends on the past only through the latent, there is no separate token transition chain, and the bound collapses to something practical. The encoder that maps the clean token sequence to the clean latent is jointly trained with the generative model, so the latent geometry can adapt to what denoising needs rather than being frozen at a fixed embedding. The sampler then alternates: advance the continuous state, read out tokens, re-noise them, and condition the next step on them, repeating until the clean states are reached. They also add classifier-free guidance on the token channel, where a fresh random token sequence is sampled each step and the prediction is extrapolated along the direction the token scaffold contributes, so a guidance scale above one increases reliance on the token channel. Their main language-modeling run uses a guidance of 2.75.

The experiments are small but controlled, and the matched-size comparisons are the honest ones. For Sudoku, all models are at 6 million sampling-time parameters, excluding embeddings. The autoregressive baseline without its learned ordering heuristic scores 9.73 on the easy split and nothing on hard. Masked diffusion with a top-probability-margin unmasking rule reaches 89.49 easy and 49.88 hard. The matched hybrid CCDD, reproduced at the same 6M scale, gets 94.65 easy and 70.73 hard. HC-DLM gets 94.21 easy, marginally below CCDD, but 72.41 hard, beating it. The out-of-distribution hard split, where puzzles require a strategy outside the fixed set of seven in training, is where the latent scaffold pays off.

Countdown is the arithmetic-planning task, the generalized game of 24, with four and five input numbers. Again at 6 million parameters, HC-DLM reaches 84.41 on CD4 and 37.52 on CD5, against 81.18 and 25.35 for matched CCDD, and the margin widens on the longer-horizon five-number version. Much larger discrete diffusion models, at 85 million parameters, still post the best overall numbers, but those are a different scale, and the paper is careful to read the same-magnitude comparison as the informative one. The setup uses 500,000 problems with 10 percent of target values held out for out-of-distribution evaluation, and because a Countdown puzzle admits many valid arithmetic chains, they report answer-correct accuracy rather than exact-chain match, so the number measures whether the produced arithmetic reaches the target, not whether it reproduces one particular solution.

Language modeling is the clearest result. On the One Billion Word benchmark at sequence length 128, with 118 million parameters in the denoiser and token decoder, they report generative perplexity under a GPT-2-Large scorer. The autoregressive transformer is 66.7. The discrete diffusion models MDM, SEDD, and Duo land at 103.9, 115.9, and 97.6. The continuous models Plaid and LangFlow get 77.3 and 92.2. HC-DLM reaches 75.5, the best of all the diffusion models, beating both continuous baselines that decode only once at the end. Ground truth is 40.4, so there is still a large gap to autoregression, but the point is that iterative token feedback beats decoding the continuous representation a single time. They also analyze how information crystallizes along the trajectory: at each reverse step they decode the intermediate clean-latent prediction into tokens and measure accuracy, which is near chance when the latent is still near Gaussian noise but rises significantly earlier and to a higher plateau than a latent model without the discrete conditioning, meaning the token scaffold pulls useful structure out of the latent sooner rather than waiting for the final decode.

The ablation is the part that most directly supports the design. Removing the token level, which gives a plain continuous latent diffusion model that decodes only at the end, collapses the hard split from 72.41 to 24.74 and the easy split from 94.21 to 50.46. Removing the latent level gives the purely discrete chain at 89.49 and 49.88. So both channels are load-bearing, and the discrete conditioning is what rescues the hard split. They also show the discrete forward kernel matters: the uniform kernel, which corrupts all tokens uniformly, is substantially stronger than the absorbing mask kernel for this hierarchical sampler, because the scaffold needs real content to condition on and per-step readout keeps every position revisable. On efficiency, at 128 function evaluations and sequence length 128, HC-DLM is faster than LangFlow from batch size 16 onward, with the gap widening as the batch grows.

Skeptically, this is a 6-million-parameter result on puzzles and a 118-million-parameter result on a 2013 corpus, so the claim is really about a training and sampling recipe at small scale, not about scaling to frontier language models. The gains over the matched hybrid CCDD are real but narrow on easy Sudoku, and the LM1B perplexity, while best among diffusion models, is still more than double the autoregressive baseline. The mechanism story is coherent and the ablation is clean, which is exactly what you want before trusting the hierarchical coupling as the cause rather than a confound, but I would want to see the hard-split advantage hold at a scale where the latent has enough capacity to actually carry a plan, and where the extra per-step readout cost has been amortized.

The bottom line: HC-DLM couples a continuous latent trajectory with per-step token readout and scaffold feedback under a single variational bound, and at matched size it beats both discrete and continuous diffusion baselines on Sudoku, Countdown, and LM1B, with the discrete conditioning carrying the out-of-distribution reasoning gains. It is a well-motivated, well-ablated architecture contribution to the non-autoregressive lane, but the evidence is small-scale, so treat it as a promising recipe to scale rather than a solved perplexity problem.
