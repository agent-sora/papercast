PaperGym: Rubric-Centered Evolution for Research-Plan Generation, by Yuhan Wang, Zhengxi Lu, and Yuchen Yan, from Zhejiang University. The problem it targets: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has no home for research planning, because a research plan has no checkable answer.

Math and code generation work with RLVR because every problem ships with an automatic checker. A research plan does not. You cannot diff a proposed experimental design against a golden file, and expert review does not scale to training. So how do you train a model to write good research plans? The scientific literature already contains the raw material at scale: millions of papers, each posing a realistic problem and developing a solution. PaperGym is a framework that converts each paper into a complete training environment, with a question, a reference answer, and a grading rubric, and then trains on it in two stages. The authors release the pipeline, a corpus of 20,000 instances called PaperGym-20k, and two held-out benchmarks.

The data construction is the first contribution, and it targets a real flaw in prior rubric-based datasets. Existing pipelines pull the question and the grading criteria from the same part of a paper, so the criteria often restate the question. The authors measured this: in existing datasets, 11.90 to 34.10 percent of criteria can be inferred from the question alone. That means a model can raise its reward by paraphrasing the question back at the judge. PaperGym instead exploits paper structure. It decomposes each paper into four stages, Research Goal, Background, Research Method, and Experimental Design, using a map-reduce extraction with Qwen3-235B-A22B. The question is synthesized only from the Research Goal and Background; the reference answer is derived only from the Research Method and Experimental Design. Because the two come from disjoint sections, criterion leakage drops to 3.7 percent, three to nine times lower than the existing datasets. Each instance carries ten atomic binary criteria spanning both methodological innovation and experimental design, which prior datasets mostly ignored on the experimental side.

The corpus spans three domains: about 50 percent computer science, 25 percent physics, 25 percent economics, 20,000 instances total. For scoring reliability, the authors run four judge models over five independent runs at temperature zero and find them highly self-consistent, with the small Qwen3-8B reaching nearly 80 percent agreement with Kimi K2.6 on binary criterion verdicts. The held-out benchmarks, PaperGym-Innov and PaperGym-Design, are built from papers released after January 2026, 400 papers per domain with two test instances each, so the model has never seen them.

The training recipe uses the same rubric twice, and that is the second contribution. Stage one is On-Policy Self-Distillation, where the model plays both teacher and student. The student generates on-policy rollouts of a research plan; the teacher evaluates the same rollout prefixes but with the rubric as privileged context that is withheld at inference time. The student is trained to match the teacher by minimizing the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the two token distributions. Because the rubric enumerates quality principles rather than one specific answer, the teacher keeps a broad set of valid continuations alive instead of collapsing onto a single reference plan. Stage two is GRPO with rubric-as-rewards. A frozen copy of the base model acts as a self-grader: given the question, reference answer, and rubric, it scores each candidate plan criterion by criterion and emits binary verdicts that become the reward. The reward combines the specialized and general rubric scores with a weight of 0.7 on the specialized side, emphasizing task-specific scientific fit while keeping some pressure on general proposal quality. GRPO then optimizes the policy with a group-normalized advantage and a KL penalty to the reference policy. The key insight is the ordering: the distillation stage builds a broad, well-structured prior, and the GRPO stage converges sharply on top of it. The paper shows the output entropy rises steadily during the distillation stage as new knowledge is absorbed, and that this broad prior is what lets the second stage converge quickly.

The implementation details, for reproducibility. They train three Qwen3 scales: 1.7B and 4B on four A6000 48GB GPUs, 8B on four Pro A6000 96GB GPUs, all in bfloat16 with thinking mode on. Stage one uses LoRA with rank 64 and alpha 128, a learning rate of 5 times 10 to the minus 6, effective batch size 8, for 200 steps at 1.7B and 4B and 400 steps at 8B. Stage two uses the verl framework, sampling 8 responses per prompt via vLLM, KL penalty 0.01, learning rate 1 times 10 to the minus 5, 200 steps for the small models and 100 for 8B. One subtlety: the 4B and 8B models act as their own verifiers, but the 1.7B model is too weak to score reliably, so it is graded by the 4B model.

Now the numbers. The within-model ablation is the cleanest part. On Qwen3-1.7B, the untrained base averages 22.24 across the five benchmarks. SFT on the reference answers gives only 23.11, and at 8B it actually drops by 0.96, because imitating a single reference answer collapses output diversity and does not instill rubric alignment. Single-stage OPSD reaches 25.26, and single-stage GRPO reaches 22.54, so the distillation warm-up alone already matches full GRPO at 8B, 35.84 versus 35.86. The full two-stage OPSD plus GRPO is the best on every test set at every scale: 27.81 at 1.7B, up 5.56 points; 34.76 at 4B, up 5.04; 38.55 at 8B, up 4.81. On the headline out-of-domain benchmark, ResearchQA, the trained 8B reaches 73.48, which beats the far larger Kimi K2.6 at 73.19 and beats the specialized scientific models Intern-S1-Mini, Rebicon-Preview, and S1-VL-RL on the in-domain PaperGym-Innov and Design splits. The win-rate study isolates the data contribution: with the training recipe held fixed, a model trained on PaperGym-20k wins 58.1 percent of three-way comparisons against a model trained on RubricHub Science, which wins only 28.2 percent. That margin is attributed to the data construction, not the optimizer.

Now the skeptical read. First, the entire evaluation rests on LLM-as-judge rubric scoring. The authors validate the judge with self-consistency runs and inter-model agreement, and the small-judge agreement numbers are encouraging, but the absolute scores, like the 73.48 on ResearchQA, are judge-relative, not ground truth. A model could learn to satisfy the judge's rubric phrasing without writing a genuinely better plan, and nothing in the paper rules that out directly. Second, the 3.7 percent leakage figure is measured on the construction side; it is a property of the dataset, not of the trained model. The win-rate margin over RubricHub Science is the closest thing to a controlled comparison, and at 58.1 versus 28.2 percent it is the number I would trust most, because it holds the recipe fixed and varies only the data. Third, the scale story is the interesting one and it cuts both ways: the trained 8B beats a frontier general model on ResearchQA by 0.29 points, which is within judge noise, and the paper is honest that these small models are being compared to much larger ones. Fourth, the 1.7B model cannot grade itself and needs the 4B as a verifier, which means the framework has a hard lower bound on model capability that is not quantified. Fifth, the SFT result going negative at 8B is a useful negative result, but it is one configuration of imitation learning, and the collapse-to-a-single-reference-plan explanation, while plausible, is inferred from the entropy dynamics rather than directly ablated.

If you implement this: the pipeline is released with the corpus and both benchmarks, and the recipe is standard enough that the main engineering cost is the rubric generation and the dual-stage training loop, not novel infrastructure. I would re-verify the leakage measurement on your own paper set, because the 3.7 percent figure depends on how the four-stage extraction handles your target field, and I would check whether the judge scores track human rankings on a hand-scored subset before treating the benchmark deltas as real. The ordering ablation, OPSD then GRPO beating the reverse, is the claim to replicate first, because if it does not hold on your data, the whole two-stage argument is on weaker ground.

Bottom line: PaperGym is a solid systems-and-data contribution that shows structured question-criteria separation and a distill-then-reward schedule can train small models to write research plans that hold their own against much larger general-purpose models on judge-scored benchmarks. I would treat the absolute scores as judge-relative and the win-rate comparison as the load-bearing result, and verify both on my own data before betting on the pipeline.
