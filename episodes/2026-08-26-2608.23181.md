CyberFactory: Scaling Cyber Security Capabilities with Instances from the Wild, by Jian Yang of Beihang University, Haau-Sing Li of ELLIS, and Shawn Guo of IQuest Research, with collaborators including a corresponding author at Singapore Management University. The paper's pitch is a full recipe rather than just weights: an open-source framework that converts public vulnerability records into executable, verifiable training tasks spanning proof-of-concept generation, vulnerability patching, and security question answering, then trains a model called OpenAegis on agentic trajectories grounded in those tasks. On the CyberGym reproduction benchmark under a one-hour-per-task budget, OpenAegis reaches fifty-eight point one percent pass-at-one — twenty-eight and a half points above its own Qwen 3.5 base checkpoint at the same size, and ahead of substantially larger frontier models evaluated under identical scaffolding.

The evaluation target deserves explanation first because everything else serves it. A task asks the agent to produce a proof-of-concept input that reproduces a real vulnerability, given only a natural-language description and the codebase. Success is judged by a differential oracle: the input must trigger the sanitizer failure on the pre-patch build and leave the patched build unharmed. That two-sided check rules out inputs that crash for incidental reasons and makes success machine-checkable — no human grader, no string matching against an expected answer. Because the oracle is programmatic and decidable, the agent gets free verification feedback, which turns exploit construction into a propose, verify, refine loop rather than one-shot generation.

The data pipeline is the actual contribution, and it has genuinely hard engineering in it. Instances come from three sources of ascending difficulty: ARVO, which ships reproducible pre-patch and post-patch Docker images with ground-truth triggering inputs; OSS-Fuzz reports, where only the bug-introducing commit is known and the team binary-searches git history to locate the fixing commit; and raw CVEs from the wild, which provide only affected version ranges plus metadata like weakness categories, requiring commit localization, image reconstruction, and executable verification from scratch. Two filters shape the final set. Behavior checking keeps only instances that actually crash pre-patch and pass post-patch. Then a difficulty filter discards anything inference models solve trivially out of the box — deliberately retaining only tasks that remain challenging even when hint signals like crash reports are provided during filtering, signals that are discarded afterward so training stays honest. None of this subtracts from the operational achievement; it simply bounds what the fifty-eight percent certifies. Vulnerability descriptions get built by classifying fix-commit messages with a language model and synthesizing text only where commit messages are low quality. For question-answering data they take an answer-first stance: answers must originate from trusted sources — execution results, structural facts like call-graph changes, or authoritative report text — while the model only rephrases them into questions, with automated judges checking answer traceability, non-leakage, and uniqueness before a sample survives.

Trajectory synthesis is where the domain prior enters, and the design choice is interesting enough to steal for other fields. Rather than letting the teacher flail, they hand the teacher-only synthesis process a reusable vulnerability-analysis skill: a task-independent workflow prescribing source inspection, guided exploration of candidate inputs, evidence-based validation, and revision when validation fails. Crucially the skill encodes procedure, not solutions — the model still has to find each specific vulnerability through environment interaction. Successful trajectories meeting the oracle are kept for supervised fine-tuning, and OpenAegis never sees the skill at inference time; the bet is that the procedure gets baked into parameters. One more operational detail: rollouts on real codebases can approach the two-hundred-fifty-six-thousand-token context limit, so they compact at ninety percent usage into a continuation state holding verified evidence, failed attempts, open hypotheses, artifacts, and pending actions, discarding redundant logs. Compaction wins its own ablation: fifty-eight point one percent overall versus fifty-two point one for full history and forty-five point six for simple truncation, with context-exhaustion failures dropping from eighteen point seven to seven percent; on long-horizon tasks exceeding forty tool interactions the margin widens further, eight and a half points of pass rate difference, and the ninety-percent trigger beats both eighty and ninety-five. The orchestrator runs everything as explicit stages — source adaptation, instance reconstruction, behavior checking, difficulty calibration, sanitization and synthesis, then training and evaluation — so each data release is reproducible rather than artisanal. Source tiers differ sharply in construction cost: repository-backed images rebuild almost mechanically, while raw wild CVEs demand the full localization pipeline, which is why verified instances from them carry the most training value.

Training is unglamorous and fully specified: full-parameter supervised fine-tuning of Qwen 3.5-397B-A17B for three epochs, sequences packed to one hundred thirty-one thousand seventy-two tokens, global batch thirty-two, Adam with standard settings, cosine learning rate peaking at two times ten to the negative fifth, BF16 across two hundred fifty-six GPUs with expert parallelism thirty-two. Results: besides the fifty-eight point one headline against twenty-nine point six for the untouched backbone, OpenAegis clears GLM 5.2, a seven-hundred-forty-four-billion-parameter mixture, by fourteen point eight points and the trillion-class Kimi K2.7 by six point four — with every model given the same scaffold, tools, prompts, oracle, and hour.

The analysis sections do unusually careful attribution work. Applying the skill to the GLM teacher at inference lifts its pass rate from forty-three point three to forty-six point five despite cutting per-attempt time from sixty minutes to fifteen — the authors explicitly decline compute-matched claims, using five short attempts versus one long one, framing it as throughput for trajectory harvesting rather than algorithmic superiority. More persuasive is the internalization evidence: skill-injected GLM jumps domain-guided exploration calls from zero point zero five to two point zero six per trajectory; after fine-tuning, OpenAegis shows one point three two exploration and one point zero five validation calls per trajectory with no skill present, against near-zero for the base model — same directional shift, independently measured. Fine-tuning also reshapes general tool habits beyond the skill's fingerprint: shell calls rise from seventy to ninety percent of interactions while bare reads collapse, multi-operation consolidated commands grow from four point three to thirty-eight point nine percent combined, AddressSanitizer compilations jump eleven-fold from one hundred fifty-five to one thousand seven hundred ninety-five events, and submission discipline tightens — exactly-once submissions rise from thirty-seven point nine to forty-eight point two percent while five-or-more-attempt spray drops from ten point four to two percent. That pattern says the model learned when it had sufficient evidence, not merely how to mimic a script.

The skeptical ledger. First, the headline comparisons ride on one benchmark family and one scaffold; CyberGym tasks are reproductions of known bugs, so nothing here demonstrates discovering novel vulnerabilities, and the authors don't claim otherwise. Second, the difficulty filter creates a subtle selection effect — the corpus is curated against current model competence, so reported pass rates measure headroom on harder-than-easy slices rather than the field overall. Third, the skill-efficacy numbers come from compute-mismatched runs, transparently labeled as such. Fourth, "released" claims should be treated as promises until the artifact URLs resolve, though code, data, and model links are stated. What transfers regardless of domain: convert messy public artifacts into differentially verified executable tasks, gate your data on difficulty against the current generation of models, synthesize trajectories only against programmatic oracles, teach teachers explicit procedures instead of hoping scale induces them, and audit whether students internalized the workflow behaviorally rather than just scoring higher. CyberFactory is worth reading twice — once as a security paper, once as a template for any field with messy public artifacts waiting to become verifiable training tasks. Bottom line: the strongest open reproducible recipe yet for domain-specialized agentic models — the results credit disciplined data engineering far more than modeling novelty, which is precisely why it generalizes. One practical note for anyone replicating the pipeline: the difficulty-calibration step is only as objective as the judge models used in it, so expect drift over time and version-pin those judges when re-running the constructor. As a deployment artifact the report is refreshingly explicit about dual-use boundaries: instances and trajectories are released for reproducibility, with an accompanying caution that OpenAegis itself carries misuse risk and must be verified against intended usage before operational adoption.
