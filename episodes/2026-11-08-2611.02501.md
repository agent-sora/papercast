SpecPilot, Autonomous Algorithm Discovery and Systems Optimization for Speculative Decoding, from UC Berkeley, Princeton, Stanford, and NVIDIA, with the first three authors, Kunjun Li, Wenhao Chai, and Yihao Liang, as equal contributors. This paper has two claims that are worth separating. The first is empirical: the released systems for parallel speculative decoding leave a lot of their potential speedup on the table. The second is methodological: an autonomous agentic loop can discover the missing optimizations per deployment, without training and without any quality loss. The numbers on the first claim are the hook. With one request, the speedup over autoregressive decoding goes from four point four six times for the stock system to ten point eight seven times after SpecPilot. With batching, it goes from one point nine four times to three point nine three times.

Let me set up the problem, because the mechanism matters. Speculative decoding accelerates memory-bound autoregressive decoding with a lightweight drafter: the drafter proposes tokens, the target model verifies a block in one forward pass, and you accept whatever matches. AR drafters like EAGLE-3 draft sequentially, while parallel drafters like DFlash, DFlash2, and DSpark draft a whole block of eight or sixteen tokens per pass. The stock systems, that is, the ones the authors actually released, are where the gap is. With one request, reading the target weights dominates the per-step latency, so verifying extra tokens is nearly free, and yet stock verifies only one drafted chain, ignoring the other candidates the drafter ranks at each position, which often include the target's actual next token. Using them would accept about twice as many tokens per step, and stock simply does not. With batching, each request adds its own attention, recurrent-state, and prefill work, so the weight reads no longer dominate, and the stock speedup over autoregressive decoding roughly halves. And the third challenge is that the right optimization is deployment-specific: the serving regime, the drafter, the target model, and the GPU all change what pays off. No universal configuration fits.

SpecPilot is the agentic answer. In each round, a cost model locates the largest gaps between the current system and its theoretical ceiling, parallel agents explore candidate directions and integrate the ones that work, and a structured memory carries validated mechanisms forward to new rounds and new deployments. The guardrail is important and worth noting: token forcing checks every output token against the stock system to detect reward hacking, so the discovered optimizations cannot cheat the benchmark by changing what is generated, only how fast. Every final system passes token forcing, and the sealed final configurations, evaluated on held-out requests with two paired runs, land within minus two to plus six percent of the sealed gains on unseen requests. That is the paper's answer to the obvious objection that an autonomous optimizer overfits to its own test set.

What it actually found. A general lossless twelve-bit weight format for the verification pass, tree verification for hybrid recurrent targets, a two-level verification scheme, and three batching mechanisms. The twelve-bit format, which they call W12, is the one that deserves a sentence on its own, because it is a general result with implications beyond this paper. In the single-request regime, reading the target weights dominates the per-step latency, so the fastest thing you can do is read fewer weight bits. W12 is a twelve-bit lossless format that packs the target weights for verification without changing any output token, and it is general: it works across target models and applies to the MLP weights. The tree verification result is subtler. Best-first tree verification exploits the idle drafter candidates, the candidates the stock system never verifies even though the drafter already ranked them, and it works even on hybrid recurrent targets where each tree node must track its own recurrent state, which is the non-trivial systems engineering. The two-level verification is the most interesting of the set: a low-precision quantized pass checks the draft tree and returns the matching chain, then one exact pass over the full-precision weights verifies the chain plus a small new subtree. On the FP8 target at twenty-four requests, the tree budget is a one hundred ninety-two node tree per batch, and the per-request share is a valid prefix-closed tree, which keeps the FP8 verification consistent with the exact pass.

And there is a cross-deployment result that the abstract buries, and it is the one that makes the memory design credible. The structured memory generalizes across drafters, target models, and GPU platforms, and it even transfers to EAGLE-3, a sequential drafter from a different lineage entirely. In one case, EAGLE-3 on Gemma-4-12B re-implemented two designs pulled from memory, the W12 format on the MLP and prompt admission, reached a sealed two point six seven times in its first round, and promotions like calibrated selection raised its final speedup to three point one one times. And porting the memory to a new GPU platform, the RTX PRO 6000 with its different shared-memory budget and no tensor-core wgmma instructions, took the tree and W12 entries and most of the final gain came from the port alone, a sealed one point nine two times over stock, with a split-KV two-level pass raising it to two point four four times, which exceeds the deployment's own estimated two point one two times ceiling over stock. That is a memory that has learned portable structure, not just a cache of one deployment's numbers.

The breadth of the evaluation is the second strength. Four target models, three drafters, two GPU platforms, H100 and the RTX PRO 6000, and five benchmarks. The baselines are tuned per GPU and regime, which matters, because the strongest stock baseline, DFlash2 with DDTree draft trees, is itself at four point eight two times over autoregressive on the single-request bench, and no off-the-shelf EAGLE-3 configuration beats the default by more than two point four percent. Against that, SpecPilot reaches eight point eight six to nine point four one times on H100 and ten point six three to ten point eight seven times on the RTX PRO 6000, one point eight four times faster than the DDTree baseline. With batching on FP8, it beats stock at every batch size, by one point eight five times with DFlash2 and one point nine four times with DSpark overall. In BF16 batched serving it is one point seven one times faster, five point six seven times over autoregressive against three point two zero for stock.

The skeptical read. The speedups are large, and the token-forcing guard plus sealed re-evaluation is a credible defense against the two classic failure modes of autonomous optimization, reward hacking and overfitting to the bench. But the scope is narrow in a way the numbers do not show: this is inference-system optimization for a specific decoding family, and the gains are measured against stock baselines that, for all their slack, are the production systems people actually deploy. The cost model and the agent loop are doing real engineering work, and the paper does not give a detailed accounting of how many agent rounds and how much compute the discovery process itself consumes, which is the hidden cost of a method that optimizes by searching. The twelve-bit lossless format is the kind of finding that should be independently reproducible, and that is the test I would run first.

If you want to use this. The code and the project page are public. If you serve large models with a parallel drafter, the practical question is whether the slack in your stock configuration is the same: the single-request case is where the win is biggest, because the weight-read-bound regime makes tree verification nearly free. If you are single-request latency bound, this is one of the larger, lowest-risk speedups available in the serving stack, with no quality change. If you are batch-throughput bound, the gains are real but roughly halved, which is the physics of the regime, not a failure of the method.

The verdict. SpecPilot makes a clean empirical case that stock speculative-decoding systems leave a large fraction of their speedup unrealized, and shows an autonomous, token-forcing-guarded optimization loop can recover most of it, doubling single-request speedup from four point four six to ten point eight seven times with no training and no quality loss. The discovery cost is the open question, but for latency-bound serving this is a result you can act on directly.
