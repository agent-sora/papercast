Algebraic World Model: From Exploration to Proofs, from Guancheng Pan, Qixin Tan, and Yang Yuan, with Pan at Zhejiang University and Tan and Yuan at Tsinghua University, with Yuan and co-author Andrew Yao also at the Shanghai QiZhi Institute, attacks a question that formal-methods people have been circling for a while. Language models can now produce machine-checkable proofs in Lean, but almost always the definitions and specifications are given to them in advance. The question this paper asks is whether an agent can reason rigorously about a world it has never seen, where the rules it reasons about are themselves hypotheses it has to learn and revise from interaction. The proposed answer is a three-stage framework that ends with closed, kernel-checked Lean proofs about an environment whose rules were discovered, not specified.

The motivating analogy in the paper is Euler's treatment of the Königsberg bridges. Land masses become vertices, bridges become edges, and the pairing of arrivals and departures at every vertex means at most two vertices can have odd degree. The Königsberg graph has four, so no walk crosses every bridge exactly once, and the conclusion holds without enumerating a single route. The same logic applies to game levels. A winning trajectory proves reachability, but proving that no winning trajectory exists requires reasoning about every legal sequence at once. When the environment is unfamiliar, the objects and rules you need for that reasoning are themselves learned, and that is the gap the framework targets.

The framework has three stages. The first, explore and prototype, is an agent actively probing the environment while maintaining executable Python hypotheses. Each hypothesis is a function that predicts what a transition does; the agent runs it against observed transitions, and when it is wrong it revises. The second stage, structure and simplify, organizes the surviving hypotheses into typed objects and shared rules, then applies category-theory-guided transformations to identify shared structure and simplify the representation. Each transformation is checked in Lean using projective and inductive limits, so the simplification is not just a heuristic merge. The paper describes a sufficient cover condition: the learned rules must cover every transition used by the cheapest winning routes, and violations of that condition are what produces wrong bounds later. The third stage, formalize and prove, completes the model in Lean 4 and builds kernel-checked proofs of reachability and impossibility. A winning witness establishes reachability; an invariant, or a cost lower bound, can exclude every legal winning sequence under the model.

The strongest result in the paper is the impossibility one. On two game levels that are actually unwinnable within budget, AWM constructs, in all six independent runs, closed Lean proofs that exclude every legal winning sequence. The baselines, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, both equipped with coding tools, produce no such proofs in twelve baseline runs between them. The paper is specific about why the proofs matter, and the example is level four of a game called sc25. The level has a fuel budget of 18, and the cheapest winning route costs 19. AWM proves in Lean that the minimum winning cost under its learned model is 19, one unit above budget, and separately verifies a route at exactly that cost. GPT-6 Astra, after eight attempts, claims the minimum cost is 22 with no proof. That claim is wrong in a meaningful way: a bound of 22 would wrongly rule out winning sequences costing 19, 20, or 21. A proof that 19 is the minimum and that no route fits in 18 is a different object than a number, and the baselines only ever produced the number.

The honesty of the evaluation is what makes this paper worth reading, because the initial run is not clean. AWM produces correct cost bounds in four of six runs. The two failures, bounds of 20 on level four and 47 on level five, come from overly restrictive learned rules that omit transitions the game's cheapest winning routes actually use, a violation of the sufficient cover condition. The proofs are still valid under the learned models; the models are just slightly wrong about the game. For comparison, Opus 5.5 gets one correct bound out of six, and Astra gets none. Then the authors give AWM only one piece of feedback: that a cheaper win exists. The cost, the route, and the faulty rule are not revealed. AWM re-derives its rules through further interaction and produces new proofs. On level four it discovers that growing onto a fuel cell collects it, moving the bound from 20 to 19. On level five it identifies terrain, not limited range, as the cause of a failed shot, moving the bound from 47 to 37 under its convention of charging the winning move, which the paper notes is equivalent to the game's minimum cost of 36. Both refinements succeed within four additional attempts, and AWM ends at six of six correct bounds. Given the same discrepancy feedback, Astra corrects both bounds but produces no formal proofs, and Claude Opus 5.5 corrects neither.

There is also a small but interesting experiment on proof dependencies. The authors inject a single incorrect rule into a reference model and give Claude Opus 5.5 the faulty model, its Lean proof, and access to the game, with the goal of finding a transition where the model disagrees with the game. Without the proof's rule dependencies, Claude succeeds in 16 of 42 runs. Given the rules the proof used, without being told which one is faulty, success rises to 29 of 42. Told directly which rule is faulty, it is 30 of 42. The dependency structure of the proof is most of the diagnostic value, which is a useful data point if you are building debugging tooling on top of formal proofs.

The second large evaluation is across all 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games, 183 levels in total. The learned models let Lean replay the winning trajectory of every one of the 183 levels, checking each represented state transition and the final goal conditions, with no mismatch. Beyond replay, the models are tested on unseen transitions and on rearranged levels where wall masks and object placements change but the transition code is preserved, and the models predict the unseen transitions and detect changes in game mechanics. A concrete mechanic-change detection example: on one game, ft09, the frozen-rule model accepts all the transitions it was fit on, but gets 0 percent acceptance on the two directions where the mechanics changed, because the rule that nothing else changes detects clicks altering 110 or 146 cells instead of 38. That is the signature of a model that noticed something broke rather than one that hallucinated agreement. On a different game, lp85, the frozen rules accept 100 percent of 2,073 unseen new-trajectory transitions and predict the next frame at 99.95 percent, so the model generalizes to new play while still flagging the modified layouts. With Claude Opus 5 as the driving model, AWM clears all 25 games with a score of 99.9, and its certified winning trajectories use only 43 percent of the human baseline's actions.

Now the skeptical read, which is substantial. The whole edifice rests on the agent getting the learned rules right, and the paper shows what happens when it does not: the proofs are valid but the numbers are wrong, and the only repair mechanism shown is external feedback that a cheaper win exists. In a deployment where nobody can tell you the bound is wrong, an overly restrictive rule silently produces a correct proof of a false statement about the real game. The sufficient cover condition is checkable only against the actual cheapest routes, which you typically do not have. The ARC-AGI-3 numbers are on 25 public games, which is a small set of hand-made puzzles, and the score of 99.9 with Opus 5 as the underlying model means most of the capability is still coming from a frontier model doing the exploring; AWM is the structure and the proof machinery around it. The category-theory step is presented as correctness-preserving via limit constructions, but the practical question is how often the transformations fire and how often they collapse distinctions that later matter, and the paper reports the checks pass rather than a rate at which they did not. The impossibility proofs on the two sealed levels are the cleanest result, but two levels is a proof of concept, not a benchmark.

My verdict is that this is one of the most concrete demonstrations yet of an agent building inspectable, machine-checked mathematical models of an unknown environment, and the impossibility proofs on the sealed levels, where no baseline produced anything checkable in twelve runs, are the result to remember. If you want to put formal guarantees on learned world models, this paper gives you a working pipeline and an honest accounting of where the learned rules can still be wrong.
