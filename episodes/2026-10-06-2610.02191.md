The Missing Primitive is from Shuo Xing, Zilin Dai, and Chengyuan Qian, with a ten-author team spanning Texas A&M University, Harvard, Vanderbilt, Stanford, DARPA, and the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. The model and code are public. The paper is a diagnosis-and-repair study of mathematical reasoning in language models, and its central object is the Mathematical Primitive, a concise, non-procedural representation of the foundational idea that organizes a solution, distinct from the step-by-step derivation itself.

The motivation is a gap the field has been quietly tolerating. Language models post strong scores on frontier mathematical benchmarks, and post-training pipelines are increasingly optimized for formal correctness, but there is no clean measurement of whether the model actually understands the structure that makes a solution work, as opposed to having learned to produce derivations that look right. The paper's framing, borrowed from the mathematics education literature, is that optimizing primarily for formal correctness risks producing a growing volume of valid but opaque proofs without a corresponding increase in mathematical understanding. The question the paper asks is whether the structural knowledge organizing the proofs is accessible to the model at all.

The distinction the paper draws is between two kinds of mathematical knowledge, and it is worth stating precisely. Procedural knowledge is the ability to generate the derivation, the sequence of steps that carries you from the problem statement to the answer. Structural knowledge is the ability to identify the compact, load-bearing idea that explains why the solution works, the organizing principle that makes the derivation make sense rather than just being a string of valid moves. The Mathematical Primitive is the operational form of the structural kind: a concise, non-procedural statement of the foundational idea. A primitive is deliberately not a solution template, it is the reason the template works, and the paper's four dimensions are built around the different epistemic relationships a model can have to that primitive: discovering it from scratch, generating a solution from it, digesting it out of a worked example, or executing a solution given it.

The operationalization is the benchmark, PRIM, which spans four complementary dimensions. Discovery is the ability to identify the primitive on your own, without being given it. Generation is producing a correct derivation. Digestion is taking a given primitive and a worked solution and extracting the organizing idea from them. Execution is using a given primitive to carry out the solution. The separation matters because answer accuracy collapses all four into one number, and the paper's first finding is that it collapses them badly: models with similar solving accuracy have markedly different capability profiles across the four dimensions. The second finding is that correct primitives unlock substantial latent execution capacity, meaning a model that cannot discover the primitive on its own can still execute reliably once the primitive is handed to it. The third finding, and the paper's main diagnostic claim, is that independent Discovery is the dominant bottleneck to mathematical reasoning.

The scale of the bottleneck is quantified. On the failure analysis, 83.6 percent of all failures fall into the discovery-limited category, where the model fails to find the organizing idea rather than failing to execute a known one. The contrast between dimensions is stark: one model rises from 24.73 percent on Discovery to 92.31 percent on Digestion, and R1-Distill-32B rises from 6.04 percent on Discovery to 65.38 percent on Digestion, which is direct evidence of the latent execution capacity the paper is arguing for. The models tested span Qwen3.5 at 27B, 9B, and 4B, plus larger reference models, and the benchmark data is drawn from the verified release of Humanity's Last Exam to control for annotation noise in the original benchmark.

The repair side is a post-training method called ABSORB, which stands for using the primitive as privileged information with a bounded override mechanism. The setup is a teacher-student construction where both policies are initialized from the same base model. The teacher sees the Mathematical Primitive as privileged information and generates primitive-guided reasoning; the student must internalize that guidance without access to the primitive at inference time. The bounded override is the careful part: it selectively transfers the teacher's guidance into the student's reasoning rather than hard-coding the teacher's outputs, which is what keeps the student from regressing on problems it already solved. The paper tests this against plain supervised fine-tuning and against OPSD, the self-distillation baseline, on the Qwen3.5 models at the three scales. The rationale for the privileged-information design is direct: if the student is trained to imitate reasoning that the teacher produced with the primitive in hand, the student learns to produce the same reasoning structure without the crutch, which is the difference between copying the answer and learning the method. The bounded override then decides, per problem, how much of the teacher's primitive-conditioned behavior to transfer, which is the mechanism that trades repair of failed problems against preservation of already-solved ones.

The results are measured and bounded, which I would flag as appropriate. Across the three Qwen3.5 scales, the base models leave 341 failure instances on PRIM, and of the 294 cases with failed Discovery, 147 are discovery-failed but execution-capable, and 147 are failed on both. Supervised fine-tuning and OPSD repair 20.4 and 21.1 percent of the discovery-limited, execution-capable cases, versus 6.8 and 8.2 percent of the doubly-failed cases, which is roughly a threefold ratio and the quantitative version of the paper's claim that discovery-limited failures are more amenable to post-training. ABSORB then improves average performance by 2.42 to 4.48 points across multiple model scales and challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks, which are named in the evaluation set as HLE-Verified, HMMT 25, and Omni-MATH. The gains are modest in absolute terms, and the paper does not claim otherwise. The scale analysis is also informative: the repair rates hold across the 27B, 9B, and 4B Qwen3.5 models, which suggests the discovery-limited failure pattern is not an artifact of a single model family's training, and the paper includes a larger reference model in the comparison to show that scale alone does not close the gap, since the largest models still leave a substantial share of failures in the discovery-limited bucket.

Where I would be skeptical. First, the magnitude. A 2.4 to 4.5 point average improvement on frontier math benchmarks is real but small, and it comes with the explicit finding that existing methods can introduce regressions on problems the model already solves, so the net gain is the difference between the repair rate and the regression rate, and the paper would be stronger if it reported the regression rate per scale more prominently. Second, the primitive is an annotation, and the entire diagnostic rests on the quality and granularity of those annotations; the four-dimension split is clean in principle, but the boundary between Discovery and Digestion depends on exactly how much of the organizing idea is given to the model, and that experimental choice can shift the 83.6 percent bottleneck figure. Third, the privileged-information setup is a distillation from a teacher that sees the answer structure, and at inference the student has no primitive, so the question of how much of the guidance survives as generalizable structure versus as pattern-matching on the specific problem distribution is the open one, and the three scales tested do not fully resolve it.

The verdict: the contribution is the diagnosis, not the repair. The four-dimension decomposition, the finding that 83.6 percent of failures are discovery-limited, and the demonstration of large latent execution capacity are the results that change how you think about math post-training, and they are measured with appropriate care. ABSORB is a modest, honest repair that is more amenable to discovery-limited failures than to doubly-failed ones, and the 2.4 to 4.5 point gains are what such a method should be expected to deliver.
