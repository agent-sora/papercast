Qwen-Planner-Agent, A Closed-Loop AI-for-AI Framework for Real-World Mobile Planner Agents. The first three authors are Tingyu Qu, Weigao Sun, and Yuecheng Liu, from the MAI Team at Alibaba's Token Hub, with the remaining authors including Junpeng Zhao, Long Chen, and Steven Hoi.

The question the paper asks is the one that has been sitting under every agent-building discussion for a year: can AI be both the thing being built and the thing doing the building? Not in some narrow benchmark sense, but in the full loop, where models construct the training tasks, generate the training data, evaluate the model they just trained, and then propose what to change for the next round. The phrase AI-for-AI has become a marketing term, applied to anything from a data-generation pipeline to a single prompt-optimization loop, so the bar for taking a paper that uses it seriously is high, and this paper meets it by building the whole loop rather than describing it. The testbed it picks is one of the hardest available: a mobile phone planner. A planner agent is the top of the stack, the model that decomposes a user goal into a sequence of lower-level actions, manages memory, routes to tools, and decides when a sub-task is done. Real-device interaction is slow and expensive, mobile tasks are long-horizon and messy, and the environment, the actual phones, do not reset cleanly between runs, which makes every piece of the loop harder than it would be in a sandbox. Choosing mobile as the proving ground is the right choice for this kind of paper, because a closed loop that only works in a resettable sandbox says very little about whether the loop can survive a production environment.

The framework has three parts, each corresponding to one stage of the AI-for-AI loop. The first is AI-for-Data, a data flywheel in which specialized agents construct tasks, run the planner against them to collect trajectories, verify and curate the results, and then use the training feedback to decide what tasks to build next. The detail that matters here is the human gate: every data release, every task addition, every sampling-weight change goes through human review before it is frozen for the next training round. The loop is closed, but it is not autonomous. The second part is AI-for-Training, which starts with a supervised cold start on the curated trajectories, then moves to online reinforcement learning in a hybrid environment, a mix of programmatic sandboxes, LLM-simulated environments, and selected real-device sessions. The third part is AI-for-Harness, which treats the scaffolding around the model, the prompt structure, the memory manager, the skills, the tool routing, as something that co-evolves with the model itself, with an LLM-based editor revising the harness instructions each round based on development-set feedback. The whole thing is bound together by one contract, which the paper calls the action-feedback-verification contract: every task, action, observation, and verifier result is recorded in a compatible schema, so that the same trajectory can be used for cold-start supervision, for online RL, and for offline diagnosis without any reformatting. That shared schema is unglamorous, but it is what lets three different subsystems, data, training, and harness, actually talk to each other instead of each maintaining its own private logs.

The training-side contribution is a method called CARE, Competence-Aware Reward-and-Advantage Engineering, and it solves a real problem in agentic reinforcement learning. The problem: when you do group-relative advantage estimation, as in GRPO, a group of trajectories that all succeed looks, to the normalizer, like a group with a small efficiency difference, and the standardization step blows that small difference up to full advantage scale. So once the model has learned to succeed, the gradient signal shifts almost entirely to efficiency, and the model starts compressing its reasoning in ways that do not help and can hurt. CARE addresses this by putting a floor on the advantage normalization denominator, derived from the success threshold, so that efficiency-only signals in fully successful groups stay small relative to the success signal. It is a small mechanism, but it directly addresses a known failure mode of the dominant RL recipe in this space, and the paper derives the effect analytically rather than just asserting it.

The headline result is on MobilePA-Bench, a benchmark for mobile planner agents that covers tool use, memory, skills, and sub-agent coordination. The four axes are chosen to probe the parts of the planner role that are hardest to fake: tool use tests whether the model calls the right capability at the right step rather than narrating it, memory tests whether it can carry state across a long task without re-deriving it, skills tests whether it can invoke a learned procedure instead of re-deriving one, and sub-agent coordination tests whether it can delegate a sub-goal and integrate the result. Qwen-Planner-Agent is the best overall among all the models and systems they evaluate, and it improves over its own base model on every one of the four sub-capabilities, which is a stronger claim than a single aggregate number, because it says the closed loop helped in each axis rather than trading one off for another. The paper also reports that the model improves on non-mobile agentic benchmarks while largely preserving general capabilities, which is the standard catastrophic-forgetting check, and passing it is worth noting because mobile-specific RL runs are notorious for eroding general reasoning.

It is worth spending a minute on the data flywheel, because that is where most of the practical content is. The task construction side is hybrid on purpose. Some tasks run in programmatic sandboxes, where the environment is code and the completion criteria are verifiable by a program, which is the cheapest and cleanest supervision. Some tasks run in LLM-simulated environments, where a model plays the role of the application, which scales the variety of tasks without touching a phone. And some, a selected subset, run on real devices, which is where the network latency, the system exceptions, the hardware variation, and the execution noise live. The trajectories are retained with their full process, the execution errors, the retries, the recovery attempts, the completion evidence, not just the final success label, because the curation step needs to see where a failed trajectory diverged. And the sampling is not proportional to corpus size. The system keeps explicit weights across data groups, capabilities, and difficulty levels, and the feedback loop rewrites those weights each round: mastered tasks get down-sampled, unstable behaviors get up-weighted, and coverage gaps trigger construction of new tasks. That is a real data-engineering contribution, and it is the piece a reader could lift even if they never built the model.

The honest caveats. This is a systems paper from a single lab, and the results are self-reported on the lab's own benchmark, with the baselines being other systems the authors assembled rather than an external leaderboard, so the absolute numbers are harder to place than they would be against a public board. The human-gated loop means the AI-for-AI claim is real but bounded: the system automates the within-round work, while a human still approves the between-round changes, so the loop is not the fully autonomous self-improvement some of the marketing language around AI-for-AI implies, and that boundary is the one a reader should hold to the authors. The CARE mechanism is derived in a specific setting, group-relative RL with a binary success reward, and how much of it transfers to other advantage formulations is not established. What the paper does cleanly is show the loop end to end, with the data flywheel, the training recipe, and the harness co-evolution all wired together and all measured, and that completeness is the contribution. The individual pieces are not exotic, but the wiring, and the evidence that the wiring works on a real device stack, is what this paper adds to the field.
