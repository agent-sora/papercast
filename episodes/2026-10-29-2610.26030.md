Truncate or Not, by Xuyuan Xiong, Siyu Chen, and Zihan Shao, from MMLab at the University of Hong Kong and Yale University, answers a question that anyone training a looped transformer has been answering by hand-waving. A looped transformer reuses a shared block of layers, running it many times to get depth without extra parameters, and to save training compute, most people truncate backpropagation through time, propagating gradients only through the last few loops. But truncation produces biased gradients, so it weakens the very benefit you are paying for. The paper asks whether the compute you save is worth the depth you lose, and it answers by running nearly six hundred training runs and fitting a single scaling law that covers both choices.

The experimental grid is the core of the paper. They vary four things jointly, model size, training tokens, forward loop count, and backward loop count, and the scaling suite spans 58 million to 919 million non-embedding parameters, over three orders of magnitude in training tokens per parameter, with forward loop counts up to sixteen. The key move is separating the forward loop count from the backward loop count. In a run with T forward loops and B backward loops, the model runs the shared block T times in the forward pass, but gradients only flow through the last B of them. Setting B smaller than T is exactly truncation. Most prior work either fixed B equal to T, which is full backprop, or prescribed a specific truncation recipe, like truncate to half the loops, and fit a separate scaling law to each recipe. This paper varies both independently, which is what lets it answer the actual question, namely, given a fixed compute budget, how should you split compute between forward loops, backward loops, model size, and training tokens.

The scaling law they fit expresses the benefit of looping through an effective parameter count, defined as the size of a non-looped model that would achieve the same loss at the same token budget. That is a clean way to talk about the value of depth. Fully trained loops increase the effective parameter count with diminishing returns, because each additional loop through a shared block buys you less than the one before, and truncation discounts that gain, because the untrained loops do not contribute their gradients to learning. So the law has two competing terms, more forward loops raise the effective size, truncation lowers it, and the total compute is spent on forward passes, backward passes, model size, and tokens.

The answer to the title question depends on what you hold fixed, and that is the subtlety. If the forward loop count is prescribed, if you have decided to run the model a fixed number of times at inference and you are only choosing how to train it, then truncation does pay, and the paper recovers the result from prior work. The backward compute you save lets you afford a larger model and more training tokens, and the gain from that more than offsets the discounted loop benefit. That is the regime where the truncated recipes in the literature are correct. But if both loop counts are free, if you can choose the architecture as well as the training, then the law favors full backpropagation, and the mechanism is worth understanding. When you reduce the forward loop count, you free up compute, and the law says the right way to spend it is on additional backward loops until every remaining loop is trained. In other words, rather than running eight loops and training four, the law prefers running four loops and training all four, because the reallocated compute buys more effective parameters at the same total cost than the truncated eight-loop setting. In matched-compute experiments, where they hold total FLOPs constant and compare a full-backprop run against a truncated run, the full-backprop setting with fewer loops consistently wins, which is the empirical confirmation of the law.

The practical recommendation falls out of the law when model size is also free to vary. The compute-optimal looped setting is two fully trained loops, meaning T equals B equals two. A model that runs the shared block twice, with gradients through both, at a given compute budget, beats the compute-optimal non-looped model while using about twenty seven percent fewer non-embedding parameters and only about eight percent more training compute. The paper gives a concrete instance, a 344 million parameter model with two fully trained loops achieving a validation loss of two point three six nats, against a 471 million parameter single-loop model at two point three seven nats, with the looped model using fewer parameters and a little more compute. The other finding is that at fixed model size, both the compute-optimal loop count and the optimal token budget grow with compute, so as you scale up your training budget, the law says you should use more loops, not fewer, which is the opposite of what the truncation reflex would suggest.

The mechanism behind the law is worth spelling out, because it is where the practical advice comes from. Truncated BPTT saves backward compute by cutting the gradient chain after B loops, but the cut leaves the gradient biased, it over-weights the recent loops and under-weights the earlier ones, and the bias does not go away if you run more forward loops, because the backward chain is still severed. Full BPTT pays the full backward cost but gives you the true gradient through every loop you unrolled, so the optimization signal is faithful to the architecture you are actually running. When you then let the optimizer choose the loop count jointly, it prefers fewer, fully trained loops over many, partially trained ones, because the faithful gradient lets it get more value per parameter out of the shared block. The law's parameterization of that trade is through effective parameters, a single number that folds the loop count and the truncation depth into the model size, which is what lets the authors fit one unified scaling law across all roughly six hundred runs instead of a separate surface for every loop and truncation combination.

The limitations are the usual ones for a scaling study. The runs go up to 919 million non-embedding parameters and sixteen loops, which is well below frontier scale, so extrapolating the law to much larger models is an assumption, and the effective-parameter-count parameterization is a fit to this grid, so its accuracy outside the fitted range is not established. The comparison is on validation loss for language modeling, not on downstream task performance, which is the standard caveat, though for a study of training compute allocation, loss at matched compute is the right currency. There is also a subtle regime issue the authors are careful about. The law says the optimal loop count grows with compute at fixed size, and that is the opposite of the truncation reflex, so if your training run is at a compute budget well above the study's range, the right move under the law is more loops with full backprop, not fewer loops with truncated gradients, and the paper does not give you the exact loop count at your budget, it gives you the direction of the effect. The code is released, which lets you reproduce the grid at small scale and check the law against your own hardware, and the prelude-and-coda structure, where the looped block is bracketed by unshared layers that are always fully trained, is worth noting, because it is a standard detail in looped architectures that affects how the effective parameter count maps to the actual parameter count, and any reproduction needs it.

What I would trust is the regime distinction, that truncation is right when the forward loop count is fixed by inference and wrong when the architecture is free to move, because it follows directly from the law and is confirmed by the matched-compute runs. What I would re-verify is the two-loops recommendation at your own scale, because the compute-optimal loop count grows with compute, and at a budget well above the study's range the optimal may be three or four fully trained loops rather than two. The engineering takeaway is a default. If you are starting a looped transformer run from scratch, do not truncate by reflex. Train the loops you run, keep the loop count at two, and spend the compute you would have saved on backward passes on more tokens and a smaller model, because the parameter savings are real and the compute penalty is small. That is a concrete, testable rule, and it is the kind of result that belongs in the default configuration of a training framework.
