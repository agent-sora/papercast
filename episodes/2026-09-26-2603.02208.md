Reasoning Core, by Valentin Lacombe, Valentin Quesnel, and Damien Sileo,
from a group in Lille, France, that spans the University of Lille, Inria,
the CNRS, and Centrale Lille, and the paper notes that the suite is
released under the MIT license, which is a detail worth holding onto,
because a data-generation tool under an MIT license is something a
practitioner can actually use, which is a different category from a
benchmark that is only useful as a measurement.

The setting is a specific bottleneck in how language models get their
reasoning ability, and the paper frames it as a data problem rather than
an architecture problem. Training on verifiable symbolic data, data where
the answer to a reasoning task can be checked by a program rather than by
a human, is a promising way to expand the reasoning frontier of language
models beyond what the standard pre-training corpora provide, because the
standard web text has reasoning in it, but it is reasoning without
verification, the model sees the argument, it does not see the check, and
the paper's argument is that the check is the part that matters, because
a model trained on tasks where every example has a verifiable answer
learns the structure of correct reasoning in a way that is not available
from passive text. The specific gap the paper identifies is in the
existing procedural generators, the tools that produce synthetic reasoning
data, and the gap is distributional breadth, the existing generators often
rely on fixed puzzles or on a small set of templates, and a fixed puzzle
is a single distribution, and a small set of templates is a few
distributions, and neither delivers the breadth that a pre-training run
needs, because pre-training is a process that sees trillions of tokens, and
a data source that is a few templates is a drop in that, and the paper's
contribution is a suite that is designed to be scalable, to generate
verifiable symbolic reasoning data at the scale that pre-training
requires, with the distributional breadth that the template-based
generators lack.

The suite covers five formal domains, and the list is the part that gives
it the breadth, and each one is a core area of formal reasoning rather
than a puzzle. The first is PDDL planning over randomized domains, which
is the classical planning problem, the problem of finding a sequence of
actions that achieves a goal in a domain described in the planning domain
definition language, and the randomized-domains part is what gives it the
breadth, the planner is not solving one fixed puzzle, it is solving a
distribution of puzzles generated by randomizing the domain. The second is
first-order logic with equality, which is the core of mathematical
reasoning at the level of quantifiers and predicates, and it is the domain
that connects the suite to the theorem-proving work that this podcast has
covered a lot of. The third is context-free grammar parsing and
generation, which is the structural reasoning of linguistics and compiler
design, parsing a sentence into a tree or generating a sentence from a
tree, and it is a domain where the verification is clean, the parse either
is valid or it is not. The fourth is causal reasoning over random Bayesian
networks, which is the reasoning about cause and effect in a probabilistic
graphical model, and the random-networks part is again what gives it the
breadth, the causal questions are generated over a distribution of
networks rather than a fixed one. The fifth is systems of equations, which
is the algebraic reasoning domain, and it is the one that is closest to
the standard mathematics that the models are already good at. Each of the
five is paired with an external solver, and that is the verification
mechanism, the solver is the program that checks whether a generated
example has a valid solution, and it is what makes the data verifiable
rather than just generated, and the suite admits continuous difficulty
control, which is the property that makes it usable for curriculum design,
because a data generator that can dial the difficulty continuously is
something you can use to build a training curriculum, starting easy and
getting harder, rather than a generator that produces one fixed difficulty
level.

The paper reports that the data is usable at both ends of the training
process, and that is the part that distinguishes it from a post-training
only tool. The examples can optionally include solver-derived reasoning
traces, which means the verification is not just a binary check, the
solver can produce the chain of steps that leads to the answer, and that
trace can be used for supervised training, and the paper notes that this
enables supervised training from the earliest pre-training stages, so the
data is not just for the final fine-tuning, it is for the pre-training
itself. The experiments show that mixing the Reasoning Core data into
pre-training improves downstream reasoning while preserving, or slightly
improving, the language modeling quality, and that second clause is the
part that matters, because the concern with adding synthetic data to
pre-training is that it crowds out the natural language, that the model
gets better at the puzzles and worse at the text, and the paper reports
that the language modeling quality is not hurt, which is the result a
practitioner needs to hear before trusting the synthetic data. The
zero-shot evaluations confirm that the tasks challenge the frontier
models, which is the property that makes the data useful, because if the
frontier models already solved the tasks, the data would not be expanding
the frontier, it would be reinforcing what the models already know, and
the challenge property is what makes it a frontier-expanding tool.

Now the skeptical read. The first question is the distributional breadth
claim, which is the paper's central contribution, and the honest version
of the question is whether the five randomized domains are actually broad
enough, because the paper's argument is against the template-based
generators, and the comparison is real, the randomized domains are broader
than the fixed templates, but the question is whether five domains, even
randomized, is broad enough for pre-training, because pre-training is a
process that is trying to build a general reasoning ability, and a data
source that is five formal domains is a narrow slice of reasoning, and a
reader should check how the breadth of the five domains compares to the
breadth of the natural-language reasoning in the standard corpora, because
the contribution is only as strong as the comparison, and the comparison
the paper makes is against the template generators, not against the full
pre-training corpus. The second question is the verifiability, because the
external solver is the verification mechanism, and the question is whether
the solver is checking the right thing, because a solver can verify that
an example has a solution, but the question is whether it is verifying that
the reasoning trace is valid, and the paper notes that the traces are
solver-derived, which is the right call, the trace comes from the solver,
not from a model, so it is guaranteed to be valid, but a reader should
check the solver for each of the five domains, because a solver that is
itself buggy is a verification that is not a verification, and the
trustworthiness of the data is only as strong as the trustworthiness of
the solvers. The third question is the scale, because the paper says the
suite is scalable, and the honest version of the question is how scalable,
because scalable is a design property and the question is whether the
generation actually runs at the scale that pre-training needs, and a
reader should check the generation throughput, because a generator that
produces verifiable data but too slowly is not a pre-training data source,
it is a fine-tuning data source, and the pre-training claim depends on the
throughput. And the practical question is the MIT license, which is the
strong part of the contribution, and the question for a practitioner is
whether the solvers are also under the MIT license, because the suite is
the generator, but the verification is the solvers, and if the solvers are
under a different license, the practical use is constrained by the
solvers, not the generator, and a reader who wants to use this should
check the license of each of the five solvers, not just the suite.

Bottom line: this is a well-motivated and practically useful contribution,
a scalable suite for generating verifiable symbolic reasoning data across
five core formal domains, each paired with an external solver for
verification, with continuous difficulty control for curriculum design,
and with solver-derived reasoning traces that enable supervised training
from pre-training, and the experiments show that mixing the data into
pre-training improves downstream reasoning without hurting language
modeling quality, and the tasks still challenge the frontier models. The
open question is whether the five randomized domains are broad enough for
pre-training, whether the solvers are verifying the reasoning traces or
just the answers, whether the generation actually runs at pre-training
scale, and whether the solvers are under the same MIT license as the
suite.
