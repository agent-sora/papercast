Title: Beyond Attention Maps: Understanding and Improving Medical MLLMs via Visual Values
Authors: Yichen Zhao, Fenghe Tang, Wenxin Ma
Labs: University of Science and Technology of China; The University of Texas at Austin
Arxiv: 2611.29361
Day: 2026-11-24
Upvotes: 1
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29361
---

Beyond Attention Maps, by Yichen Zhao, Fenghe Tang, and Wenxin Ma, at the University of Science and Technology of China and the University of Texas at Austin, is an interpretability paper with a clean experimental design and a practical method attached. The claim is that the field's obsession with attention maps is missing half of the mechanism. In an attention block, the attention weights decide where to look, but the value representations decide what information is actually retrieved, and the attention readout is the weighted sum of values. Nearly all existing visual grounding work for multimodal large language models shapes the weights and leaves the values untouched. This paper uses paired medical imaging, where the same patient has a non-contrast scan and a contrast-enhanced scan of the same anatomy, to separate the two variables, and it finds that swapping values moves the model's answers far more than swapping attention maps.

The testbed is a paired medical visual question answering dataset across three anatomical sites, liver CT, brain MRI, and breast MRI, and two imaging modalities. The liver component is built from MCT-LTDiag, 500 patients split into 450 training and 50 internal test, with 44 additional patients from TriALS held out for external evaluation, and all splits are patient-disjoint, the correct hygiene for a clinical study. The brain component uses paired native and contrast-enhanced T1 scans from the BraTS 2021 release. The pairing is the key property: registered image pairs give the same patient, the same question, and the same anatomy under two different visual evidence conditions. The model can answer incorrectly from the non-contrast image and correctly from the enhanced one, and the paper's first finding is that this happens even when the spatial attention patterns are nearly identical, with an attention map similarity of 0.915 in their example. Where the model looks cannot explain the different answers. What differs is what is retrieved at those locations.

The controlled intervention quantifies it. In the contrast-enhanced to non-contrast direction, they substitute either the attention weights or the visual values from the enhanced view into the non-contrast branch and measure the change in correct-option probability and accuracy. On the liver CT set with a Qwen3-VL model, transferring the attention weights shifts accuracy by 22.36 percentage points while transferring the values shifts it by 37.55. A second medical model, MedGemma, shows the same pattern with an even larger gap, 34.76 versus 57.75. The reverse direction, non-contrast into enhanced, is negative for both, as it should be, and the value transfer hurts more. They also perturb the attention weights with noise while keeping the transferred values, and the value effect persists at 25 and 50 percent noise, which rules out the explanation that the values only help because they arrive attached to a better map. The conclusion is direct: the performance gap between the two views is driven by the visual content carried in the values, not by the spatial allocation in the weights.

The method, VIVID, builds on that finding. The setting is distillation: a contrast-enhanced teacher has richer visual evidence, and the goal is to transfer its advantage to a non-contrast student that at inference sees only the plain scan, because contrast administration adds cost and risk. Standard on-policy distillation aligns the student to the teacher's predictions along student-generated trajectories, but it does not check whether the teacher's corrections actually align with the visual evidence the student can use. VIVID adds an auxiliary branch that shares the student's non-contrast input and generated answer prefix, but injects the paired enhanced visual values into the visual readout at the locations the student's own attention selects. At each decoder layer, it ranks the non-background image tokens by their averaged attention weight and selects the smallest set whose cumulative weight reaches half of the total, a threshold of 0.5, and mixes the difference between the enhanced and non-contrast values into those positions with a mixing coefficient of 0.5. The resulting prediction shift captures how the student responds to the enhanced evidence, and that shift becomes a reference direction: the teacher's correction is projected onto it in vocabulary space, and only the positively aligned component is kept as the distillation target. A weak direct teacher supervision term fills in when the aligned correction is small, and the whole objective is a Jensen-Shannon divergence to the constructed target, with the student being the only thing that updates. At inference, the auxiliary branch is gone.

The results are consistent but, in the honest reading, small on top of a large supervised fine-tuning gain. On their own paired dataset, a 2-billion Qwen3-VL fine-tuned on supervised data reaches 76.81 internal average, and VIVID adds it to 77.43, 70.14 external, while the on-policy distillation baselines, OPSD, Med-OPD, and VA-OPD, all sit at 76.47 to 76.49 internal and below 68 external. The 4-billion model shows the same shape, VIVID at 79.49 internal and 72.05 external versus 79.11 and 71.01 for the best baseline. Against the best off-the-shelf medical model, the trained system is up 15.26 points internal and 11.27 external, but that comparison is dominated by the fine-tuning, not the distillation method. The ablations support the mechanism: random region selection and swapping attention weights instead of values both lose, and skipping the projection, distilling the auxiliary prediction directly, also loses, which is the cleanest single evidence that the value-induced direction, not the auxiliary prediction itself, is what carries the signal. The full method's hyperparameters are modest, a value mixing coefficient of 0.5, a cumulative-attention threshold of 0.5 for patch selection, a top-100 vocabulary representation, and a teacher supervision weight of 0.1. On the distribution side, the paper reports that VIVID's outputs are more selective at task-critical positions: its margin selectivity, the probability gap between the top two options on medical-judgment spans, is 2.45 points for the 4-billion model versus 1.21 and 1.22 for SFT and OPSD, and on questions all three models answer wrongly, VIVID assigns the correct option more probability than either baseline. The cross-benchmark generalization is the more impressive number: on VQA-RAD, SLAKE, PMC-VQA, and ChartQA, VIVID achieves the highest average at both scales, 68.72 for the 4-billion model, without any task-specific adaptation, and the 4-billion SFT baseline already scores 67.80, so the distillation contribution is about a point there.

What I would verify. The interpretation of the value effect is strong but rests on one imaging modality pair and a specific registration quality, and the value intervention is a surgical operation on activations that is easier to justify in a medical setting with a clean enhanced-to-plain contrast than in general visual grounding, where no privileged enhanced view exists. The distillation gains over the on-policy baselines are under a point on the internal sets, which is real by their multi-seed reporting but small enough to be benchmark-sensitive, and on the external liver set the 2-billion model jumps from 48.89 to 52.64, so the external improvement is where the method shows its largest margin, which is the right place for a generalization claim. The honest summary is that the interpretability finding is the contribution, and the method is a credible but modest application of it.

Two-sentence verdict: this paper gives the field a clean, falsifiable demonstration that what multimodal models retrieve through their value vectors matters more for their answers than where their attention maps point, and that result survives noise perturbations of the maps themselves. The reverse-direction experiments, where non-contrast values are pushed into the enhanced branch and correct-answer support drops by 24.89 and 32.09 percentage points for the two models, are the part that rules out the trivial reading that the enhanced values are simply a better image. VIVID is a small but mechanistically grounded improvement over on-policy distillation in medical visual question answering, and its real value is as a template for value-aware interventions in settings where a privileged view exists.
