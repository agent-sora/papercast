The paper is called MMDM: Measuring Massive Multitask Decision Making. The first three authors are Junhao Shen, Qing Zong, and Yue Guo, with the work spanning the Chinese University of Hong Kong and the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology.

Let me start with what a "System-1 decision model" is, because the whole benchmark hangs on that term. It is a model that, given a textual state and a query, returns a probability distribution over a set of options that the request itself defines, all in a single forward pass. It does not write out a chain of reasoning and it does not produce a free-text answer. It reads the situation, looks at the candidate options, and hands back a ranked set of probabilities. Think of it as the fast, intuitive reflex of a model rather than the slow deliberative one, which is where the System-1 naming comes from. These models are increasingly being wired into agent pipelines for things like intent classification, choosing which tool to call, routing a request to the right sub-agent, and scoring a piece of work against a rubric. The practical question the authors pose is whether these models actually make sound decisions when the decision is hard, or whether they only look good on easy, familiar, already-circulated test data.

The gap they identify is about test contamination and scope. A lot of the existing decision-model benchmarks are built from widely published public datasets, so there is a real risk that the models being scored have already seen the test material in pretraining, which inflates scores without measuring generalization. On top of that, the existing benchmarks tend to keep option spaces small and contexts short, whereas the real decisions these models face involve long inputs, hundreds of possible options, and options that depend on each other. A genuinely useful model, for example, has to track the current state of an agent, decide whether an approval given earlier is still valid after the document changed, and pick an eligible tool from a large catalog where each tool has different prerequisites. No single existing benchmark tests all of that at once.

So MMDM is their answer: a benchmark of five thousand four hundred forty-eight questions spread across sixteen task categories. The categories range from world knowledge and reading comprehension to deductive reasoning, constraint planning, agent state tracking, tool and skill selection, preference judgment, and rubric scoring. The construction is deliberately mixed. Some tasks are newly authored from scratch, and others are source-grounded recompositions and rewrites of existing material, where the source answer and the contextual evidence are used to rewrite the state, the query, and the distractor options so that memorizing the original surface form is less useful. The option spaces are large by benchmark standards, ranging from as few as two options to as many as one thousand twenty-four, and the inputs run up to sixty four thousand eight hundred seventeen tokens in the longest cases.

There are three answer types, and this is where the benchmark is more careful than most. Hard single-select asks for one correct option, and the model is right if it assigns that option the highest probability. Hard multi-select asks for the complete set of valid options, and the model only counts as correct if every valid option ranks above every invalid one, which is a much harder bar than getting the top one right. And soft-label asks the model to match a reference probability distribution over outcomes, so if a tool call has a seventy percent chance of success because the service is intermittently up, the reference puts zero point seven on success and zero point three on failure, and the model has to get the whole distribution right, not just the modal answer. Scoring rubric tasks fit the soft-label track, where the expected score over the rubric is what matters.

The scoring is worth a sentence. For single-select, a question is correct if the chosen option beats the next-best. For multi-select, a question is correct only if the lowest-probability correct option still beats the highest-probability wrong option, and they also report a separation-balance F-score that rewards both separating correct from incorrect options and distributing mass evenly across the correct set. For the soft track they use Brier error over the full reference distribution and a normalized mean absolute error for the expected score. All of these are standard and defensible, and the fact that they report accuracy and calibration separately rather than collapsing everything into one number is a good sign of care.

Now the results, because this is the part you actually want. They evaluate twenty-eight decision models. The headline finding is that the models that do well on common knowledge fall apart on the interdependent and constrained tasks. Perplexity's Decider V1.2, one of the stronger models in the set, gets seventy eight point seven five percent hard single-select accuracy on world knowledge, but only fifty three point nine five percent on option logic and forty seven point nine five percent on constraint planning. The best hard multi-select accuracy across all twenty-eight models is just thirty six point eight one percent. That is the number to sit with. Recovering the complete valid set, which is exactly what you need when you are choosing which tools from a catalog can each fulfill a request, is still close to a coin flip even for the best systems out there.

They also break the results down along two axes that most benchmarks smear together: how long the input is and how many options the model has to choose among. The same set of models does not rank the same way in every slice of those two dimensions. A model that leads in the small, two-to-four option region is not necessarily the model that holds up in the largest option-count region, the one with one hundred twenty-nine up to one thousand twenty-four choices, and the same is true as the complete input grows from a few hundred tokens to tens of thousands. The point is structural: a single aggregate accuracy number would hide all of that per-bin variation, and the whole reason the benchmark reports the slices separately is so you can see which capability is actually failing.

A few of the case studies are genuinely illuminating about what the models cannot do. In one, a document is certified, then edited, then converted, and publication requires the certificate to match the final revision. Perplexity picks a workflow that approves before the edits, which invalidates the certificate, while the other two models pick a valid ordering. In another, eight reports refer to each other with truth and falsehood claims, and there is a unique consistent assignment under which seven of the eight reports are true. All three models still rank the one false report above at least one true report. And in a scoring case, eight of nine records pass every check and one is missing a stamp, so a three-record sample gets the lower score exactly one third of the time, yet two of the three models assign more probability to the lower score than the higher one. These are not esoteric edge cases; they are the kind of thing a real agent pipeline hits.

Where I would push back, or at least be careful, before treating this as settled. First, the authors themselves note a limitation that matters: the benchmark is text-only, defined by a fixed state, query, and option set, so it does not cover multimodal inputs or interactive multi-turn trajectories, which are a large part of what agents actually do. Second, the multi-select questions are concentrated in option logic, constraint planning, workflows, and tool or skill selection, while most of the soft targets come from rubric scoring. Those are the settings where clean, verifiable labels are easy to construct, but they are not the full space of subjective judgment. Third, and this is the one I would re-verify before citing the headline numbers, the construction mixes newly built tasks with rewrites of public source material. They argue that the rewrites reduce memorization-induced inflation, but prior exposure to the underlying source can still happen, so the contamination risk is reduced, not eliminated. If you are going to use these numbers to compare models in a real pipeline, I would run them on a held-out slice you assemble yourself before trusting the cross-model ordering.

There is also a calibration finding worth flagging because it is easy to miss. Higher selection accuracy does not mean better calibration. One model has higher single-select accuracy than another yet a larger expected calibration error, and one of the five representative models is overconfident in the middle of its range. If you are deploying these models with a confidence threshold to decide when to act versus defer, that gap between accuracy and calibration is exactly the thing that will bite you, and the benchmark's separation of the two is what lets you see it.

So here is my bottom line. This is a well-constructed, unusually careful benchmark that does what most decision-model evaluations do not: it keeps fresh instances, large option spaces, and long inputs together, and it scores accuracy and calibration as separate axes, and the twenty-eight-model evaluation makes the current capability gaps in multi-select recovery and constrained decisions hard to ignore. The caveats are real but modest, centered on text-only scope and residual source exposure, so treat the cross-model rankings as directionally strong but re-verify on your own tasks before you bet a production pipeline on them.
