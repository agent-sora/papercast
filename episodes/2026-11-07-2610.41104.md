MUON-RT, Reusing the Teacher's Trajectory for Momentum-Free Muon for LLM Self-Distillation, from the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur and Amazon. The authors are Aryan Das, Vinay Kumar Verma, and Aakansha Mishra. This is an optimizer memory paper, and the result is a small, clean win. If you are running self-distillation with an exponential moving average teacher, the teacher's trajectory is already sitting in your memory, and it can replace the momentum buffer that Muon would otherwise store. The paper shows that removing that buffer cuts persistent training memory meaningfully and, counterintuitively, improves accuracy on top of it.

The setup. Self-distillation for large language models, in the form of Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning, SDFT, and Self-Distillation Policy Optimization, SDPO, adapts a model by feeding it privileged context to elicit supervision from its own existing capability. The student learns toward a teacher that is an exponential moving average of the student's own weights. That teacher is the standard technique, it is what makes the target stable. The catch is memory. An EMA teacher gives you a moving copy of the parameters, but the Muon optimizer on top of it stores its own separate momentum buffer for each hidden weight matrix. So you are carrying two histories of the same update process, one in the teacher, one in the optimizer. The question the paper asks is whether the teacher's history can stand in for the optimizer's momentum.

The core observation is that the teacher-student displacement, the difference between the EMA teacher's weights and the student's current weights, is not just a gap. Under the EMA update rule, that displacement is exactly an exponentially weighted sum of all the updates the student has applied, discounted by the EMA coefficient. So the displacement already encodes a smoothed, exponentially weighted history of the applied updates, which is the same thing momentum is trying to compute. The paper proves this in a lemma and then shows that under a local model of the objective, the displacement smooths temporally uncorrelated noise in the updates, which is the second job momentum does. There is one asymmetry the paper is honest about. The displacement is a history of applied updates, whereas classical momentum is a history of gradients from possibly outdated targets, so the two are not identical objects. But for the purpose of replacing the buffer in a self-distillation run, the displacement is close enough, and it is already in memory.

A quick word on the baseline optimizer, because the contribution is relative to it. Muon is the optimizer of choice for many LLM training runs these days. For each hidden weight matrix it maintains a single momentum buffer and then approximately orthogonalizes the update direction using a few Newton-Schulz iterations, which pushes the update toward a direction that is as close to orthogonal as the matrix structure allows. That orthogonalization is what makes Muon different from AdamW on these problems, and it is also why you cannot treat the momentum term as a disposable extra. The buffer is not just a memory cost, it is part of what gives the update its shape. So the question is not simply can you delete a buffer, it is can you delete a buffer that the optimizer is structurally relying on, and replace it with something that already exists elsewhere in the system.

The method, Muon-RT. You do not just drop the momentum buffer and use the raw displacement, because Muon orthogonalizes its updates and the displacement has structure that would get distorted if you mixed it in naively. The paper does three things. First it removes the radial component of the displacement, the part that is parallel to the student's current weights, because that component does not carry useful directional information for the orthogonalized update. Second it normalizes the remainder spectrally, so the reused history has a controlled magnitude rather than dominating or vanishing relative to the gradient. Third it mixes the processed displacement with the current gradient and then runs the standard Newton-Schulz orthogonalization iterations. The ordering matters. The paper notes that mixing the displacement with the gradient before Newton-Schulz is wrong, because Newton-Schulz can amplify weak singular directions, and a small contaminated component would get amplified. So the displacement is prepared, bounded, and only then combined.

The theory is a convergence statement. Under explicit regularity and alignment assumptions, the paper establishes linear contraction of an expected objective-gap bound toward a residual error level. That is the standard form for these results, a contractive bound with a floor, and the residual reflects the fact that you have replaced a true momentum with a proxy. The paper also gives a corollary that interprets when the reuse actually helps, namely when the teacher branch raises the effective descent coefficient, so the mechanism is not hand-waved.

The numbers are from two self-distillation settings. With SDFT on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Muon-RT improves average accuracy over AdamW by two point six points, while using forty-two percent less persistent memory. Against momentum Muon, the normal Muon that keeps the buffer, it improves average accuracy by five point four points while using twenty-seven percent less persistent memory. The persistent memory figures are the concrete ones. Momentum Muon needs one hundred and three point two gigabytes of GPU memory for persistent state, AdamW needs one hundred and twenty-nine point zero, and Muon-RT needs seventy-five point four. That is the headline, a seventy-five point four gigabyte persistent footprint for the same seven billion model, against one hundred and three for the momentum variant. With SDPO on Olmo-3-7B-Instruct, Muon-RT improves average accuracy over AdamW by two point zero points. The gain over AdamW breaks down by task, with the larger gain on tool use and a smaller one on science in the SDFT setting.

A skeptical read. The result is exactly the kind of small, well-scoped optimizer contribution that is easy to reproduce and low risk, and the memory arithmetic is the part I would trust most, because persistent state size is a hard number you can verify by counting buffers. The accuracy gain is the part I would treat with more care. Two and a half point average improvements on two datasets, tool use and science, is a modest result and it is measured on a single model family and a couple of self-distillation recipes, so I would want to see it hold on a different base and a broader eval before I changed my optimizer choice. The convergence proof is under alignment assumptions, and the residual floor in the bound is the honest admission that the displacement is a proxy, not a replacement in the strict sense. The practical applicability is also narrower than it looks. The paper is explicit that Muon-RT only helps when a parameter-aligned EMA teacher already exists, which is true for SDFT and SDPO, but if your method has no such teacher, keeping one purely to replace momentum would not save any memory, so the win is specific to the self-distillation regime. There is also a subtlety in what gets removed. The buffer is dropped for the hidden weight matrices, which are the large ones and the ones that dominate the persistent footprint, so the memory savings scale with model size in the direction that matters for a seven billion or larger run. The embedding and output layers, which are handled differently by Muon anyway, are not the source of the savings. That is a useful framing if you are doing your own memory budget, because it tells you where the win actually comes from rather than just reporting a percentage.

For a team already running self-distillation with an EMA teacher, though, this is a free lunch, drop the buffer, keep the teacher you already have, get less memory and a small accuracy bump, and there is little to lose by trying it.

The bottom line. Muon-RT reuses the EMA teacher's trajectory as a momentum surrogate, cutting persistent memory by up to forty-two percent over AdamW and improving self-distillation accuracy on top. It is a low-risk, easy-to-verify win, but it only applies where an EMA teacher is already part of the training loop.
