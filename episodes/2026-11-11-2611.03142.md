The paper is called DepthGraft: Structural Regularization through Hierarchical Cross-Layer KV Reconstruction. The first three authors are Xiaohan Qin, Xiangdong Zhang, and Yu Wang, with the work spanning the School of AI at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, the Shanghai Innovation Institute, and Dots Studio at Xiaohongshu.

Let me set up the problem, because it is a subtle one that a lot of people who scale language models hit but do not name. When you make a transformer deeper, you expect it to get stronger, and usually it does. But in the modern Pre-Layer-Normalization design, there is a failure mode called depth-wise information dilution. The residual stream is a single hidden-state trajectory, and it aggregates every preceding computation through fixed additive updates. As the stack gets deeper, the features that early layers formed can get progressively diluted and become hard for the later layers to retrieve. So the extra layers are there, but they are not using the earlier representations efficiently. Adding depth is less productive than the representational capacity would suggest.

The standard fix in recent work is cross-layer connectivity: instead of forcing all the depth history through the residual stream, you let deep layers read earlier representations directly. Attention Residuals reweight the preceding hidden states to replace the fixed residual accumulation, and Mixture-of-Depths Attention augments attention with key and value pairs from previous layers. These clearly work. But they cost something: dense cross-layer access introduces extra computation, extra memory traffic, and more systems complexity, and you have to do careful performance versus efficiency engineering to keep it from hurting.

Now here is the reframe that this paper is really about. Cross-layer key-value sharing, which people have mostly treated as a cache-compression and inference-speed trick, can also be read as a form of that same cross-layer connectivity. It gives a deep layer a channel that bypasses the residual stream's dilution by giving it direct access to a shallow layer's memory, and the gradients flow from the deep layer back through the shared key and value to the shallow layer's projections. So a deep layer is effectively telling the shallow layer's key and value projections what it needs. The authors' central claim is that this makes key-value sharing an implicit structural regularizer for the geometry of the key and value representations during pretraining. That is a genuinely different lens than the cache-compression one, and it is the lens they use to design the architecture.

Their analysis, using effective rank as a measure of representation dimensionality, says the following. When a source layer's key and value is reused by a distant deep layer, that source key and value receives training signal from multiple different query spaces. This reshapes the geometry in an asymmetric way: the keys are encouraged to concentrate around shared discriminative retrieval directions, while the values are pushed to cover a richer content subspace. In plain terms, the sharing makes retrieval sharper and the content channel more expressive. And the effect is governed by the sharing topology, which is the design knob. The existing designs both get this wrong in complementary ways. Adjacent-layer sharing, where a group of consecutive layers reuses the first layer's key and value, provides too little separation between query spaces, so the regularization is weak. Global-anchor sharing, where one anchor layer serves all the deeper layers, concentrates heterogeneous deep-layer gradients on just a few sources, so those sources get a muddled signal. Neither fully exploits the effect.

So DepthGraft is a hierarchical design derived from that analysis. It partitions the network into shallow, middle, and deep stages. The shallow and middle stages compute their key and value normally. Each deep layer does not compute its own independent key and value; instead it reconstructs its key and value from a stride-aligned pair, one shallow source and one middle source, using learnable channel-wise coefficients. This does three things at once. It creates non-local source-and-reuse connections with enough query-space separation to get the regularization. It distributes the cross-layer gradients across many source layers instead of piling them onto a couple of global anchors. And it gives each deep layer access to memory at two levels of abstraction. That two-level access is worth pausing on, because it is the specific structural property that separates this from the earlier designs. A deep layer can pull on the shallow memory, which carries lower-level features, and the middle memory, which carries more processed ones, and the channel-wise coefficients let it learn how to blend them. The earlier designs, adjacent or global-anchor, give each deep layer a single source or a single anchor, so they cannot represent that blend, which is part of why their regularization is incomplete.

The efficiency benefits of key-value sharing are preserved. Because the deep layers no longer materialize their own independent key and value, the deep stage removes that work entirely. You can finish building the key-value cache before the final stage during prefill, which is prefill early completion, and you cut the key-value cache by roughly a third. The stride-aligned source assignment is also friendly to pipeline and virtual-pipeline parallelism schedules, which is what you want if you are actually going to use this at scale. The stride alignment is a deliberate engineering choice, not a cosmetic one: it lines up which layers serve as sources with the parallelism boundaries, so the reconstruction does not force extra cross-device communication that would erase the cache savings.

The results are where I would want to look first, and they are solid. They validate on both dense and mixture-of-experts models from half a billion to sixteen billion parameters, and the gains are consistent under both grouped-query attention and multi-head latent attention, which matters because those are the two attention variants you actually deploy. On the sixteen billion mixture-of-experts model, DepthGraft gets a seven point zero percent absolute improvement on MMLU-Pro, along with six point six percent on CMMLU and six point four percent on C3. And that is while reducing prefill time and key-value cache usage by about a third, with almost no loss in decoding speed. A seven point improvement on MMLU-Pro at the largest scale, on top of a one-third cache reduction, is a strong combined result, not a free lunch where you trade one thing for another.

Now the skeptical pass, because I want to be fair about what this does and does not show. First, the gains are reported at training time on models they trained themselves, from zero point five to sixteen billion parameters. That is a real validation across scales, but it is on their training setups and data mixtures, not a drop-in to an existing frontier model. The claim that this is a scalable design for large-scale pretraining is supported by the trend across their scales, but I would want to see it on a larger, more mature pretraining run before I treat the absolute numbers as portable. Second, the theoretical analysis is elegant, but it is the kind of result where the geometry argument is suggestive rather than a tight bound, and the actual performance depends on the specific stride-aligned topology they chose. The topology matters a lot, and they have tested adjacent and global-anchor baselines, not a broad sweep of alternatives, so it is possible the exact hierarchical stride design is doing more work than the general "distribute the gradients" story implies. Third, there is a practical systems cost that the abstract undersells: reconstructing deep-layer key and value with learnable channel-wise coefficients is more moving parts than a plain cache-compression scheme, and the fact that they note it is pipeline-parallelism-friendly is a good sign, but the engineering burden is real.

Here is my bottom line. This is a clean, well-motivated architectural paper whose core reframe, that key-value sharing is a cross-layer gradient regularizer and not just a cache trick, is the most interesting idea, and it is backed by consistent gains across dense and mixture-of-experts scales while cutting the key-value cache by a third. The main caveats are that the results come from their own training runs rather than a drop-in to a deployed frontier model, that the exact hierarchical topology is doing substantial of the work, and that the engineering complexity is real. But as a design for large-scale pretraining, the direction is sound and the numbers are hard to ignore.
