EVAThink: Evidence Sufficiency-Guided Training for Adaptive Thinking in Video Understanding, by Changti Wu, Ruizhe Fan, and Bin Wang, from East China Normal University, Zhongguancun Academy, and Huawei, is a reinforcement-learning method for teaching a vision-language model to decide, per video question, whether to answer directly or to reason, guided by how much question-relevant evidence the sampled frames actually contain. The central result is that trained on only six thousand seven hundred curated samples, the model improves over its base model across six video understanding benchmarks at both thirty-two and sixty-four frame budgets, and its adaptive policy beats both the always-answer and always-reason policies by two point three and three point six percentage points respectively, while cutting average output length by about sixty-two percent compared to always reasoning.

The problem is a practical one for any video model that runs on a limited visual budget. In practice these models do not see the whole video, they see a uniformly sampled sequence of frames, so their answers depend on whether the question-relevant information is preserved in those frames. The authors call that information frame evidence, which includes visual cues inside individual frames and temporal relationships across frames. With a fixed budget, some sampled sequences carry enough evidence for a reliable direct answer, while others only sparsely cover the relevant content, especially when that content occupies a small fraction of the video. The key quantity they introduce is evidence sufficiency, the extent to which the frame evidence supports a reliable direct answer. Explicit reasoning can help connect cues across frames when evidence is sparse, but when the evidence already supports a direct answer, extra reasoning buys little while adding generation cost and the risk of unsupported inferences. So the natural strategy is adaptive thinking, answer directly when the evidence is sufficient, reason when it is not, and the question is how to train that decision.

Prior work on adaptive reasoning for video selects the response mode using the model's confidence in an initial answer, or using objectives that balance correctness and grounding against generation cost. Those are reasonable signals for a single input, but the authors point out that supervision across different evidence conditions is underexplored, and that learning a mode policy is also a training-stability problem. Prior studies report that adaptive reasoning training can prematurely favor one mode and collapse into always-answering or always-reasoning, and many existing methods need large training sets, on the order of eighty-three thousand to one hundred forty thousand samples, and some need a supervised fine-tuning cold start, which adds the burden of preparing response demonstrations.

EVAThink's approach has two parts. The first is a data curation step they call Recoverable Evidence Gap Mining. It constructs contrastive pairs of an evidence-dense view and an evidence-sparse view that share the same question and answer under an identical frame budget, so the two views differ only in how much of the question-relevant segment is covered by the sampled frames. The starting point is a candidate pool of about one hundred eighteen thousand multiple-choice video-question samples drawn from eight existing datasets, grouped into general, reasoning-oriented, and perception-oriented video understanding. For each sample, the model is given thirty-two uniformly sampled frames and its direct-answer accuracy is estimated using eight rollouts. Samples answered correctly in all eight rollouts are discarded as too easy. For the rest, the dense and sparse views are built differently depending on the baseline accuracy: for hard samples, the original video is the sparse view and the dense view is a crop around an evidence segment that a stronger model annotates, extended by two or three frames before and after; for medium samples, the original is the dense view and the sparse view is built by concatenating another video from the same source. Then two filters keep only pairs where the direct-answer accuracy drops from dense to sparse by more than one-eighth, and where explicit reasoning on the sparse view improves accuracy by more than one-eighth. That second condition is what makes the gap recoverable, it links a drop in direct accuracy to a genuine gain from reasoning, which is exactly the supervision you want for teaching when to think.

The second part is the training, which they call Evidence-Guided Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning, built on GRPO with no supervised cold start. A training prompt instructs the model to answer directly when the evidence clearly supports an answer and to reason otherwise, using two distinct response formats with different openings, a direct-answer format and a think format, and those distinct openings are what enable the stability mechanism. Stage one trains the mode selection using high-confidence contrastive pairs, with a stage-specific reward that gives two points for a correct answer in the preferred mode, one point for a correct answer in the other mode, zero for an incorrect answer in the preferred mode, and minus one for an incorrect answer in the other mode. Stage two removes the view-dependent mode preference and adds a small bonus for correct direct answers to push efficiency. The stability mechanism is first-token advantage regularization, which discourages the policy from concentrating on a single mode too early and mitigates mode collapse. They also sample the dense and sparse views of each pair into the same training batch to keep the evidence conditions balanced.

Now the skeptical part, which is where I would start before trusting this. First, the whole method hinges on the evidence-sufficiency proxy, which is the difference in direct-answer accuracy between the dense and sparse views, measured with eight rollouts. Eight rollouts is a small number for estimating an accuracy to one-eighth granularity, and the curation threshold of one-eighth is close to the resolution of that estimate, so a nontrivial fraction of the retained pairs are likely to be right at the boundary and only weakly supported. I would want to know the stability of the retained pairs under a re-rollout, because if a meaningful share flip when you re-estimate, the supervision signal is noisier than it looks and the mode policy is being fit to estimation noise.

Second, the contrastive construction is doing a lot of the heavy lifting, and it is not a natural distribution. Cropping around an annotated evidence segment and concatenating another video are artificial ways to make a sparse view, and the question is whether the model is learning a general principle, reason when evidence is scarce, or a shortcut tied to the visual signatures of those two operations, a tight crop or a spliced video. The adaptive policy beating direct-only and think-only is the key claim, but those baselines are mode-fixed, not evidence-conditioned, so the win is partly against a weaker, non-adaptive comparator. I would want an ablation where a strong baseline is also allowed to adapt its mode from its own confidence, to see whether the evidence-contrastive supervision beats a confidence-based baseline on the same test set.

Third, the training set is small, six point seven thousand samples, which is a real strength for efficiency, but it is drawn from eight multiple-choice datasets, and the adaptive-thinking result is reported across video understanding benchmarks that are mostly multiple-choice question answering. The mode decision is essentially a routing decision over a binary answer space, and the gains may not transfer to open-ended video captioning or temporal grounding, which the related-work section names but the results do not stress. I would want to see the length reduction and the accuracy gain hold on an open-answer task before I treated adaptive thinking as a general video-understanding capability rather than a multiple-choice trick.

The engineering gotchas are worth naming. The curation pipeline is as expensive as the training: for each candidate you run eight direct-answer rollouts on two views plus reasoning rollouts, so the one hundred eighteen thousand pool requires a large inference budget before you have your six point seven thousand pairs. The mode is selected by the first token, which means the two response formats have to stay reliably distinct at inference or the first-token regularization loses its signal, and any drift in how the model opens a response can quietly break the mode routing. And the two-stage reward schedule is tuned, the bonuses of two and one and one point one, and the threshold of zero point eight five for the preferred mode, so reproducing the result requires those exact numbers rather than a free choice.

The bottom line is this: tying adaptive thinking to a measured evidence-sufficiency gap, rather than to the model's own confidence, is a clean and genuinely useful supervision signal, and getting it to work on only six point seven thousand samples without a supervised cold start is a strong efficiency result that should lower the bar for adaptive-reasoning methods in video. But the sufficiency proxy is estimated from few rollouts and the contrastive construction is artificial, so I would verify that the evidence-gap supervision generalizes beyond the multiple-choice setting and beats a confidence-based adaptive baseline before I adopted it as a default for my video model.
