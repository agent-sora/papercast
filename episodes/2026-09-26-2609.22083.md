MintAct: A Unified Visual Agent for Digital Environments, by Mingfei Gao, Rui
Tian, Haiming Gang, and a long co-author list, from Apple, where the team works
on general-purpose models that operate digital devices directly from raw pixels.

The pitch is a single vision-language model that does three jobs at once: UI
grounding, multi-step navigation across mobile, desktop, and web, and visual
tool use, at three scales, two billion, four billion, and eight billion
parameters. The claim is that one model, trained with a carefully designed
multi-stage recipe, matches per-domain specialists across all of those
capabilities simultaneously, rather than needing a different model for each
screen type. That is the unification, and it is the thing worth testing, because
the usual failure mode of unification is that averaging several tasks together
leaves the model mediocre at all of them.

The training recipe is the real content here, and it has four stages that each
target a different gap. The first is a high-resolution single-step supervised
stage that teaches basic grounding, the ability to look at a screenshot and put
a box or a point where a UI element is. The second is a low-resolution
multi-step supervised stage over trajectories generated across all the domains,
which is where the model learns to chain actions. The third is a per-domain
reinforcement-learning specialist stage, where they train a separate specialist
for each domain and then distill those specialists back into a single model
through rejection sampling. The fourth, and the one the paper leans on hardest,
is a joint agentic reinforcement-learning stage where the model interacts
directly with the live environments and learns from the outcomes of its own
actions. Each stage, they note, shows an ablation contribution, so none of them
is decorative.

The infrastructure is what makes that fourth stage feasible, and it is worth
describing because it is the part that usually breaks in practice. The
environments are slow, unreliable, and produce long multimodal trajectories,
which is a hostile setting for synchronous rollout. Their answer is an
asynchronous reinforcement-learning framework that decouples rollout generation
from policy optimization and keeps explicit control over the cross-domain
training distribution, so that the mix of mobile, desktop, and web tasks stays
balanced even as individual rollouts take wildly different amounts of time. The
design has two properties I would flag as the non-obvious parts. The first is
that it stays stable under noisy environment feedback, which in practice means
that a flaky backend returning a wrong reward, or a timeout that looks like a
failure, does not poison the policy update the way it would in a synchronous
loop where every gradient step waits on every environment. The second is that it
controls for off-policy drift, the situation where a rollout was generated by an
older version of the policy than the one you are now updating, which is
inseparable from asynchrony once rollouts are expensive. On the environment side
they host more than two hundred concurrent instances, deploying desktop
environments on a Linux cluster with each instance getting ten CPU cores and
forty gigabytes of memory, and they use a computer environment built on the
public OSWorld codebase. Complementary to the real backends, they also build
synthetic mobile and desktop operating-system environments that the agents can
interact with at high throughput, which is how they get enough on-policy signal
to train without paying the full cost of a live device for every rollout. The
actions are grounded in pixel coordinates normalized to a 999 by 999 grid, which
is a standard trick to make the action space resolution-independent across screen
sizes, and each domain exposes its own action tokens, so the model has to learn
the right vocabulary per platform while sharing one underlying policy. The whole
point of the asynchronous design is fault tolerance: if one environment hangs or
drops, the training loop keeps moving on the rest, and that fault tolerance is
also what lets them run the scale of rollout needed for the joint stage.

The headline results are the numbers I would come back to. The eight-billion
model reaches 48.9 on OSWorld-Verified, 39.1 on Online-Mind2Web, and 67.0 on
AndroidWorld. These three benchmarks are not interchangeable, which is the point
of reporting all three rather than one: OSWorld-Verified is a curated subset of
desktop tasks that were validated to be actually solvable, Online-Mind2Web
measures live web navigation, and AndroidWorld measures multi-step mobile
interaction, so the three together cover the desktop, web, and mobile surface
the model is supposed to unify. They report that across all three scales the
unified models match or exceed per-domain specialists of similar size on
grounding, navigation, and visual tool use at the same time, and are competitive
with the best public models at comparable sizes. One detail that makes the data
pipeline credible is how the tasks are generated in the loop: they prompt a
computer-use specialist model, in this case a 32B model, to roll out the current
tasks in the environment using the action space, and then feed those collected
rollouts to another strong vision-language model that proposes new tasks grounded
in what the rollouts revealed about the environment. That is a self-bootstrapping
task curriculum, and it is what lets them keep the cross-domain distribution
fresh without hand-authoring an ever-growing task set. The last phrase,
competitive with the best public models, is doing a lot of work, because it is
the comparison that determines whether unification is a win or a wash, and a
reader who is deciding whether to adopt a unified agent will care more about that
relative comparison than about the absolute score on any single benchmark.

Now the skeptical read. The unification claim is the thing to press, and the
honest version of it is that they match specialists, not beat them by a wide
margin, at least in the reported numbers. Matching across four or five different
benchmarks with one model is impressive engineering, but the interesting
question for a deployment is whether the unified model's weaknesses are
concentrated, and a single aggregate score per benchmark hides where it is
fragile. The benchmark composition is also worth noting: OSWorld-Verified is a
validated subset, which is a good sign, but it is a smaller and cleaner
distribution than the full OSWorld, so a model can look strong on the verified
slice while being less reliable on the harder, unverified tail of desktop tasks
that a real user would hit. The other thing I would want to see reported is a
per-domain breakdown, so the reader can tell whether the unified model's losses
are spread evenly or concentrated in one of the three surfaces. The asynchronous reinforcement-learning framework is the most
reusable contribution, but it is also the least self-contained: it only helps
if you have the environment infrastructure to feed it, and the two-hundred-
instance, forty-gigabyte-per-instance setup is a substantial resource commitment
that a small team reproducing this would not have. There is also the distillation
step, where per-domain specialists are folded back into one model by rejection
sampling, and that is a stage where quality can silently leak, because the
distilled model can only be as good as the best of the specialists it is
sampling from, and worse in the corners where the specialists disagreed. And the
action space, normalized to 999 by 999, is a convenience that can cost accuracy
on very high-resolution desktop screens where sub-element precision matters.

Bottom line: this is a solid, well-engineered unification result, with a
four-stage recipe that is explicitly ablated and an asynchronous reinforcement-
learning framework that is the most transferable piece, and headline numbers
that show one model matching per-domain specialists across mobile, desktop, web,
and tool use at the same time. The open question is whether that match holds up as a comfortable
margin at scale, and whether the environment infrastructure cost to run the
online stage is low enough that the unification is worth it over just keeping
separate specialized models for each screen type.
