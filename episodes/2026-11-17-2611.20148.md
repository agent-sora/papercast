Today's paper is Centered Ablation: Why Last-Layer Directional Ablation Under-Fires or Collapses, and a Closed-Form Fix, from Quy-Anh Dang and Chris Ngo, at the Knovel Engineering Lab in Singapore. The subject is one of the most reused tricks in interpretability — directional ablation — and the paper's claim is that the standard recipe is erratic for a specific, fixable mathematical reason, and the fix is a closed-form projection you can compute before you ever generate a token.

Start with what directional ablation is, because the whole paper hinges on the last-layer version. The recipe: estimate a direction in hidden space as the difference between the mean activations of two classes of prompts — say, prompts that elicit refusals and prompts that do not — and then, while the model runs, remove each hidden state's component along that direction. Remove the refusal direction and a chat model complies with harmful requests; add it back and the model refuses more. It is cheap, it is gradient-free, and it is widely reused. The problem, documented in the paper and in the prior literature's scattered observations: on some models the recipe barely fires — the paper cites a 3 billion parameter Llama that, after its refusal direction is ablated, still opens 86 percent of its answers to harmful requests with a refusal. On others it fires everywhere, and the text collapses into repetition — the paper's own Gemma-2-9B run collapses on 50 percent of cases before the fix. The literature has treated these as model-specific quirks. This paper says they are the same phenomenon.

The key result is an exact reduction. At the last residual stream, ablating along a direction is exactly a logit bias: a fixed vocabulary footprint — the unembedding matrix times the direction — scaled by a gate, and the gate is the cosine between the current hidden state and the direction. So the entire effect of the ablation on the output distribution is one number, the cosine gate, multiplying one fixed vector of logit shifts. That is the clean statement of what is happening, and it immediately exposes the bug. The gate is measured from the origin — the cosine is the dot product divided by the norm of the state — but last-layer hidden states share a large common mode, a mean direction that most states point close to, and that common mode sits far from the origin. A behavior direction that has any overlap with the common mode therefore fires on benign text, where the state points along the common mode, and under-fires on its actual targets, where the relevant variation is the small deviation away from the common mode. The gate is measuring the wrong thing.

The fix, Centered Ablation, projects the common mode out of the direction, in closed form. You take the behavior direction, remove its component along the mean of the last-layer states, and use the residual direction. The projection is a one-line computation on pre-generable quantities: you compute it once per model, before any generation, and the modified ablation then fires on the deviations that carry the behavior, not on the common mode that every token has. The paper also gives two predictive numbers — an overlap measure and an efficacy measure, both computable before generation — that tell you, on a model the analysis has never seen, which regime you are in. The overlap predicts how many benign answers the ablation preserves, with a Spearman correlation of 0.98 across the eight models tested. The efficacy of the direction predicts the attack-success gain, with a Pearson correlation of minus 0.95, and the two measures are not interchangeable: the overlap barely orders the attack-success gain. There is also a collapse index that flags when the text is degenerating before it happens.

The results across the eight models. Where the standard recipe under-fires, Centered Ablation raises the measured effect by 29 and 46 points — two models with low efficacy gain plus 29.0 and plus 46.3 points of attack success. On Gemma-2-9B, the collapse rate drops from 50 percent to 1 percent. On the high-overlap models, the centered version preserves 12 to 40 more benign answers than the standard recipe, and 39 to 46 more than the affine offset correction from the prior work. And there is a result I think is underrated: two standard safety judges score collapsed text in opposite directions. When the text degenerates into repetition, one judge reads it as compliant and the other as refusing, and the disagreement between the two can fabricate a 46-point effect — meaning that some of the reported effects in the existing literature on activation steering may be measurement artifacts of collapsed text being judged inconsistently.

Skeptical assessment. First, the scope of the reduction. The exact logit-bias reduction holds at the last residual stream, which is where directional ablation is usually applied, but it is a last-layer statement. If you ablate at earlier layers, the reduction does not hold in this form, and the common-mode argument — that the mean state direction dominates the cosine — is empirically a last-layer phenomenon. The paper is precise about this, but it means the fix is scoped to the standard usage, not to arbitrary activation editing. Second, the eight-model sample is small, and the predictive claims — that overlap and efficacy, computed on a held-out model, tell you the regime — rest on eight data points. A Spearman of 0.98 on eight points is a strong fit but a thin one, and I would want the same two numbers computed on 30 models, across families, before treating the regime prediction as a practical tool rather than an encouraging pattern. Third, the attack-success framing. The paper evaluates the ablation both as a faithfulness intervention — preserving benign behavior while steering — and as an attack-success measure, and the dual use is honest about what directional ablation does in practice: it is the same operation that makes a model comply with harmful requests. The centered version is more predictable in both directions, which is good for the safety-evaluation use and bad for the evasion use, and I would note that the collapse-index and the two-regime prediction make the attack side more reliable, not less. Fourth, the judge disagreement result is the paper's most transferable finding, but it is a warning about the measurement stack more than a result about the models, and it generalizes to any evaluation where a model judge reads degenerate text.

A practical note on how to read the paper's framing of directional ablation as attack versus defense. The same projection that lets a safety researcher preserve benign behavior while steering a refusal direction is the projection that makes it easier to reliably suppress a refusal, and the paper is unusually explicit about this dual use. The efficacy and overlap measures are, in effect, a pre-flight check for both purposes: they tell you how much an intervention will do and what it will cost before you commit compute to a single run. That is a genuine efficiency win for the safety-evaluation workflow, and it is also a lowering of the cost of the evasion workflow, and the paper's contribution is honest about both sides rather than pretending the second does not exist. For a lab using this internally, the regime prediction changes the experimental design: you stop running the full ablation sweep on every model and instead compute the two numbers first, and you only run the sweep on the models the numbers say are in the interesting regime.

Engineering notes if you use activation steering. Compute the common mode — the mean of last-layer states on your evaluation prompts — before you compute your behavior direction, and project. It is one matrix product. Compute the overlap and efficacy before you run the intervention, because they tell you whether you are in the under-firing regime or the collapse regime, and those regimes need different follow-up. And if you are judging steered outputs with a model judge, run two judges and check their agreement on the collapsed subset specifically, because a 46-point phantom effect is exactly what the disagreement produces.

Bottom line: Centered Ablation is a small, exact, closed-form fix for a widely used technique whose failure modes were previously treated as model quirks, and the pre-generable predictive numbers are a genuinely useful addition to the interpretability toolbox. I would trust the exact reduction and the collapse fix, and I would want the regime prediction validated on a larger model panel and the judge-disagreement finding replicated on your own evaluation stack before either of them drives a deployment decision.
