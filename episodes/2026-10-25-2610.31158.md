Beyond Watching and Searching: Benchmarking Agents for Video-centered Deep Research Reports, by Jiahao Meng, Qize Yang, and Yuxuan Wang, from Peking University and Alibaba's Token Hub.

The deep research agent genre has produced a lot of text-based report generation work, where the agent is given a topic, searches the open web, and synthesizes a long report. This paper identifies a gap in that line of work: what happens when the research is centered on a source video, not a text topic? A video tutorial on a repair procedure, a product review, a news broadcast, a lecture. The viewer's needs are different. They want to locate relevant moments, verify claims made in the video, find external evidence that supports or contradicts the video's statements, reproduce a demonstrated procedure, and make a decision informed by both the video content and outside knowledge. The task the paper formalizes is Video-Centered Deep Research Report Generation. Given a video and a user request, the agent must identify the research questions the video raises, retrieve external multimodal evidence, and produce an interactive report that is linked back to specific video moments.

The benchmark, OmniDR-Bench, contains 50 tasks in Chinese and English across 19 real-world subcategories. The tasks span video families that differ in what kind of research they demand: tutorials and procedure demonstrations, where the user wants to reproduce a demonstrated action and verify the claims made while demonstrating it; reviews and advertisements, where the user wants external evidence about the product or claim; and informational content, where the user wants background, context, or counterpoints to what the video states. The bilingual composition is a deliberate design choice, because the deep-research agent literature has been almost entirely evaluated on English, and the video-grounding failure modes can differ across languages when the retrieval evidence is predominantly in one language. Each task package includes the source video, the user request, the rubric tree, and the audited reference evidence, so the evaluation is fully reproducible offline without re-running any live retrieval. The evaluation methodology is the more interesting contribution. They propose Omni Atomic Rubric Trees, or Omni-ARTs. Each task gets a rubric tree with three dimensions, 23 shared directions, and task-specific atomic binary criteria. The three dimensions are audiovisual grounding, which tests whether the agent actually looked at and heard the video correctly; multimodal evidence use, which tests whether the agent retrieved and integrated external information; and interactive presentation, which tests whether the report is structured, linked, and navigable. The atomic criteria are binary pass or fail, so scoring is not a single holistic judgment but a decomposition into hundreds of checkable claims. They count 6,414 criteria across the 50 tasks. The rubric construction process is called Audited Rubric Construction, or ARC, and it involves independent evidence collection, automated checks, behavioral calibration, and iterative human review. The inter-rater agreement on comparable decisions is 93.0 percent and 91.0 percent for the two agreement rates they report, which is high for rubric-based evaluation.

They evaluate 18 agent configurations, which is a cross-product of models, harnesses, and skill configurations. The best system scores 63.3 out of 100. That number is worth sitting with. On text-based deep research benchmarks, frontier agents often score in the high seventies or low eighties. Dropping to 63.3 when the research must be grounded in a video is a substantial drop, and it tells you that the video-grounding requirement is not a marginal difficulty bump. The dimensional breakdown explains where the points are lost. Presentation averages 81.8 points, which means the agents can produce well-structured, navigable reports. Content and citation average only 45.4 and 47.8 points respectively. The agents can format a report beautifully but struggle to fill it with correctly grounded, properly cited content. That is a telling asymmetry for anyone building these systems: the presentation layer is largely solved, the content layer is not. There is a completion dimension behind the headline numbers that is worth separating out. Some configurations do not finish the task at all. Qwen3.8-Max scores 60.1 on the reports it does complete, but an 88.0 percent completion rate drags its overall score down, while GLM-5.3 completes every task and scores 62.0. The 1.3 point gap between the two has a task-bootstrap 95 percent confidence interval that spans zero, from negative 4.0 to positive 5.6, so the paper is explicit that their relative order is statistically uncertain. Reliability, in the sense of finishing the task, is a first-order component of the score that a single average score hides.

The failure analysis is detailed. Across the 18 configurations, the direct-video-fact pass rate is 79.9 percent, meaning agents can extract facts stated in the video reasonably well. The pass rates collapse for harder categories: 8.7 percent for external evidence integration, 8.8 percent for cross-source verification, and 5.0 percent for multi-fact composition. Fact coverage, causal analysis, and comparison average 7.6, 9.5, and 4.6 percent. Video faithfulness averages 92.4 percent, but logical consistency and claim support reach only 21.2 and 15.9 percent. In other words, the agents quote the video accurately but fail to reason across the video and the retrieved evidence. A concrete example from the paper: under the Claude Code harness, GPT-5.6 Sol passes 98.0 percent of source-count criteria and 93.5 percent of multimodal-material criteria, but only 23.9 percent of claim-support criteria. The agent finds the sources and includes the material, but does not actually support the claims it makes with them.

The skill-based workflow they propose decomposes the task into atomic skills that can be orchestrated. Full orchestration adds 7.3 points over running all atomic skills independently, with the gain concentrated in citation quality: a 20.7 point improvement in citation points while content rises only 0.8 points. Multimodal retrieval adds 1.1 points overall, with a 5.2 point gain on the hardest tasks. The orchestration result is the practically useful finding: if you are building a video research agent, the marginal value of a sophisticated orchestration layer is real but modest, and it is almost entirely in citation management. The paper also analyzes the research trajectories themselves: under the Claude Code harness, the median fraction of the report written before the first post-write search ranges from 33.3 percent for GPT-5.6 Sol to 61.5 percent for Claude Opus 5, and GLM-5.3 reaches 54.2 percent with a median of 15.5 write blocks. The pattern across configurations is that agents tend to draft a large fraction of the report before doing the retrieval that should support it, which is consistent with the citation failure mode: the report is written first and the evidence is fitted after, so unsupported claims are not surprising. The video categories span only 2.0 points, from 52.8 to 54.8, suggesting the difficulty is not strongly category-dependent. Query types show more variation: the three query families average 41.3, 52.5, and 50.4 points.

The skeptical read: 50 tasks is small for a benchmark, and the 19 subcategories mean some have only two or three tasks each, so per-category numbers are noisy. The paper acknowledges this: the category differences are labeled descriptive because of small sample sizes. The 18 configurations are all frontier models with standard agent harnesses; there is no ablation of the video-perception component, so you cannot separate whether the failure is in the vision model's understanding of the video or in the reasoning pipeline downstream. The rubric trees, while high-agreement, are authored by the benchmark team, so there is a risk that the atomic criteria encode the authors' notion of what a good report looks like rather than a ground-truth user need. The 63.3 best score is a snapshot of current frontier capability; as video-language models improve, the content and citation dimensions may close faster than the grounding dimension. For practitioners, the most actionable output is the dimensional decomposition: build your presentation layer to the 82-point standard, and spend your research budget on the evidence-integration and claim-support dimensions, which are at single digits. The code and data are released at the GitHub repository linked in the paper.
