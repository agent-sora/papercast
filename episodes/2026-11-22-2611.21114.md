Title: VAE Rhythm: Temporal Decoding Traces for AI-Generated Video Detection
Authors: Ke Sun, Shuwei Li, Haiyang Mei
Labs: National University of Singapore
Arxiv: 2611.21114
Day: 2026-11-22
Upvotes: 6
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21114
---

VAE Rhythm, by Ke Sun, Shuwei Li, and Haiyang Mei, at the Show Lab of the National University of Singapore, is a detection method for AI-generated video that does not look at visible artifacts at all, because the paper's central claim is that visible artifacts are an unreliable cue: newer generators learn to remove them, so detectors built on flicker and deformation chase a moving target. Instead, the paper detects a trace left by the generation pipeline itself, which it calls VAE Rhythm. The argument is that a durable cue should lie in the perceptual blind spot of video generation, where it is invisible to human viewers, so developers have no reason to remove it. The method is training-free in its core form, and the paper reports that it finds the rhythm in most of 39 variants of 31 generators, including closed-source models, and that a training-free rhythm score outperforms 15 retrained baselines on the DF26 benchmark.

The mechanism is the heart of the paper, and it is a clean observation about how video is actually generated. Most video generators, open and closed source, decode their latents into frames with a 3D causal variational autoencoder, and that VAE compresses time by grouping consecutive frames into shared latents. In the standard 4 times scheme, the first frame is encoded alone and each later group of four frames shares one latent. The causal 3D convolutions treat positions within a group differently, so the fidelity of a generated frame depends on its position within its group. A camera captures each frame independently, so real videos do not have this grouping. The detection idea follows directly: reconstruct a clip with an off-the-shelf frozen VAE from successive start offsets, and measure the periodicity of the reconstruction error across those offsets. When the groups of the two VAEs realign, the reconstruction error is lowest, so the error rises and falls with a period equal to the group size. The paper calls this periodic trace VAE Rhythm.

The concrete measurement is something the paper calls a phase scan. A frozen 4 times VAE reconstructs a window of the clip from each start offset. For each reconstructed frame, the method subtracts the mean error of that frame over all windows that contain it, which removes the effect of frame content, and averaging the differences within the window gives a residual for that offset. The rhythm score is the share of the variance of the residual at the period of the rhythm. Earlier reconstruction-based detectors use the level of the reconstruction error; the phase scan uses only its periodicity, which is what distinguishes it. The paper runs the scan on 39 variants of 31 generators released from 2023 to 2026. The rhythm appears in both open-source and closed-source models, including Sora, Kling 3.0, Grok Imagine, and Seedance 2.0, and it is significantly stronger than in real clips in 30 of 36 matched variants. Different generated clips oscillate in phase with their error minima tending to fall at the same start offsets, because every generated video starts its first group at its first frame, whereas real clips show no such common offset. The control experiments are the ones that make the causal claim: a VAE round trip induces the rhythm in real clips, whereas re-encoding with H.264 does not, which pins the trace to VAE decoding rather than to compression in general.

The paper uses the rhythm three ways. First, the rhythm score itself is a zero-shot detector that needs no training, reaching a 76.2 percent AUROC on DF26 and beating the best of 15 retrained baselines by 5.8 points. Second, the rhythm acts as a plug-in: it adds the rhythm score to the score of any existing detector when the rhythm is stronger than in typical real videos, so the detector needs no retraining, and it improves all 19 tested detectors on DF26 by 6.7 to 23.7 percent AUROC. Third, the rhythm is a data engine: the method passes each real clip through a frozen VAE and post-processes the clip and its reconstruction identically, so the two differ only by VAE decoding, and it trains a detector called CaDet, for Cadence Detector, on these pairs. CaDet has an artifact path that learns the VAE decoding traces and a semantic path that never sees them, and a generator-free variant, CaDet-GF, trains only the artifact path on real videos and pairs so it never sees a generated video, yet still reaches 90.9 and 91.9 percent on AIGVDBench's open and closed source generators. The paper reports that CaDet is best on AIGVDBench, DF26, and three 2026 closed-source models.

Now the skeptical read. First, the entire method rests on the assumption that future generators will keep using a 3D causal VAE with grouped temporal latents, and that the grouping will be detectable. That is a strong assumption. The observation is well supported across 31 current generators, but it is a property of the current generation architecture, and a generator that changes its temporal compression scheme, or that is trained to remove the periodic trace, could eliminate the cue. The paper's own argument that the trace is in the perceptual blind spot so developers have no reason to remove it is a good defense, but it is an argument about incentives, not a guarantee, and a motivated adversary who discovers the cue can remove it the same way they remove visible artifacts. Second, the closed-source results depend on the closed models sharing the same VAE structure the authors infer, which is reasonable given the breadth of the observation but is an inference rather than a verification. Third, the plug-in result, improving all 19 tested detectors, is the most broadly useful number, but it is measured on a fixed set of detectors and benchmarks, and the 6.7 to 23.7 percent range is wide, which means the benefit varies substantially by detector and by how much the detector already captures the underlying signal.

Still, the contribution is a real and elegant one. Finding a single, architecture-level trace shared by a large fraction of current generators, and turning it into a training-free score, a drop-in plug-in, and a data engine, is a strong result, and the control experiments that attribute the trace to VAE decoding rather than compression are exactly the rigor a detection paper needs. The generator-free variant is the most important for practical use, because a detector that never sees generated video is much harder to fool by simply adding the latest generator's output to the training set, which is the standard way current detectors get obsoleted.

The control experiments are the part of the paper that separates a real finding from a correlation worth noting. Two of them are decisive. The first is that passing a real video through a VAE round trip induces the rhythm in it: the periodic trace is not a property of the content but of the decoding, so any video that has been through a causal 3D VAE carries it, and any video captured by a camera does not. The second is that re-encoding the same clips with H.264 does not induce the rhythm, which rules out the obvious alternative explanation that the periodicity is an artifact of video compression in general rather than of the VAE's grouped temporal latents. The phase behavior adds a second signal on top of the periodicity: generated clips tend to have their error minima at the same start offsets because every generated video begins its first group at its first frame, while real clips show no common offset, so the detector has both the presence of a period and a consistent phase to work with. The three uses of the trace are ordered by how much infrastructure they require: the zero-shot score needs nothing but a frozen VAE, the plug-in needs nothing but adding the score to an existing detector's output, and CaDet is the only component that is trained, and its generator-free variant is the one that should age best, because it is trained on real videos and their reconstructions and therefore has no exposure to any specific generator's style.

My verdict: VAE Rhythm is a clever, well-controlled, and practically useful detection paper whose central trace is real across a large swath of current generators and whose training-free and generator-free variants are the features that will age best. The main risk is that it is a property of the current VAE-based generation architecture, and a determined adversary or an architectural shift can remove it. Worth reading for anyone working on media forensics.
