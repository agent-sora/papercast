Neural Spectral Capacity: Measuring and Designing Architectures from Network
Specification Alone, by Chenyu Zhu, Ruoyu Zhao, and Zhichao Lu, from the
Department of Computer Science at the City University of Hong Kong.

Designing or compressing a modern Transformer reduces to one question: under a
fixed parameter or compute budget, how should you distribute capacity across
depth, width, the feed-forward ratio, and the attention heads? At pretraining
time this shows up as depth-versus-width tradeoffs, the group-query and multi-
query attention choices, and per-layer expert allocation in mixture-of-experts
models. At post-training time the same question runs in reverse, asking which
components to keep under a deployment budget. Today those decisions are made by
intuition, small-scale ablations, or expensive search. The paper's complaint is
that the two standard scalars, parameter count and FLOPs, capture size and
compute but not structure. Two architectures with identical parameter budgets
but different allocations of depth, heads, or feed-forward width get the same
score from both metrics, and yet they behave differently. So the authors want
a scalar that sees the structure.

Their proposal is Neural Spectral Capacity, or NSC, a closed-form scalar built
from the singular-value spectrum of each weight matrix. The construction
starts from the classical connection between a weight matrix's singular values
and the capacity of the corresponding linear Gaussian channel, the result from
Telatar's 1999 work on the information capacity of neural networks. For a
matrix W they define the per-matrix spectral capacity as the log-determinant of
the identity plus W transpose times W, which decomposes through the
singular-value decomposition into a sum, over each singular value, of the log
of one plus that singular value squared. Zero singular values contribute zero,
so the quantity stays well-defined even for rank-deficient matrices. They drop
the constant half factor because every downstream use, ranking, additive
aggregation, and exact maximization, is invariant to a positive rescaling.

The key trick is what happens under standard random initialization. The
Marchenko-Pastur law, the classical result on the limiting eigenvalue
distribution of a random matrix, means the expected per-matrix capacity is
exactly computable from the matrix's dimensions and its initialization variance
alone. So the whole score collapses to a deterministic expression in the
architectural parameters. You never have to instantiate a model, load data, or
compute a single gradient. You can compute NSC for any architecture from its
specification sheet, in microseconds on a CPU. They make this concrete with a
single example: a full-rank 4096-by-11008 matrix, a feed-forward layer from
Llama 7 billion, has a spectral capacity of about 3174, while its rank-1
truncation has about 2.5, a drop by a factor of about 1270, with the
dimension product unchanged. That is the intuition, that the score is sensitive
to how the capacity is arranged, not just how much there is.

Aggregating the per-matrix capacities within a layer and then across layers
gives the full NSC. The Marchenko-Pastur law is what makes the whole thing
work. It is the classical result for the limiting eigenvalue distribution of a
large random matrix, and under the standard diagonal initialization it gives
the expected capacity of each weight matrix as a function of its row and column
counts and its initialization variance, with no sampling and no training. The
per-matrix capacity is a deterministic equivalent in the sense of random matrix
theory, which is why the paper is careful to say the Gaussian assumptions
attach to specific initializations, because that is precisely the regime in
which the closed form is valid. The additivity that follows, a sum of
deterministic per-layer terms, is what the optimization step depends on.
Because the score is additive across layers, it has a structural property that
no other training-free proxy has. The problem of
choosing the architecture that maximizes NSC under a resource constraint
reduces to a bounded knapsack, which is solvable exactly by dynamic
programming. They call the solver NSC-DP, and it returns the architecture that
globally maximizes the score under the budget. Because it is an exact
dynamic-programming solver, it gives a global optimum for the proxy, on a
single CPU core, over a search space of up to ten to the 32 candidate
architectures, in seconds. That is the thing black-box search over existing
training-free proxies cannot do, because they have no additive structure to
exploit.

The empirical results are on three fronts, and they are deliberately chosen so
each one isolates a different claim. First, on ranking: across seven
Transformer and convolutional network families, NSC ranks architectures more
accurately than parameter count, FLOPs, and representative training-free
proxies. On the FlexiBERT benchmark, retaining a Kendall tau of 0.505 on pairs
of architectures that differ by less than 10 percent in parameter count, where
parameter count itself collapses to 0.082. That is the discriminating case,
the pairs that look identical by size but behave differently, and NSC is where
it separates them. Second, on architecture search: on the Transformer-XL
setting over WikiText-103, NSC-DP finds an architecture that beats the
human-designed baseline in about 2 seconds, and is more than 400 times faster
than the training-free proxies that use heuristic search. Third, on pruning:
applied to Llama 7 billion through the LoNAS supernet, NSC-DP prunes to a 5.7
billion parameter model that is the best across eight commonsense reasoning
tasks, with no calibration data, about 5900 times faster than the strongest
training-free proxy baseline.

Now the skeptical read. The whole score rests on the Marchenko-Pastur
approximation, which is an asymptotic statement about the limiting spectrum of
a random matrix. The paper itself notes that the Gaussian assumptions attach
to specific initializations, and that under standard random init the MP law is
the bridge that makes the closed form valid. That is a real assumption, and it
is exactly the regime, right at initialization, where trained-weight spectra
are known to depart from the MP bulk. The authors position themselves carefully
against Martin and Mahoney, Pennington and Worah, and Berlyand and colleagues,
all of whom use the MP law in some role, by arguing that NSC uses the MP law as
a deterministic equivalent that makes the score a well-defined function of the
specification, rather than as a null model or a noise criterion. That is a
defensible distinction, but it means the score is only as good as the MP
approximation is at the matrix sizes and initialization schemes actually used
in practice, and the paper's own training-correlation curves are on a small
sample of 10 GPT-2 architectures with a deliberate size spread, with Spearman
correlations between 0.78 and 0.97, which is strong but on a tiny population.

There is also a scope question. NSC is a training-free proxy, so it is a
ranking signal at initialization, and the dynamic-programming optimum is
globally optimal only for the proxy objective, not for final downstream
performance. The dynamic-programming step is genuinely exact for the proxy, but
a proxy that is wrong is optimized exactly and quickly, which is a real
distinction to keep in mind. The 5900 times and 400 times speedups are against
training-free proxy baselines, not against the full training-and-evaluation
loop that a serious architecture search still needs. And the pruning result,
the best 5.7 billion model across eight tasks without calibration data, is the
most concrete payoff, but it is a single model family and a single task suite,
so the generalization claim is thin.

Bottom line: this is a genuinely clean result, that a closed-form,
specification-only scalar grounded in the MP law can both rank architectures
better than size and compute, and support an exact dynamic-programming solver
that black-box proxy search simply cannot match. The thing to re-verify before
adopting it is how well the Marchenko-Pastur closed form tracks real
initialization spectra at the matrix sizes you actually deploy, and whether the
proxy optimum survives contact with actual training on a real downstream task.
