Massive Activations in Hybrid Linear Attention Large Language Models: Pre-Attention Spikes and Inter-Spike Plateaus, by Zunhai Su, Bohan Sun, Xialie Zhuang, Shuibai Zhang, and colleagues at Startlux, Tsinghua University, the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, The University of Hong Kong, the University of Sydney, and Columbia University. If you ship hybrid architectures, models interleaving cheap linear attention layers with occasional full attention layers, this is the first systematic map of what massive activations do inside them. Massive activations are the sparse hidden-state entries that exceed typical activation values by orders of magnitude and concentrate on specific tokens; in full attention transformers they are tightly coupled to attention sinks and have been implicated in attention dynamics generally. The question here is what layerwise interleaving does to them, and the answer turns out to be structured enough to matter for anyone debugging or quantizing these models.

The setup first. A hybrid linear attention model alternates sequence-mixing layers: full softmax attention, which is content-dependent but quadratic in sequence length, and linear attention variants that keep a fixed-size recurrent state and run in linear time. The hybridization ratio rho counts sequence-mixing layers per full attention layer; twelve to one means one full attention layer per twelve mixing layers, and one to one recovers a plain transformer. The study uses the M-A-P Hybrid Linear Attention Research suite, twenty-four-layer models trained from scratch on FineWeb-Edu with identical recipes, spanning five linear attention backbones, Transformer, GLA, DeltaNet, Gated DeltaNet, HGRN, and RetNet, at scales of 340 million parameters trained on twenty billion tokens and 1.3 billion trained on one hundred billion tokens, with AdamW and cosine schedules under the Flash Linear Attention framework.

Two morphologies emerge. First, pre-attention spikes: across all five linear attention backbones at a fixed twelve-to-one ratio, sink-associated activations form pronounced local maxima immediately before each full attention layer. Second, inter-spike plateaus: as full attention gets denser, activations between successive spikes stay elevated instead of decaying to troughs, bridging the spikes into sustained plateaus. At the full attention limit, spikes and plateaus merge into the model-wide stable morphology conventional transformers show. The quantitative tool is the inter-spike retention score: for each pair of adjacent spikes, it measures how much activation persists through intervening layers relative to the weaker neighboring spike, clipped at one, averaged over five hundred inputs drawn equally from five domains, WikiText-103 prose, Scientific Papers, GSM8K math, CodeSearchNet Python, and FLORES-200 multilingual text. The numbers confirm the picture: at three-to-one hybridization, retention reaches 85 to 92 percent across backbones, while at twelve-to-one it collapses to single digits or low tens for most, for example 7.2 percent for HGRN and 18.4 percent for GDN. Architecture matters too; DeltaNet starts forming plateaus already at six-to-one while GDN needs three-to-one.

A methodological finding deserves emphasis because it affects how you should instrument your own runs. In full attention models, magnitude ranking reliably locates massive activations because magnitude aligns with attention sinks. In hybrids, that alignment degrades: sink tokens sit lower and less stably in per-layer magnitude rankings, and the maximally activated token switches between adjacent layers far more often than in the full attention baseline, with switching rates in the forty-six to sixty-two percent range versus fourteen percent for the transformer. So the authors anchor tracking on consensus attention sinks instead, identifying the dominant sink token by averaging causal attention mass across all full attention layers and heads, then tracing that fixed token's maximum absolute hidden-state entry across depth. If you are hunting outliers in hybrid checkpoints, magnitude ranking alone will mislead you; use attention-derived anchors.

The phenomenon survives contact with production-scale models. Across twelve public checkpoints from Kimi Linear, Qwen3.5, Nemotron-H, and Zamba2, spanning 1.2 billion to 397 billion total parameters, periodic and nonuniform attention schedules, base and instruct variants, and both linear-attention and Mamba-style state-space mixers, spikes align with full attention placement and plateau spans stay stable. Matched Kimi Linear base and instruct checkpoints retain aligned spike locations despite different magnitudes, and input domain changes spike strength rather than position, which supports reading the layerwise organization as architectural, not data-driven.

Controlled pretraining answers the emergence question. Training GDN hybrids from scratch, with a single full attention layer inserted at depth four, twelve, or twenty of twenty-four layers, shows the spike forms immediately before whatever layer holds full attention and shifts predictably when placement changes; deeper placement produces stronger spikes, and in the layer-twelve configuration the spike is visible after just one billion training tokens and sharpens monotonically through ten billion, while the 1.3 billion scale shows a weak precursor at five billion tokens, a pronounced spike by ten billion, and persistent localization through fifty billion. Plateaus likewise emerge early in three-to-one models and consolidate during optimization, at both scales, with training loss curves confirming stable optimization throughout. The recipes are documented for replication: sequence length 4,096, weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping at 1.0, learning rate warmed linearly up to around the three-times-ten-to-the-minus-four scale and cosine-decayed back down, batch sizes from half a million tokens at 340 million parameters to two million at 1.3 billion, all trained on FineWeb-Edu under Flash Linear Attention, with checkpoints publicly released. Output gating experiments then reveal a sharp asymmetry: adding output gates to the full attention layers markedly attenuates both spikes and plateaus, though a small pre-attention peak persists throughout training, while removing the native gates from every GDN layer produces only moderate amplification. Fewer gates on full attention layers move the system more than gates everywhere in linear layers, indicating full attention organizes these dynamics and linear gating mainly modulates propagation. Notably, Qwen3.5 ships native full attention output gating and still exhibits both morphologies, so gating attenuates rather than eliminates the organization.

The mechanistic account ties it together through cancellation timing. Fixed-coordinate analysis on the spike preceding full attention layer twelve traces one token-feature coordinate through three stages: the pre-attention layer writes an extreme value into the residual stream, the sink token then collects a disproportionate share of attention during the full attention layer, and a subsequent opposite-signed update cancels the outlier, dissipating the spike. That is a localized write-sink-cancel lifecycle. Plateaus fit the same lifecycle with delayed cancellation: the outlier written before full attention persists across intervening linear layers until a later opposite-signed update arrives. Spikes, plateaus, and stable full-attention massive activations then read as one continuum of increasing outlier persistence, parameterized by when cancellation happens. Downstream numbers contextualize but do not dramatize: the gated variants track each other closely on perplexity, around 13.7 to 14.0 for the 340 million scale and 9.8 at 1.3 billion, and the controlled models were additionally evaluated on WikiText-103 language modeling, zero-shot HellaSwag, PIQA, and ARC-Easy commonsense reasoning, plus needle-in-a-haystack retrieval tasks where values are replaced with UUIDs and evaluated by exact match at a context length of 4,096 tokens, all without claiming performance differences from the morphology itself. What regulates cancellation timing, and whether transient spikes and persistent plateaus serve distinct computational roles, are explicitly open questions.

Skeptical read. This is characterization plus a consistent mechanistic story, not a causal proof that cancellation timing drives anything; what regulates cancellation timing is explicitly left open, and no experiment manipulates it directly. The controlled pretraining covers one backbone family, GDN, up to 1.3 billion parameters, so the gating asymmetry could look different in other linear attention designs. The inter-spike retention metric normalizes by adjacent spike magnitude, which makes cross-variant comparisons interpretive rather than absolute, as the authors themselves caution. And the practical payoff, whether managing these outliers improves quantization, KV cache decisions, or training stability in hybrids, remains untested here. Before adopting anything, replicate the sink-anchored tracking on your own checkpoint and verify your magnitude-based outlier detection is not silently mislocating the action. Bottom line: a careful, well-instrumented anatomy paper that gives the fast-growing hybrid architecture space its first coherent account of massive activation behavior, organized around one testable lifecycle idea. The observation toolkit is the part worth stealing today; the mechanism is the part worth testing tomorrow.
