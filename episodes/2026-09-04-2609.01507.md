LatentPress: Context Compression Beyond Text and Vision, by Zhengze Zhou and Hejian Sang, from Cornell University and Iowa State University. This is a small, focused paper on a specific infrastructure problem, and the idea is a clean one: stop carrying compressed context as human-readable text or as rendered images that have to be decoded, and instead write it directly into a third representation, continuous memory tokens that a frozen language model reads straight through its input-embedding interface. If you are building long-context or agentic systems where you compress history to fit the context window, this is a proposal to compress into the model's own latent space rather than into a format you then have to decode back out.

The motivation is that the consumer of compressed context is usually a language model, yet the compressed context is almost always produced in a format meant for a human. You either summarize the history into readable text, or you render it into an image and let the model OCR or decode it. Both routes add a decoding step the model did not ask for, and both lose information in the process. Text summaries are cheap to produce but throw away the fine-grained detail a memory task depends on, and image-based compression is more faithful but pays a decoding cost and inherits the OCR error floor. LatentPress's argument is that if the reader is a model, the writer should write in the model's native representation. So the system writes conversational histories and long documents into continuous memory tokens, and the frozen decoder reads those tokens directly through its input-embedding interface, with no text reconstruction at inference time. The result is a third representation that is neither text nor image, but the internal form the model already speaks.

The architecture is deliberately small on the trained side. There is a reader-matched writer, meaning the writer is trained to produce tokens that the specific frozen decoder can read, and only an adapter is trained, ranging from 4.2 million to 26.2 million parameters, which the paper notes is about 0.1 percent of the decoder. That is a real implementation property, because it means you can drop a compact writer in front of a model you do not want to retrain, and the writer is small enough that you can afford to train it per-decoder or per-domain without a serious compute budget.

The compression ratio is the headline number on the capability side. The writer compresses context by 4 to 16 times. On LongMemEval, which is a long-memory question-answering benchmark, LatentPress reaches 0.504 accuracy at 7.7 times compression, versus 0.490 for the uncompressed evidence. That is the number to sit on, because it says that at a meaningful compression ratio the latent representation is not just competitive with reading the raw context, it is slightly better than it. The comparison set is what makes that interesting: text summaries reach 0.184, and OCR-based image compression falls in a range from 0.426 down to 0.312. So the latent representation beats both of the human-facing compression routes by a wide margin, and it does it while being shorter. The gap over the text-summary baseline is the largest and the most telling, because it says that summarizing a long memory into readable prose throws away most of what the memory task actually needs, and that the latent tokens carry that information without the lossy text round-trip.

The speed numbers matter as much as the accuracy, because this is a serving-infrastructure paper. Writing takes 43 milliseconds per conversation, which the paper frames as roughly an order of magnitude faster than text summarization or OCR reconstruction, and reading is 5 to 9 times faster than reading raw context or cached OCR. If your bottleneck in an agentic system is the time you spend compressing and re-reading context on every step, those latency numbers are the actual contribution, and they are consistent with the mechanism: you are skipping the decode step entirely, so you pay only for a small adapter forward pass on the way in and a direct embedding lookup on the way out.

The ablations are where the method gets stress-tested, and they are worth a moment because they separate the parts that are load-bearing from the parts that are incidental. The authors run a writer ablation and an encoder-training ablation, both on LongMemEval, using Qwen2.5-7B as the reader and a Llama-3.1-70B judge to score accuracy, and they report per-category accuracy rather than just the aggregate, which is the right call for a memory benchmark, because a long-memory QA task is a mixture of different retrieval behaviors and an aggregate can hide a category where the method is quietly failing. The compression setting they center on is the one that yields the 7.7 times figure in the headline, and the point of the ablation is to show that the advantage over text summaries and OCR compression is not an artifact of a particular compression ratio but holds across the range. The reader-matched design shows up here too: the writer is trained against the specific frozen decoder, and the ablation confirms that the small adapter, not the size of the compression, is what carries the result.

The transfer experiments are the part I would weigh most carefully, and they are the honest core of the paper. The authors validate the interface under two transfer settings. One is zero-shot transfer from UltraChat to LongMemEval memory question-answering, and the other is transfer from LongMemEval-derived question-answering to unseen LongBench document domains. In-domain writers match or exceed raw-context reading at 4 to 8 times compression, while at 16 times they trail the raw context. That last clause is the limitation stated plainly: the higher you push the compression, the more you fall below reading the raw context, and the sweet spot is the middle of the range rather than the extreme. The cross-domain transfer results are what justify calling this a general machine-facing context interface rather than a memorization trick tuned to one benchmark.

The skeptical read. The strongest result is on one benchmark, LongMemEval, and the margin over uncompressed context at 7.7 times is 0.014, which is small enough that you should want it reproduced on a second long-memory or long-document evaluation before building a system on it. The in-domain advantage at 4 to 8 times compression is reassuring, but the 16 times result trailing raw context tells you the method has a hard wall, and that wall is exactly where a production system would want to be if it is trying to fit very long histories. The reader-matched design is a feature and a cost at the same time: because the writer is trained against a specific frozen decoder, it is not a drop-in universal compressor, and you inherit the cost of retraining the adapter whenever the reader changes, which is a real constraint if your model lineup turns over as fast as it does. The 43 millisecond write latency is per conversation, so for a system that compresses hundreds of short turns per episode the per-turn cost is what you actually feel, and the paper's per-conversation figure does not fully answer that. And the 0.1 percent adapter size is impressive but it is a relative size, so the absolute parameter count still matters at the very top end of the compression range, where the small adapter has to do more work per parameter.

The bottom line: this is a clean, well-scoped paper on a real serving problem, and the result that latent context beats both text summaries and OCR-compressed images while being shorter and faster is a legitimately useful contribution for anyone compressing context for a model. The defensible claims are the LongMemEval accuracy at 7.7 times, the 5 to 9 times read speedup, and the two transfer settings that argue for generality. The limits are the small margin over uncompressed context, the hard wall at 16 times, and the reader-matched design that means you retrain the adapter when the model changes.
