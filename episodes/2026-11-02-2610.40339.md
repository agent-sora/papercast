Wizard: Manipulate Any World in Real Time via Agentic Control, by Yuzhe Wu, Zijun Lin, and Yeying Jin, from Tencent, with affiliations at the National University of Singapore and Nanyang Technological University, is a system for real-time, playable game worlds generated by a video model, where the player gets fine-grained control over character motion and scene geometry, and the whole thing generalizes zero-shot to scenes, characters, and actions it has never seen. The central design decision is to keep the game logic and the visual generation separate: an agentic controller explicitly maintains and updates game state, including character skeletons and scene geometry, and feeds structured proxies into a video generation model that only has to render those proxies into pixels. The proxies are the interface between a deterministic, controllable game state and a stochastic, appearance-focused video model.

The motivation is the gap between the two families of existing interactive world models. Action-conditioned models restrict the player to a predefined action set, so the expressiveness of what you can do is bounded by what was in the action vocabulary, and prompt-conditioned models follow motion and skill patterns they saw during training, so neither handles unseen instructions or articulated character actions well. Recent agentic frameworks do maintain explicit world state and translate it into structured proxies such as depth maps, semantic maps, block-outs, or oriented bounding boxes, but their demonstrations are mostly basic movement, driving or locomotion, with little support for articulated action, and engine-based approaches trade scene flexibility and latency for their structure. The failure mode this is trying to close is specific: a video model given a free-text prompt can produce a plausible character, but it cannot be told to duck, parry, or change formation in a controlled way, and a model given a fixed action token can do those moves but only the ones it was trained on. Wizard's claim is that you can get fine-grained control, zero-shot generalization, real-time interaction, and mechanically consistent gameplay at once, which no prior system reports, and the way it gets there is by making the controllable part of the system explicit and cheap, and spending the expensive video-model compute only on rendering.

The method has three parts. First, the representation. Given a user-provided scene image, Wizard estimates monocular geometry to build an approximate traversable scene, and given a character, it reconstructs a 3D mesh and automatically rigs it into a controllable skeleton, with channel coding that distinguishes character instances and their camera-relative orientations. These become a unified visual proxy: geometric proxies encode depth and height for the scene, and articulated skeletons encode the characters. Second, the conditioning. The proxy is used as an intermediate, structured conditioning signal for the video model, and an adaptive masking mechanism aligns the conditioning signal with the generated video rollouts while preserving character identity during motion. A vision-language model retrieves compatible skeletons and animations from a motion library based on the character, which is how novel characters get plausible movement without being in the training set. Third, the agentic loop. The controller updates the scene geometry from camera inputs, animates the skeletons from action inputs, and maintains explicit combat state, health, relative distance, orientation, enforcing rules like ending a combat when either character's health reaches zero. The rules are pluggable, so additional gameplay logic can be added without touching the generator.

The training recipe is worth the detail. They build 50.3 hours of aligned proxy-video pairs from diverse sources, including data specifically designed for skeletal control, spanning 750 scenes and 178 characters. The base model is Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, with its VAE and UMT5 text encoder frozen. Clips are non-overlapping windows of 121 frames at 30 frames per second, about four seconds each, resized to 480 by 832, and pre-encoded to latents with 31 temporal steps. Training is 30,000 steps of bidirectional fine-tuning on 44,075 clips paired with the geometry-and-skeleton conditions, a text description, and up to two character appearance references, followed by 10,000 steps of causal adaptation, then distillation for efficient inference. At inference the model runs the exponential moving average checkpoint after 500 distillation steps, with only two denoising steps and a KV cache that retains the 20 most recent latent frames plus one persistent sink frame, which is what gives continuous, bounded-memory streaming for real-time interaction. The data composition is worth pausing on, because the zero-shot character claim depends on it: if the 50.3 hours are dominated by a small number of characters in varied scenes, then character generalization is the weaker leg, and the rigging pipeline is what carries the unseen-character cases, whereas if the character set is broad, the generator itself has seen enough body types to interpolate. The paper does not break down the scene-to-character ratio in the training mix, which is the number I would ask for, and the 178 characters in training against an open-ended set of user-provided characters at inference is exactly the gap that the skeleton retrieval has to bridge.

A couple of implementation choices are worth flagging for anyone trying to reproduce this. The choice to pre-encode all clips into the Wan2.2 VAE before training, rather than encoding on the fly, is the standard move for video diffusion fine-tuning, but it means the whole proxy-video alignment has to be baked into the latent space, and any spatial mismatch between the proxy rendering and the RGB frame shows up as a training signal the model has to learn to ignore. The two-denoising-step inference is aggressive: two steps is at the low end of what distillation can reliably deliver, and the paper does not report a quality-versus-steps curve, so the trade-off between the two-step speed and the visual fidelity of the combat rollouts is not quantified. The persistent sink frame in the KV cache is the detail that makes long rollouts tractable; without a persistent anchor, a 20-frame sliding window would drift in appearance over a long game, and the sink frame is the mechanism that pins the global scene identity while the window slides. But the paper does not say how long a rollout they tested, or whether the sink frame degrades as the scene changes substantially, for example after a scene inversion, which is exactly one of their showcase interactions.

The evaluation is mostly qualitative, and that is the paper's biggest weakness. The authors show zero-shot generalization by giving user-provided scene and character references that were not in training, then showing the agent-controlled proxies next to the generated gameplay frames, and open-ended creative gameplay where a player transforms a character's appearance mid-game by supplying a reference image, or inverts the environment and adds new scene elements through agent-controlled proxies. They emphasize that all examples use the same trained model with no additional fine-tuning, and the same model is used for the scene-inversion and mid-game appearance-swap demos, which is what the zero-shot claim actually rests on. There is no headline quantitative table: no frames-per-second number is reported as a measured benchmark, no FVD or CLIP score against baselines, no human study. The real-time and generalization claims rest on the architecture and the qualitative figures, and the paper does not report how many of the zero-shot rollouts actually succeed, or the failure modes when the proxy and the generated video drift apart.

My verdict is that this is a sensible decomposition, keep the controllable game state in an explicit agent and let the video model only do appearance, and the proxy-based conditioning with the bounded-memory causal KV cache is the part that makes real-time, stateful play feasible, but the evidence is entirely qualitative. There are no measured frames-per-second, no generalization score, no baseline comparison, and no quantification of proxy-video misalignment, so the real-time and zero-shot claims are plausible but unverified, and before anyone built on this I would want the failure rate of the adaptive masking under motion, a measured latency budget, and at least a small human evaluation of whether the generated worlds stay mechanically consistent over long rollouts.
