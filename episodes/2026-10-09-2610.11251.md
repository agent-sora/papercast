"V-CoLA: vision token compression with linear attention," by Hao Jiang, Yiru Mao, and Tianpeng Bu from Alibaba Cloud Computing. This sits in the linear-attention architecture space, so it is in scope on the standing rule for alternative attention. The problem is a practical one that has been getting more urgent, not less. Vision-language models take in a huge number of vision tokens, far more than text tokens, and that dominates the cost of the forward pass, especially during prefill. A whole line of work has attacked this with vision token compression, pruning or merging tokens to cut the sequence. But almost all of those methods were built around softmax attention, and they are evaluated on softmax models. The catch is that the newest hybrid vision-language models, including Qwen 3.5, now use linear attention in their stack, and the authors show that the existing compression methods just do not transfer.

The first thing they establish is the failure, and it is a surprising one. They take the two main families of existing methods, the attention-based ones that pick tokens by their softmax attention scores, and the similarity-based ones that pick tokens by how distinct their features are, and they run them on a hybrid linear-attention model. The result is that both of them perform at essentially no better than a random baseline. They are not just a little worse, they are not beating random token pruning. So the standard intuition, that important vision tokens are the ones that get attended to, or the ones that are feature-distinct, breaks down under linear attention. Their analysis explains why, and it is one of the more careful parts of the paper. In softmax attention, every token stays independently addressable, so you can always ask how much the output leaned on any one past token, and that is exactly the signal the attention-based compressors exploit. Linear attention throws that away. It replaces the explicit attention map with a single fixed-size recurrent state, a matrix of dimension d by d, and the whole history gets written into that one state through a sequence of gated update steps. They run an information-theoretic argument: the state can only hold on the order of d squared bits of information, but the source sequence, the vision tokens, carries information that grows linearly with the sequence length. Once the sequence is longer than d, which is always the case for high-resolution vision inputs, the state is provably lossy, and the gap grows linearly with length. That bounded state forces the model to update its memory selectively, and that selective retention is exactly what breaks both the shallow-layer attention concentration the attention-based methods rely on, and the token distinguishability the similarity methods rely on.

The key insight is that this same bounded-state mechanism also hands them a new signal. Because the recurrent state is forced to retain selectively, the way the state update treats each token is itself an importance signal. They lean on that to design V-CoLA, a training-free compression framework built specifically for linear attention. It has two parts. The first is a uniqueness-aware importance criterion. For each vision token, they look at two things: its long-term contribution to the final state, which is the retention signal they get for free from the linear attention recurrence, and its short-term uniqueness relative to the recent context, so that they keep a token that carries new information but skip one that is just a duplicate of what has already been written to the state. That second term is what fixes the failure mode they show in their own examples, where a similarity-based method latches onto one semantic region and misses the rest of the image. The second part is an adaptive chunk-wise merging strategy. They group tokens into chunks, and within each chunk they merge, adjusting the chunk size dynamically so that regions with a lot of important detail keep a higher resolution while sparse regions get compressed harder. The whole thing is implemented to stay compatible with the chunk-wise parallelism that linear attention acceleration relies on, so it does not fight the very mechanism it is exploiting. Before they build the method, they also do the diagnostic work that justifies it. They measure, across layers and across sequence lengths, how much of the visual information written into the recurrent state is actually retained in the final state. They find the retention rate is high in the shallow layers and falls off sharply with depth, dropping below 20 percent in the deeper layers, which tells them the visual work is front-loaded into the early layers. The retention also drops as the sequence gets longer, exactly matching the information ceiling they argued for. And the ranking of which tokens get retained stays consistent across adjacent shallow layers, meaning the layers agree on what matters during the visual-reasoning stage. That consistency is what makes reading the importance signal out of the recurrence a reliable thing to do, rather than noise.

The results are on Qwen 3.5, a 9 billion parameter hybrid model, against the state of the art. With 50 percent of the vision tokens kept, V-CoLA retains 99.5 percent of the original performance, and with as few as 12.5 percent of the tokens kept, it still retains over 88 percent. The speedup is 1.86 to 6.15 times on prefill, depending on the sequence length, and the overhead of the compression itself is only about 1.6 percent of the forward-pass cost. Those are strong numbers for a training-free method. The comparison that matters, though, is that at the same token budgets the attention-based and similarity-based baselines fall behind the random baseline, so V-CoLA is not just better by a margin, it is the difference between a method that works and methods that do not transfer at all. One thing that makes the result more useful than a single benchmark win is the generality. They test on multiple backbone configurations, including the chunk-wise and full-sequence variants of the hybrid model, and the same two-part design, the importance criterion plus adaptive merging, transfers across them without re-tuning the hyperparameters, because the importance signal is read off the model's own recurrence rather than fit to a particular dataset. That is the part of the contribution I would carry into other settings, the observation that the recurrence's own write pattern is a free, architecture-matched importance signal that softmax-based heuristics simply do not have access to.

The honest caveats. This is a compression result, so the ceiling is set by the underlying model, and the headline is really about how much of that performance you keep while cutting cost, not about new capability. The 99.5 percent at 50 percent tokens is great, but it is also close to the obvious point that keeping half the tokens will keep most of the performance, so the more interesting regime is the aggressive one, 12.5 percent, where the 88 percent figure is more meaningful but still leaves a real drop. And the evaluation is on a single family of hybrid model, Qwen 3.5, so the claim that this is the right tool for linear-attention VLMs is currently validated on one model rather than a broad range of hybrid architectures. The information-theoretic argument is clean and general, though, and that is the piece I would trust most.

Here is the bottom line. V-CoLA documents a concrete, information-theoretically-grounded reason why softmax-era vision token compression stops working under linear attention, and it replaces the broken attention and similarity signals with a retention-based, uniqueness-aware importance criterion that is native to the linear-attention recurrence. If you are running or building hybrid vision-language models on linear-attention backbones, the failure analysis and the training-free compression are both directly useful, but treat the aggressive compression numbers as model-specific until you see them hold across a wider set of hybrid architectures.
