The paper is Deep Search in Real-World Web Environments, from Xiaomin Yu and Hongliang Chen at Stanford, Zhe Huang at Microsoft Research, with a long co-author list running to twenty-five names that closes with project leaders from both institutions. It is a benchmark paper, and a serious one: the authors define a new task, OpenDS, which stands for Open-Web Deep Search, build a 200-task benchmark called OpenDS-World on top of it, and run twelve frontier models plus a five-hundred-person human study against it. The central result is uncomfortable for everyone building agents: the best model, GPT-6 Astra from OpenAI, reaches only 39.0 percent accuracy on the full set, while five hundred human solvers, each given a slice of the tasks, manage 21.1 percent across a thousand independent attempts.

The problem setting is the gap between the static retrieval environments that most deep-search agents are trained and tested on, and the actual live Web. On the live Web, the evidence for any given answer is distributed across heterogeneous sources, modalities, and file formats, and a lot of it is not directly retrievable at all. The OpenDS task formulation requires an agent to start from a single prompt, autonomously explore the real Web, discover evidence from multiple independent sources through Web search, browser interaction, and document processing, connect that evidence into a chain, and produce a single factual answer. There is no reference answer and no evidence path handed to the agent. The benchmark spans eight domains: culture, daily life, science, economy, history, nature, society, and technology, and the authors deliberately engineered three axes of difficulty. The first is modality: tasks require evidence from text, images, video, audio, and virtual tour interfaces. The second is format: PDFs, XML, Parquet, and similar structured or semi-structured files, with the paper reporting that an Other bucket of long-tail formats covers 108 tasks. The third is interactive access, where the evidence cannot be pulled by a simple retrieval call and instead requires navigation, interface manipulation, parameter changes, or moving an artifact between pages.

Construction is where the paper earns most of its credibility. Every task goes through independent human verification: reviewers re-derive the answer chain using Web sources other than the original source, to prevent over-reliance on one page, and the pipeline filters out any task whose evidence turns out to be invalid or whose answer is not uniquely determined. Difficulty labeling comes from the human study itself, which is unusually well-powered for this literature. They recruited five hundred participants, split the 200 tasks so each person solved ten, for a thousand independent solving attempts, with the only restriction that solvers could use standard browsers and not any large-model tool. Two hundred and eleven of the thousand attempts were correct, hence the 21.1 percent. At the task level, 115 tasks, or 57.5 percent, were solved by at least one human, and 85, or 42.5 percent, defeated all five assigned solvers. That 42.5 percent number matters because it bounds what you should expect from any model: a quarter of the benchmark is, by construction, harder than the task of five independent humans with open browser access.

A concrete example from the paper shows what a task looks like end to end. The agent is given an anchor, for instance a short video clip, and must first identify the organism shown in it. From that identity it has to reach a second, unrelated source, in this case a virtual tour, to find where the organism is documented, then inspect a structured document, and finally pull a value out of a tabular file to assemble the final answer. Each hop is a different access type, and the evidence at each hop is only discoverable if the agent correctly resolves the previous one. This is the design principle behind the whole benchmark: no task should be solvable by a single search-and-read pattern, and the evidence chain is constructed so that skipping any hop makes the final answer unreachable. The seven access types range from plain page navigation to interface manipulation and artifact transfer, and the benchmark tracks per-task coverage of each type, which lets you see exactly where a model gives up.

The model evaluation runs all twelve systems under a single tool environment and execution protocol. The field includes GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Qwen3.8-Max and Qwen3.8-Flash, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Kimi K3, GLM-5.3-Flash, and GLM-5.3. The headline spread, in order from the leaderboard: GPT-6 Astra at 39.0, Claude Opus 5.5 at 38.0, Gemini 3.8 Flash at 36.5, Claude Fable 5.1 at 33.5, GPT-5.6 Sol at 28.0, Qwen3.8-Max at 25.5, GLM-5.3-Flash at 23.5, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at 20.5, Kimi K3 at 17.5, GLM-5.3 at 13.5, DeepSeek-V4-Pro at 12.5, and Qwen3.8-Flash at 12.0, with human performance at 21.1 sitting between the middle and the bottom of the model pack. The difficulty breakdown is more interesting than the aggregate. On the 54 low-difficulty tasks, GPT-6 Astra reaches 59.26 and Claude Opus 5.5 reaches 53.70. On the 61 medium tasks the gap tightens, with Astra at 40.98 and Opus at 40.98. On the 85 high-difficulty tasks the field collapses, with Astra down to 24.71 and Opus down to 25.88. Cost analysis follows the same pattern: Astra used roughly 62 hours of wall-clock and 187 million tokens across the 200 tasks, while Opus spent 65 hours and 339 million, and Gemini 3.8 Flash, despite a mid-table accuracy, burned 976 million tokens.

The behavioral analysis is the part I would actually read twice. The authors instrument tool calls per task, elapsed time, termination behavior, and failure recovery, and the consistent finding is that effort is not a proxy for ability. Models that make more tool calls and run longer trajectories do not systematically score higher. What separates the strong models is source selection, that is, which pages and documents they decide are worth opening, and strategy adjustment when a source dead-ends. The weak models show a characteristic failure loop: they commit to an early source, keep querying variants of the same query, and terminate either early with a low-confidence guess or late after exhausting their step budget with no synthesis. There is also a text-only cohort in the results, marked with a superscript T, and the multimodal capability gap shows up mostly on the tasks whose evidence chain runs through video or virtual-tour content, which is exactly the axis where humans also dropped hardest.

One more finding from the analysis deserves a callout because it cuts against the common assumption that long-horizon agents improve with more steps. The paper reports per-model tool calls per task and elapsed time distributions, and the correlation with accuracy is weak. Kimi K3 and the Qwen3.8-Flash model sit near the bottom on accuracy with middling call budgets, while Gemini 3.8 Flash spends the largest token budget in the study and lands only third. The termination behavior analysis finds that the strongest models terminate with a synthesized answer a higher fraction of the time, and that the failure-recovery rate, the share of dead-ended subqueries that the agent successfully redirects after a wrong source, is the single behavioral metric most correlated with final accuracy across the twelve systems. That is a useful diagnostic: if you are building an agent for live-Web research, measuring how well it recovers from a bad source is more informative than measuring how much it searches.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? The task construction and the human study I would trust: the verification protocol, the no-LLM rule for human solvers, and the powered sample are exactly right, and the 42.5 percent human-unsolved figure gives the benchmark a genuine difficulty floor rather than a vibes-based one. What I would re-verify is the model side. The leaderboard is a single snapshot of a fast-moving field, the twelve models were run under one tool environment, and browser agents are highly sensitive to the exact browser harness, step budgets, and tool schemas. I would want the per-task trajectories before adopting any of these numbers into an internal ranking, and I would re-run the top three models under my own harness to check that the 39.0 versus 38.0 gap is real and not an artifact of one model being more tolerant of the tool wrapper. The cost-per-correct-answer analysis is also thin: with 200 tasks, the token-cost columns have wide uncertainty, and a model that spends 976 million tokens for 36.5 percent accuracy is not necessarily a worse product than one that spends 187 million for 39.0.

Bottom line: OpenDS-World is a well-built, honestly-calibrated benchmark, and its headline finding is that even the best frontier model solves under half of a 200-task live-Web deep search, with a quarter of the tasks beyond the reach of five humans each. Treat the leaderboard as directional, re-run the top systems in your own harness before making decisions on it, and watch the cost-per-solved-task, not raw accuracy, when you compare agents for production.
