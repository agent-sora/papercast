PACT: From Credit Assignment to Critic Alignment. The paper carries the byline AllSpark Research, and the first three listed authors are Jiayan Fu, Hang Xu, and Yong Zhang, with the remaining authors including Zhaokai Luo, Yao Hu, Dongyan Zhao, and Mu Chuan.

The problem is the granularity mismatch at the heart of reinforcement learning for language models. The training signal is a scalar outcome reward that arrives only after the whole trajectory is complete, but the optimization happens at the level of individual tokens. So the question that every RL algorithm actually answers, in some implicit and usually algorithm-dependent way, is: how should the final outcome be attributed to the tokens that produced it? That question is credit assignment, and the uncomfortable fact this paper starts from is that credit has no generally accepted mathematical definition. Everyone operationalizes it with a different quantity, and nobody has said what the quantity should be. The authors' move is to define it by its properties. They postulate three regularity conditions: Completeness, the credit over the whole trajectory must sum to the outcome; Prefix Consistency, the credit you assign has to be consistent as the trajectory grows one token at a time; and Neutrality, tokens that do not change the expected outcome should carry no credit. And then they prove these three conditions uniquely determine token-level credit. There is exactly one object that satisfies all three, and that object subsumes the related forms that prior work derived under specific objectives.

The power of a uniqueness result is in what you can now prove about existing algorithms. They show that under an ideal teacher, the on-policy distillation update is equivalent in expectation, up to a scaling factor, to the policy-gradient update induced by that unique credit, with the teacher acting as an implicit critic, which retroactively explains why distillation works. They show that REINFORCE Leave-One-Out, a response-level signal with coarser granularity than tokens, induces the same expected policy-gradient contribution as the unique credit despite its coarseness, which explains its surprising effectiveness. And they establish an approximate sparsity result: under bounded outcome rewards, most tokens carry nearly zero credit, which is a formal statement of the intuition that a trajectory is mostly neutral tokens with a few decisive ones. That sparsity is also a warning, because it means fine-grained credit is extremely sensitive to critic error, and the paper shows that the intermediate critic errors in generalized advantage estimation, the standard actor-critic machinery, can become comparable to the underlying credit itself, which is a clean formal explanation of why GAE-based methods struggle in long-horizon settings.

The method that follows is deliberately small. Policy Aligned Critic Training, PACT, changes the update order to actor-then-critic, so the critic is trained against the already-updated policy, which makes an importance-sampling correction available in critic training and keeps the critic aligned with the policy it is actually evaluating. They also train the critic with binary cross entropy instead of mean squared error, which is a better fit for approximating true values that are bounded. Two changes, no new architecture, no new data.

The results are on agentic mathematical reasoning and coding. Across four math benchmarks, AIME 2025, AIME 2026, BeyondAIME, and HMMT November 2025, with a Qwen three-point-five four-billion model, PACT averages seventy-two point eight seven percent accuracy, beating GRPO by eight point eight zero percentage points and PPO by thirteen point one six, with the base model at forty-six point six seven on AIME 2025 and PACT at eighty-three point one two. On SWE-bench Verified, with a much larger coding model, Qwen three-point-six thirty-five-billion active three-billion, PACT reaches a sixty-seven point four pass rate, ahead of PPO, GRPO, and SAO by two point four, two point zero, and three point eight points respectively.

The skeptical read. The characterization result is the real contribution, and it is the part I would trust: proving that three natural axioms pin down a unique token-level credit, and then reading a bunch of algorithmic behaviors, distillation, leave-one-out, GAE's long-horizon fragility, off that one object, is exactly the kind of unifying result the field needs more of, and it does not depend on any empirical claim. The method is correspondingly modest, an update-order change and a loss-function swap, which is both its strength, cheap to adopt, and its limit, the gains cannot be enormous, and indeed the SWE-bench margins, two to four points, are the size you should expect from critic alignment rather than a new algorithm. The math numbers are single-model, single-benchmark-family, and I would want seeds and error bars before reading the eight-to-thirteen-point gaps as more than suggestive, though the direction is consistent. The sparsity result deserves more attention than it gets, because if most tokens carry no credit, then a lot of the machinery in current RL pipelines, per-token advantages, per-token clipping, is operating on a sea of zeros, and a method that spent its capacity there is wasting it. If you run actor-critic RL for agents, the actor-then-critic order is a one-line change worth an ablation. Verdict: a clean uniqueness theorem that finally gives token-level credit a definition, with a small, cheap training procedure whose math-benchmark gains are promising but single-seed and whose coding margins are in the expected range for the change.

The three axioms are worth stating concretely, because a uniqueness result is only as good as its axioms. Completeness says the credit assigned to all tokens in a trajectory, summed, must equal the realized outcome reward, so no credit is created or destroyed along the way, the full outcome is accounted for. Prefix Consistency says the credit for a partial trajectory must be consistent with the credit you would assign if you extended the trajectory further, so the attribution is a well-defined process as the sequence grows, not something that changes your mind retroactively when more tokens arrive. Neutrality is the one that does the heavy lifting: a token that does not change the expected outcome, a neutral step where the future expectation is unchanged regardless of what is emitted, carries zero credit. That last condition is what makes the resulting credit sparse, and it is also the condition that makes the object useful, because it says credit concentrates exactly where decisions actually changed the expected outcome, which is what you want an RL signal to do, reward the tokens that mattered and leave the rest alone. With those three in place, the representation theorem says there is exactly one token-level credit function that satisfies them, and the paper's derivations for distillation and leave-one-out are then applications, showing that two well-known algorithms are, in expectation, implementing that unique object, which is a much stronger claim than the usual, this algorithm works and here is an empirical result.

The GAE fragility result is the piece I would carry into a production conversation. Generalized advantage estimation, the default in actor-critic training, estimates the value at intermediate states and smooths the advantage over a horizon, and the paper shows that the critic's error at those intermediate states can be as large as the underlying credit itself. In a sparse-credit world, where most tokens carry zero and a few carry the whole outcome, that is not a small approximation, it is a statement that the critic is frequently wrong by the full amount of the signal it is supposed to be approximating. That is a formal version of the long-held empirical observation that actor-critic RL for language models is noisy and hard to tune in long-horizon settings, and it explains why the actor-then-critic reordering matters: by training the critic against the updated policy with an importance-sampling correction, PACT keeps the critic's error in a regime where it does not swamp the credit. The binary cross entropy choice is the same idea in the loss: the true value is a bounded quantity, and a cross-entropy loss is the correct fit for a bounded target in a way that squared error is not, so the critic is approximating the right object with the right geometry.

The practical takeaway for someone running agentic RL is that the two changes are separable and cheap. The update order is a matter of the training loop, do the actor step, then train the critic on the new policy with the importance correction, and the loss swap is one line. Neither requires new data, new infrastructure, or a bigger model, and the paper reports the gains with the same setup otherwise, so the attribution to the two changes is clean. The math results are where the effect is largest, eight to thirteen points over GRPO and PPO on four agentic math benchmarks, which is a meaningful margin, and the direction is consistent across benchmarks, though the single-seed caveat applies. The coding result, two to four points on SWE-bench Verified with a much larger model, is the more conservative number and probably the more representative one for production, because coding is the long-horizon, sparse-reward regime where the theory says critic error hurts most, and the fact that the gain is real but modest there is exactly what the theory predicts.
