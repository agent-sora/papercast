Learning to Discover Interesting Mathematics, by Niket Patel, Ahmad Rammal, and
Amaury Hayat, out of FAIR at Meta, New York University, and the CERMICS group
at the Institut Polytechnique de Paris.

The premise is blunt. Large language models can now solve some mathematical
problems that have been open for decades, and they can conjecture and prove
theorems at a scale no human team could match. But the paper asks a question
that has not been asked often: even if a model can prove more theorems, how do
we know any of it is interesting? The authors open on Poincare's warning that
a pile of facts is not a science the way a heap of stones is not a house, and
on Borges's Library of Babel, which contains every book that can be written and
therefore every truth, and is nonetheless useless because no reader could find
meaning among the shelves. That is the failure mode they are trying to avoid. A
machine that emits a million machine-checked theorems has not done mathematics
unless those theorems are worth having.

Their definition of interestingness is deceptively simple. They call a theorem
intrinsically interesting when the length of its proof is large relative to the
length of its statement. A short statement whose proof takes a long time to
establish is interesting; a long statement whose proof is trivial is not. This
is a ratio, the proof length divided by the statement length, and it captures
the intuition that interesting results are the ones that require real work to
establish. They then show this intrinsic ratio correlates strongly with an
extrinsic, downstream measure of utility, which is the important part, because
it means the cheap-to-compute ratio is a proxy for whether the theorem is
actually useful to a growing body of knowledge.

The engine underneath all of it is a model of conditional proof difficulty.
Given a set of premises and a target theorem, how many lines of code does it
cost to prove that theorem from those premises? In Lean 4, with the mathlib
library as the ground truth, this is a concrete, measurable quantity. They
denote it as the number of lines of code required, and they formalize it with a
set of axioms they call the conditional-value axioms, which pin down how the
difficulty should behave when you add premises or introduce intermediate
lemmas. The key quantity is the difficulty of a proof conditioned on a set of
premises, and that single primitive is what lets them compute both
interestingness and utility.

To get data, they mine mathlib itself. They walk the dependency graph of
theorems and proofs, and by moving the visible frontier backward through that
graph they generate theorem, premise, and proof triplets. The median proof in
mathlib is three lines of code, and the median number of times any particular
premise gets reused is one, so the structure is sparse and the frontier-walking
has to be done carefully to get a rich enough dataset. They end up with a
sizeable training set of such triplets, which is the substrate for the next
step.

They fine-tune a 27 billion parameter model, specifically Qwen 3.6 with 27
billion parameters, using group relative policy optimization, for 350 steps.
The reward is designed to match their axioms and has three terms. The first,
the truth term, pulls the predicted difficulty toward the actual line count.
The second, the Bellman term, enforces a recursive consistency: if you
introduce an intermediate lemma, the difficulty of going from the premises to
the target should match the difficulty of going from the premises to the lemma
plus the difficulty of going from the lemma to the target. The third, a drop
term, enforces monotonicity, that adding premises should not make a proof
harder. They evaluate on a held-out set of 4,615 labeled prompts, reporting
mean absolute error and Spearman rank correlation over the parsed, non-negative
answers. All the models they test tend to underestimate how hard proofs are,
but their model is substantially more accurate and better calibrated than the
frontier general-purpose models they compare against.

That predictor is not the end goal, though; it is the instrument. The clever
part of the whole construction is that it lets the model estimate difficulty
without writing the proof first, which is the property you actually need for
discovery, where no proof exists yet. The Bellman term is what buys that. By
enforcing the recursive consistency that the cost from the premises to the
target matches the cost to an intermediate lemma plus the cost from the lemma
to the target, the reward teaches the model the dependency structure of the
proof graph rather than just regressing on a label. That is why the
conditional, premise-relative difficulty is the right primitive: it is a value
function over the library's dependency graph, and value functions are what
let you act before you have the ground truth. The drop term then keeps it
monotone, so the model cannot game the score by pretending a premise made
things harder.

They post-train a model to favor theorems with a high proof-to-
statement ratio, and the result is that the mean interestingness of the output
roughly quadruples. More strikingly, the fraction of generated statements that
are judged to be substantially or fully already contained in mathlib drops from
91.9 percent to 30.6 percent. That is the number to sit with. Before
optimization, nearly all the theorems the model produced were essentially
re-derivations of things mathlib already had, so the model was not discovering
anything, it was memorizing. After optimization, roughly a third still overlap,
but the majority are now outside what the library already contains, which the
authors frame as the creation of more out-of-distribution mathematics.

They also show the system can run as a loop. It generates candidate theorems,
ranks them by the interestingness score, selects the ones worth proving, proves
them, and adds them to the library, which then becomes the new set of premises
for the next round. It is a self-expanding, machine-verified library that
chooses its own next targets without anyone handing it a human-supplied goal.

Now the skeptical read. The interestingness metric is elegant but it is a
single ratio, and it encodes a very particular theory of what makes mathematics
valuable: that hard-to-prove, short statements are the interesting ones. That
is a defensible prior, but it is a prior, and a model optimized against it will
overproduce exactly the kind of result the ratio favors, whatever that turns
out to be. The drop from 91.9 percent to 30.6 percent overlap is the strongest
evidence that the objective actually moves the model out of the memorization
regime, but a lower overlap with mathlib is not the same as more useful
mathematics; it is consistent with producing more genuinely interesting
theorems and also with producing more obscure, low-value ones. The correlation
between the intrinsic ratio and the extrinsic utility measure is what justifies
treating the ratio as a stand-in for utility, and that is the load-bearing
claim, so it is the one I would want to see on a bigger, more diverse set of
theorems before trusting it. The 4,615-prompt validation set and the single 27
billion parameter model mean the calibration is narrow, and the fact that every
model underestimates difficulty, even the tuned one, suggests the whole
difficulty estimate is biased low and the interestingness ranking inherits that
bias.

Bottom line: this is a clean, well-motivated recipe for turning an
unsupervised, machine-checkable signal into a reward that measurably pushes a
model toward novel theorems, and the mathlib-overlap collapse is real evidence
it is not just paraphrasing the library. The open question is whether the
interestingness ratio tracks what mathematicians actually value, and until that
correlation is shown to hold beyond the algebra and analysis that mathlib
already covers, the self-expanding library is a promising instrument with an
unvalidated compass.
