Improving and Expanding Video Editing Capabilities Without Videos, from Ji Xie, Yanhao Jia, and Xiangpeng Yang, at Carnegie Mellon University, Nanyang Technological University, the University of Technology Sydney, and the University of Michigan. The claim: a pretrained video editor can be improved and extended to brand-new editing tasks using only single images, no paired source-and-edited videos, and no architectural changes.

The bottleneck in video editing is data. Image editing models follow a wide range of instructions, but video editors still lag, in part because collecting paired source and edited videos is hard. Each pair is expensive to produce and hard to keep temporally consistent, so the editing tasks a video model can do are limited by the videos someone managed to annotate. The paper's question is whether the abundant, cheap supervision of image editing can be transferred to a video editor at all. The answer is Image-to-Video Transfer, abbreviated I2VT. The key idea is to let the video editor role-play as an image editor. It edits an image that it treats as a one-frame video, with a frozen image editor guiding its predictions at each denoising step. The video editor is fine-tuned directly on single source images along its own denoising trajectory, which puts it in the on-policy distillation family rather than the offline distillation family. No video targets are constructed, no artificial motion is engineered, and the core architecture is not altered. At test time the same model applies the improved and expanded capability to whole video clips while preserving temporal consistency, even though it only ever saw single images during training. The prior-art context matters here, because several approaches train video editors without edited targets, and I2VT is positioned against them. Some use an image editor plus an image-to-video model to generate the rest of the clip, but where their guidance is weak the model regenerates the motion instead of keeping the source motion, and content not visible in the first frame gets hallucinated. Others train flow-matching editors from the base model's own cues without any external teacher. I2VT's distinction is that it uses one frozen image editor as an external teacher, which lets it teach edits the base video model does not yet make well, such as edits that change how a subject moves, while still not needing a single paired video.

The training loop is the implementable core. The frozen image editor is the teacher, and the video editor is the student. At each step of the student's own denoising trajectory, the image editor computes its velocity prediction on the same noisy state, and the student is supervised to match it. There are three velocity terms in the loss, the teacher velocity, the student velocity, and a rollout velocity, and the matching happens on the student's own samples rather than on precomputed teacher outputs, which is exactly the distinction from offline distillation. Offline distillation would fine-tune the video editor on the image editor's pre-computed edit results, a fixed target distribution. I2VT instead lets the teacher react to wherever the student currently is, so the supervision stays on the student's own distribution. Classifier-free guidance is used, with separate guidance scales for the image-conditioned and video-conditioned paths, and a user-facing instruction for the image is paired with the corresponding video instruction, for example, make the person jump becomes make the person jump throughout the video.

The evaluation has two parts. For improving existing capabilities, the authors take three pretrained video editors and measure a basic-edit score before and after I2VT fine-tuning, using Gemini 2.5 Pro as the judge on their benchmark, OpenVE-Bench, which covers the standard editing families of style, replacement, color, addition, and removal. All three improve: the scores rise from 3.10 to 3.48, from 2.29 to 3.19, and from 3.33 to 3.68. One of the improved editors also reaches state-of-the-art performance on the benchmark against prior editors. The gains are uneven by category; one editor's local-addition score is essentially unchanged, moving from 2.50 to 2.51, even as its overall score approaches that of a stronger base model. That variance is a useful signal that image supervision helps most where the base video model already has a partial capability to strengthen. For expanding capabilities, they introduce an extension of the benchmark that adds four new editing tasks useful for video production, action change, expression change, body movement, and scene relighting. These are the tasks the video editors could not previously do, and the edits that change how a subject moves across frames are precisely the ones that demand temporal consistency. The reported result is that the temporal quality of the edited videos stays on par with the base editors, which is the claim that makes the whole approach safe: the image-derived supervision does not buy the new ability at the cost of breaking the motion.

The comparison against offline distillation is the controlled result that matters. In matched comparisons, where both methods get the same image editing supervision, I2VT outperforms offline distillation. The reason is the distribution shift that offline distillation has: the teacher's precomputed edits define a fixed target, but the student at test time produces its own noisy trajectory, so the offline targets are off-distribution for the student's actual states. I2VT avoids that by supervising on the student's own samples. The user study reports that the model's scores align with human judgments, which supports using the automated judge for the headline numbers, and the method is compared against editors that train without edited targets, where it is distinguished by using a single frozen image editor and no second teacher, while still keeping temporal quality on par with the base editors.

The limitations are worth stating plainly. The new motion-editing tasks are exactly where the residual failures live. The supplementary material shows that on body-movement edits the model can still freeze a person after lowering a hand, or replace an entire dance when asked to rotate a torso by a small angle. The mechanism is structural: I2VT builds no video targets, so the student learns the edit from single frames and relies entirely on the pretrained video editor for the motion. Where the base editor's motion prior is weak, the edit inherits that weakness. The evaluation also leans on a single vision-language judge for the main scores, and the new-task benchmark is the authors' own extension, so the cross-task generalization claim rests on their benchmark design. And the method is only as broad as the image editor's capability; it can teach the video model edits the image model already does well, including edits the base video model does not yet make, but it cannot invent editing semantics that neither the image teacher nor the video student already has. The cost structure is worth stating, because it is the whole pitch: the supervision is image pairs, which are cheap to generate at scale with a frozen frontier image editor, and the video model never sees a single video of its own during fine-tuning, so the data pipeline is an image pipeline with a video inference test at the end. If your video editor is underperforming on the edit classes it should already handle, and you do not have a large curated video-edit dataset, this is a cheaper path to the same capability than collecting video.

Bottom line: a clean on-policy distillation result that makes image editing supervision a practical data source for video editors, with matched comparisons over offline distillation and temporal quality held flat. I would trust the distillation mechanism, re-verify the motion-edit failures on my own clips, and treat the judge-based scores as indicative rather than absolute. The bigger question it raises is whether image supervision is the right default data source for video editors in general, and the matched comparisons here are the strongest evidence yet that it is.
