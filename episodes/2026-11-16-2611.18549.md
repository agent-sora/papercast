The paper is JVT, for Just Video Transformer: Just Video Transformer makes pixel-space video diffusion simple, by Jianjun Zhou, Hua Geng, and Haoyu Guo, from Zhejiang University, the Shanghai Innovation Institute, and the Shanghai AI Laboratory. The claim is in the title, and it is a real one: a deliberately simple DiT-style transformer, run directly on raw pixels, can do video diffusion without any of the machinery the field has accumulated. No pretrained autoencoder, no auxiliary detailer networks, no cascaded stages, no U-Net-style skip connections.

The context. Video generation is dominated by latent diffusion: train an autoencoder to compress video into a compact latent space, then run a diffusion transformer on the latents. The efficiency gain is real, but the autoencoder is a ceiling. It imposes reconstruction error, it adds training and inference cost, and it limits what you can jointly model, because everything has to fit through the same RGB-tuned bottleneck, and a modality the autoencoder was never trained to compress has no clean representation to denoise in. Image generation has already made the case that pixel-space diffusion with a simple transformer can be competitive, and JVT is the video version of that argument. The research question is how far a simple DiT-style backbone can go in direct pixel-space video generation, and the secondary question is whether the pixel-space formulation unlocks something latent space cannot.

The architecture is genuinely minimal, and the paper spends real effort on the small design choices that make it work. The objective is the same as the recent JiT work: predict the clean video from noisy pixels under a rectified-flow velocity loss. The noisy input is a linear interpolation between the clean video and noise, the network predicts the clean video, and the loss is on the implied velocity. The key component is the video patch embedding, because in pixel space the patch embedding is what provides the initial downsampling that latent DiTs get for free from the autoencoder. They compare plain tubelet embedding, a tubelet with a channel bottleneck, a patch-first arrangement, and a frame-first arrangement. The channel bottleneck projects each patch to a compact intermediate dimension and then expands it back to the full hidden size, which trades a little capacity for a much shorter token sequence. The bottleneck sweep is not monotonic: too narrow and the embedding discards task-relevant spatiotemporal information, too wide and the extra bandwidth buys little, so the choice is a real quality-efficiency tradeoff rather than a free lunch. With a temporal downsampling factor of 4, the denoiser ends up handling 1,024 visual tokens for a 16-frame 256 by 256 video, which is the regime where a plain DiT can run on a single consumer GPU. They also study register tokens, 32 of them prepended to the visual tokens after the first 9 blocks and discarded at the end, and adaptive layer normalization for carrying the noise level and class condition. The ablations find that patch-first embedding is the best quality-efficiency tradeoff, and that standard adaptive layer normalization is the right default conditioning; removing it without compensating parameters degrades the results.

The second and more interesting contribution is the cross-modal one, and it is the payoff of working in pixel space. Because everything is generated on a common pixel grid, you can jointly generate pixel-aligned modalities without squeezing them through an RGB-specific autoencoder. They demonstrate this with camera-conditioned generation: given an initial RGB frame and a target camera trajectory, the model jointly generates the RGB frames and the disparity map. The disparity converts, by plain geometric unprojection, into a sequence of colored point clouds in world coordinates, with no additional reconstruction model at inference. That is explicit 4D scene geometry coming straight out of the generator.

The results. On camera-conditioned generation over RealEstate10K, the joint RGB-disparity model reports an FVD of 27.5, a relative rotation accuracy of 99.9, a relative translation accuracy of 79.5 to 98.4 across the evaluation horizons, and an AUC of 58.6 to 88.6, and the paper reports that joint generation improves camera following relative to the RGB-only JVT, with a small FVD tradeoff. The unconditional quality results are measured against prior pixel-space and latent methods on UCF-101, FaceForensics, SkyTimelapse, and Taichi-HD. The most informative number in the unconditional ablations is the register guidance result: the register-free baseline sits at an FVD around 413 on UCF-101, and register guidance substantially improves it, but on FaceForensics, SkyTimelapse, and Taichi-HD the same mechanism brings marginal or no improvement. The authors' own read is that register guidance is a dataset-dependent gain that helps the most diverse dataset and is nearly inert on the more uniform ones, which is a useful and honest calibration for anyone deciding whether to include the mechanism. The efficiency measurement deserves its own paragraph because it is unusually careful: every configuration is timed on a single NVIDIA 4090, in a fresh Python process per case, with 20 warm-up iterations and 100 timed iterations for denoiser latency, and the whole matrix is repeated in three independent sweeps. A full 50-step Euler sample of a 16-frame 256 by 256 video takes around 740 to 800 milliseconds without guidance, rising to about 1,200 to 1,300 with classifier-free guidance and about 2,000 with both guidance sources, at peak memory around 2.7 gigabytes. Those are single-consumer-GPU numbers, and they are the part of the paper that makes the pixel-space claim concrete rather than rhetorical: the model is small enough that you can actually measure it on hardware you can buy.

The skeptical assessment. First, the scale. This is a research-scale model doing pixel-space video at modest resolution, and the honest reading is that this is a study of what the architecture can do, not a deployment model. The efficiency argument cuts both ways: removing the autoencoder saves a stage, but denoising in pixel space at video resolution is far more compute than denoising in latent space, and the paper's quality claims are competitive, not superior, at this scale. Whether the gap closes or widens as you scale up is exactly the open question, and the paper frames it that way. Second, the quality metrics are standard FVD-style numbers on standard datasets, and FVD is known to be noisy and not fully aligned with human perception, so the competitive-with-latent claim should be taken at face value but not over-weighted. Third, the cross-modal result is a demonstration on one modality pair, RGB plus disparity, and the point-cloud quality is downstream of the disparity quality, which the paper does not analyze as deeply as it does the generation metrics.

What I would trust: the design ablations, because they are systematic and the conclusions are conservative; patch-first embedding and standard AdaLN are safe defaults if you build your own pixel-space video model. What I would trust: the cross-modal insight, that pixel space makes pixel-aligned joint generation a native extension, because that is a structural property, not a tuning artifact. What I would re-verify: the compute cost per sample against a comparable latent DiT at matched quality, and the disparity-to-point-cloud quality, before treating this as a path to 4D content generation. One more practical note for implementers: because the paper is unusually complete on the measurement side, the single-GPU latency and memory tables are reusable as reference baselines when you tune your own patch size or guidance budget, which is more than most video-diffusion papers let you do.

Two-sentence verdict: JVT is a clean, well-ablated demonstration that a simple DiT-style transformer can do pixel-space video diffusion without autoencoders, detailers, or skip connections, matching latent baselines at research scale, with single-GPU measurements complete enough to serve as a reference for your own experiments. Its durable contribution is the observation that pixel space makes joint generation of pixel-aligned modalities native, and the RGB-disparity-to-point-cloud result is a credible first example of that.
