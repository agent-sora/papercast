The Resolution Hypothesis: Agent Evolution's Key Is Identifying Improvements, by Yuanhao Zeng, Shaocheng Lan, and Ao Lu, at ShanghaiTech University and ByteDance, is a diagnosis of why agent evolution, the loop where a proposer writes candidate artifacts like prompts, skills, and memories and a selector keeps the ones that score higher, keeps failing to bootstrap on some tasks and trading wins across papers. The hypothesis is that evolution stalls not because the proposer cannot find better artifacts, but because the evaluation is too noisy to reliably identify them. The evidence is large: seven evolution methods, six agent models, over 10 million LLM calls, 150 configurations, 1,030 independent evolution runs, and over 2.1 million test trajectories. The practical implication is a shift in where the field should spend its effort, from designing ever-better proposal mechanisms to making true improvements detectable and accumulable.

The paper's framing is worth unpacking because it reframes a problem most teams would attribute to a different cause. The standard story in agent evolution is that the proposer is the weak link, that the candidate artifacts, the prompts, the skills, the memories, are not good enough, and the fix is a smarter proposer that generates better candidates. The Resolution Hypothesis inverts this: the proposer is fine, the candidates it generates are genuinely helpful, and the failure is downstream, in the selector that cannot reliably tell a helpful artifact from a harmful one because the evaluation signal is too noisy. That inversion matters because it points the engineering effort in a different direction. If you believe the proposer is the problem, you spend your time building a better generator. If you believe the selector is the problem, you spend your time making the evaluation more reliable, and the paper's concrete output is a formula for how reliable that evaluation needs to be. It is also a diagnosis that explains a recurring pattern in the literature, namely that different papers report different evolution methods as the best, and the Resolution Hypothesis explains that as a symptom of noise: the method rankings are unstable because the margins between methods are smaller than the run-to-run variance, so which method wins on any given comparison is often just which run got lucky. The paper's remedy is not a new proposer or a new selector architecture, it is a statistical budget, a minimum number of evaluations per candidate that scales with the measured noise, which is a much cheaper and more transferable contribution than a new algorithm, because any team running an evolution loop can adopt the sample-size calculation regardless of what proposer or selector they use.

The paper distinguishes two levels where evaluation scores are used, and finds failure at both. At the candidate level, the selector chooses which artifact to keep each round. The finding is that helpful candidate artifacts genuinely exist in the population, so the proposer is not the bottleneck, but the selection mechanism is no better than random at separating helpful artifacts from harmful ones. Concretely, on the text-artifact methods, the selector picks the genuinely best candidate only about 33 percent of the time, barely above a coin flip once you account for the candidate pool size, and because each round accepts whatever it picks, errors accumulate across rounds: a selector that is slightly worse than chance in aggregate does not stay slightly worse, it compounds, and the trajectory of the evolved agent degrades over rounds instead of improving. At the method level, the paper ranks the seven evolution methods against each other, and finds that the typical margin between methods, a median of 3.4 percentage points with an interquartile range of 1.2 to 6.6, is smaller than the difference between two independent runs of the same method. For every method, 53 to 79 percent of its pairwise comparisons against other methods fall within the noise band of run-to-run variance. So published rankings of which evolution method is best, a large literature in itself, often rest on luck, and the variance measurement behind that claim is concrete: evaluation noise ranges from 1.0 to 3.7 percentage points on some benchmarks and reaches 4.5 on Terminal-Bench, while between-run variance ranges up to 12.3 percentage points.

There is a constructive side. The paper derives the statistical cost of doing selection right: to identify a true margin at 80 percent power with a 0.05 false positive rate, the required number of evaluation samples scales with the noise, and the paper computes the required sample sizes per benchmark, 19 on OfficeQA, 19 on ALFWorld, 23 on Terminal-Bench, and 181 on the hardest configuration. That number, 181 independent evaluations per comparison on the noisiest benchmark, is the paper's most actionable output: it is a concrete recipe for making selection reliable, and it explains why methods that evaluate each candidate once or a few times are structurally unable to distinguish signal from noise. The deeper point is that the noise is not an artifact of a particular harness; it is intrinsic to the agent setting, where a single rollout can succeed or fail on the basis of a stochastic model decision, so the only way to estimate an artifact's true effect is to average over many rollouts, and the sample size is the price of that averaging. The paper frames this as a call to treat evaluation noise as a first-class design variable, the way a hardware designer treats signal-to-noise ratio, rather than as an implementation detail, and to make improvements accumulable, meaning a win this round should not be undone by a noisy rejection next round.

The skeptical reading. First, this is a negative-result paper, and its value depends on the robustness of the noise measurements across the six models, which span GPT 5.4 nano, GPT 5.6 Luna and Terra, DeepSeek V4 Flash, Kimi K3, and GLM 5.2, a reasonable spread but one where the noise structure could differ for models not tested, especially smaller ones where evaluation variance is typically higher. Second, the conclusion that selection is near-random is a statement about the selectors as implemented in these seven methods, not a proof that no selector can work; the paper's own remedy, more evaluations per candidate, is a cost increase that some methods may not afford, and the tradeoff between selection reliability and evaluation budget is exactly the tension the paper identifies but does not fully resolve. Third, the 1,030 runs are over 150 configurations, which is thorough, but the benchmarks are the standard agent suites, ALFWorld, SpreadsheetBench, OfficeQA, and Terminal-Bench, and the noise characteristics of novel or long-tail tasks are not measured. Fourth, the paper's own cost is substantial, over 10 million LLM calls and 400 thousand CPU core-hours, so the finding that evaluation is the bottleneck is itself demonstrated by a very large evaluation budget, and a team without that budget faces a harder version of the problem.

For implementation: if you are building an evolving agent, the paper's direct advice is to measure your evaluation noise first, run the same configuration multiple times and compute the run-to-run variance, then size your per-candidate evaluation budget so that the true margins you care about exceed that variance at acceptable power. The two specific mechanisms to adopt are (1) multi-sample selection, evaluating each candidate artifact multiple times and selecting on the mean, and (2) tracking whether your selector beats random, which the paper shows is a cheap, high-value diagnostic. If your selector's hit rate on the true-best candidate is near chance, no amount of proposal-side improvement will save the loop.

To summarize the bottom line. The Resolution Hypothesis is a well-evidenced, large-scale diagnosis that agent evolution is bottlenecked by evaluation noise rather than proposal quality, with concrete measurements of that noise and concrete sample-size formulas for fixing it. Its limits are that it characterizes current selectors and standard benchmarks, and its remedy costs evaluation budget. If your evolving agent is not compounding gains over rounds, the paper's first recommendation, measure the noise and check whether your selection is better than random, is worth doing before you invest in a better proposer.
