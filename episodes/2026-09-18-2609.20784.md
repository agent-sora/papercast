RetireOPD: Self-Retiring On-Policy Distillation for Agentic Reinforcement Learning. The first three authors are Yan Yu, Zhengxi Lu, and Yizhou Liu, from Zhejiang University, with the remaining authors also at Zhejiang University and Alibaba Group. Code is released on GitHub under the ZJU-REAL organization.

The starting point is a limitation of reinforcement learning for multi-turn agents. An agent trained with RL receives a single scalar reward per trajectory, which is dense in information only at the very end of a long episode and sparse everywhere else. On-policy distillation, OPD, is the standard remedy: you introduce a self-teacher that has access to privileged task skills, and it produces token-level supervision that the skill-free student can use to internalize those skills. The student learns from dense per-token signals instead of one terminal reward, and it does not need the privileged information at inference time. The paper's premise is that this recipe, taken as-is, is undermined by two findings the authors establish in agentic tasks. First, privileged information alone does not make the teacher reliable: a teacher that has access to extra context does not automatically produce a correct supervision signal, because the extra context does not necessarily translate into better token-level decisions. Second, the benefit of the teacher is stage-dependent: it helps early in training and stops helping, or even hurts, later.

The proposed method, RetireOPD, addresses both findings. It first optimizes a decoupled, skill-conditioned teacher using environment rewards, so the teacher is not just a frozen privileged model but a policy that has been trained to actually use its skills well. Then it trains a skill-free student jointly with RL, specifically GRPO, and on-policy distillation from that optimized teacher. The key mechanism is what the authors call Adaptive Retirement. Instead of following a predefined distillation schedule, a fixed number of steps after which the teacher is dropped, the student drops the teacher on its own, based on two conditions. The first is that the discrepancy between the student and the teacher stops shrinking, meaning the student has stopped learning from the teacher's token-level signal. The second is that the student has reached a target fraction of the teacher's success rate. Once both conditions are met, the teacher supervision is retired and training continues with RL alone. The distillation coefficient is a hyperparameter, lambda, and the retirement thresholds are gamma and delta, and the paper's ablation shows that varying either threshold shifts the selected transition step from 55 to 95 but leaves success rates essentially unchanged, so the mechanism is robust to the exact threshold values rather than requiring precise calibration.

The results are across three model scales of Qwen2.5, 1.5 billion, 3 billion, and 7 billion parameters, on two agentic benchmarks: ALFWorld, an embodied household task environment with six subtasks, and WebShop, a web-based shopping environment. The main comparison is against GRPO alone, which is the pure RL baseline with no distillation. On ALFWorld, RetireOPD outperforms GRPO by 18.8 percentage points on the 3 billion model, 93.8 percent versus 75.0 percent, with gains of 17.0 and 14.1 points on the 1.5 billion and 7 billion models. On WebShop, the gains are 19.0 points on the 1.5 billion model, 75.8 percent versus 56.8 percent for GRPO, and 14.0 and 11.8 points on the 3 billion and 7 billion models. RetireOPD also consistently outperforms a variant called GiGPO, a group-in-group policy optimization method, across both tasks and all three scales. The paper's broader claim is that RetireOPD outperforms not just pure RL but also continuous joint optimization of GRPO and OPD without retirement, and it also outperforms its own skill-conditioned teacher, in every setting. That last point is worth emphasizing: the skill-free student, trained with the adaptive-retirement recipe, ends up better than the privileged teacher that supervised it, which is the desired property of a distillation pipeline but not a guaranteed one.

The reliable-teacher finding is the one with the most direct engineering consequence. The paper compares RetireOPD against a variant, GRPO plus OPSD, where the teacher is given privileged information but is not explicitly optimized as a policy. The result is that RetireOPD substantially outperforms it, and the authors' interpretation is that privileged information is necessary but not sufficient for a reliable supervision target. A teacher that can see the ground-truth task state but has not been trained to act on it produces token-level signals that are sometimes wrong, and the student learns those wrong signals. The fix, decoupling the teacher's optimization from the student's training and training the teacher first with environment rewards, is a small architectural change with a large effect, and it is the kind of thing that is easy to miss if you treat the privileged teacher as a free oracle.

What would I trust? The two foundational findings, that the privileged teacher is not automatically reliable and that its benefit is stage-dependent, are the real contribution, and they are established with a controlled comparison against a no-teacher-optimization baseline. The Adaptive Retirement mechanism is also well-motivated and the threshold robustness result, success rates stable across a 55 to 95 step transition range, is exactly the kind of evidence you want for a mechanism that is going to be deployed without careful per-task tuning. The consistent gains across three scales and two tasks, and the student-beats-teacher result, make this a credible and reproducible method, and the released code makes it straightforward to verify.

What would I re-verify before adopting? The benchmarks are both agentic but both are relatively narrow: ALFWorld is a household simulation and WebShop is a shopping environment, and the 1.5 to 7 billion parameter scale is small by current standards. I would want to see the method on a longer-horizon, higher-stakes agentic task, something with a richer action space and a longer trajectory, because the stage-dependence of teacher benefit that motivates retirement might look different when the trajectories are ten times longer and the terminal reward is even sparser. The student-beats-teacher result is the one I would look at most carefully, because it depends on the teacher being a good but not perfect signal, and if the teacher is too weak the student will not surpass it, and if the teacher is too strong and too reliable the retirement condition might never trigger cleanly. I would want to see the teacher's success rate trajectory relative to the student's across training, not just the final numbers, to confirm the retirement is happening at the right place. The GRPO baseline is the right comparison, but I would also want to see a comparison against a fixed-schedule distillation where the teacher is dropped at a tuned step, because the Adaptive Retirement claim is that it beats a schedule, and a well-tuned schedule is a stronger baseline than pure RL alone. And the two benchmarks, ALFWorld and WebShop, both have a relatively small action vocabulary compared to the kind of agentic workloads that are driving current RL investment, so the transfer to a general-purpose tool-use agent is an open question.

One practical note: the method is a drop-in modification to a GRPO training loop, and the teacher is a separate optimized policy, so the compute cost is roughly double a standard RL run during the distillation phase, then single after retirement. For teams already running GRPO for agentic tasks, the implementation cost is low and the reported gains are large enough to justify trying it, with the caveat that the gains are measured on the two benchmarks in the paper and at the scales tested, and the retirement thresholds should be verified on your own task distribution before you trust the default values.

Bottom line: RetireOPD is a small, well-motivated change to agentic RL that addresses two real failure modes of on-policy distillation, and its reported gains of 14 to 19 percentage points over pure GRPO across three model scales and two benchmarks, with the student consistently surpassing its own teacher, are a strong result for the method's complexity. The main open question is whether the stage-dependent retirement behavior and the student-beats-teacher property hold on longer-horizon, higher-stakes agentic workloads at larger scales, and that is the experiment I would run before building a production pipeline on it.
