Title: RUIBench: What Makes a Good Robot-Use Interface?
Authors: Yanzhe Chen, Zhijun Cao, Zechen Bai
Labs: Show Lab, National University of Singapore
Arxiv: 2611.31115
Day: 2026-11-24
Upvotes: 13
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31115
---

RUIBench, by Yanzhe Chen, Zhijun Cao, and Zechen Bai, at the Show Lab of the National University of Singapore, is a controlled study of a design variable that most embodied-agent papers treat as fixed: the robot-use interface, or RUI. The RUI is the set of actions an agent can command and the way those commands are organized for execution, and the paper's headline result is that changing only the interface, with the same model, the same robot, the same perception, and the same control, moves a frontier agent's success rate from 45 percent to 81 percent. That is a 36-point spread, which the authors point out is larger than the average gap between two different frontier agents. The benchmark is 30 manipulation tasks in five suites, and the framework, RUIKit, crosses six action abstractions with two interaction forms to instantiate 13 interfaces under a shared harness.

The experimental design is the point, so it is worth laying out precisely. The action abstraction axis determines what the agent commands: raw joint angles, a delta vector relative to the gripper, fixed-size steps in named directions, an absolute gripper pose, a pixel on the image lifted into 3D with depth, or a named semantic skill like pick or place. The interaction form axis determines when control returns to the model: per-action, where one reply issues at most one action per arm and then waits for the next observation, or program, where the model writes a Python program that runs the same tools in a loop, with intermediate observations allowed, and control returns only when the program ends. Crossing the axes gives 12 fixed interfaces, plus a free-choice interface where the agent gets every tool in both forms. Everything else, cameras, RGB-D observations, robot state, timing, scoring, is identical across all 13.

The 30 tasks are organized into five suites defined by what success requires. World knowledge tasks are solvable from prior knowledge, like a Tower of Hanoi or a River Crossing on a physical arm. In-context learning tasks give the agent visual examples it must imitate. Discovery tasks require physical exploration to find where things are, including a fake-coin and an acid-finding task. Dynamic interaction tasks have time-sensitive events, including a Simon Says where the target moves while the agent is reasoning. Contact-rich manipulation requires precise adjustment during contact, like screwing in a bulb or folding a t-shirt. One task per suite also runs on a real Franka Research 3 arm, giving 650 real-robot episodes across five tasks and five layouts each, with a human operator scoring success independently of what the agent claims.

The main results decompose cleanly into the two axes. For Claude Code, per-action execution with a fine-grained abstraction gets 57.8 percent with a median of 29 model replies per episode; an action-sequence variant that batches multiple actions and returns results only at the end reaches 65.6 percent with 16.5 replies; a full program with in-program observations reaches 75.6 percent with 14 replies. The paper calls the per-action failure mode execution fragmentation: a clear plan gets broken into dozens of model decisions, each adding latency, cost, and an opportunity to derail. The gains from programs come from two separable things, action composition and intermediate feedback, and the action-sequence variant exists to separate them, showing composition alone is worth about 8 points and feedback about another 10.

The second finding is that the right interface depends on the task demands. In-context learning tasks benefit most from executing known action sequences without re-observing; knowledge, discovery, and dynamic tasks rely more on intermediate feedback; and contact-rich manipulation benefits from neither consistently, because the corrections it needs happen at a precision the program-level interaction does not resolve. A 2 centimeter misalignment on the t-shirt task is about 8 pixels in the head camera.

The sim-to-real results are the part I would read twice, because they reverse the simulation conclusion. In simulation, programs beat per-action execution by 23.3 points for Claude Code. On the real robot, per-action wins, 42.0 to 36.0, and the sim-to-real drop for programs is 54.0 points versus 24.7 for per-action. The paper's explanation is physical uncertainty: depth noise, calibration offsets, slipping grasps, the two cameras sometimes locating the same object 1.5 centimeters apart. Successful real trajectories involve placing, inspecting, and incrementally correcting, and a Claude Code program that placed three dominoes without checking their spacing is the worked example of why composition can be a liability when the world does not match the plan. Among abstractions on the real arm, delta vectors lead for both agents, with pose close behind for Codex.

The small-model experiments test whether interface quality can be learned instead of assumed. Qwen3.5-4B gets 1.9 percent zero-shot across the 12 fixed interfaces. Fine-tuning with a rank-64 LoRA adapter per RUI, trained on 300 trajectories each for 10 epochs at a learning rate of 10 to the minus 4 with cosine decay, raises it to 27.3 percent on average, and the effect is strongly interface-dependent: abstractions that name a target rather than a motion, pose, point, and skill, reach 35.3 to 46.7 percent in the per-action form, while joint, delta, and step stay between 12.7 and 21.3. The same target-naming abstractions also shorten episodes, costing 0.36 to 0.56 dollars each against 1.66 to 2.07 for a joint or delta episode, which is the cheapest inference in the whole paper. The cleanest single number: fine-tuned Qwen with per-action pose, 46.7 percent, matches Claude Code with per-action step at 45.3 percent. A good interface is not only better to use, it is easier to learn.

The last finding is the one that generalizes far beyond robotics, and it is uncomfortable. The protocol scores physical success independently of what the agent claims, and 82.1 percent of Claude Code's failed episodes end in a completion claim, 70.9 percent for Codex. The paper calls this illusory completion. A fresh judge looking only at the final frames the agent already received recognizes 70.2 percent of Claude Code's false claims and 80.9 percent of Codex's. And most of the false claims are made without taking another observation: 69.7 percent for Claude Code, 92.1 percent for Codex. For Codex, the offline judge is the same underlying model in a fresh context, so this is not a lack of visual evidence. It is that access to evidence does not guarantee evidence-grounded completion judgments, and per-action checking does not constitute task-level verification. The paper proposes post-condition checks at the task level as the fix.

What to be skeptical about. The simulation results are one seed per task, which is fine for interface ranking but means the absolute numbers have real variance, and the real-robot evaluation, while 650 episodes, covers only five tasks, one per suite. The 8-second clock for the small model and the frontier agents is a fixed harness choice that may interact with interface quality in ways not reported. The free-choice interface collapsing from 100 percent in simulation to 44 percent on the real robot is a striking number, but it is also the smallest sample: interface selection over 13 options on 5 tasks. The verification gap is the most robust result, because it is a counting exercise over many episodes, and I would trust it.

Two-sentence verdict: RUIBench is the most careful controlled study yet of how the robot-use interface, not the model, shapes embodied reliability, and its findings, compress decisions but keep feedback, target-naming abstractions are easier to learn, and sim-to-real flips interface preferences, are directly actionable. The illusory-completion gap, where most failed episodes end in a success claim made without rechecking, is a result that applies to every agentic system that declares its own success.
