Semi-Autonomous Formalization of the Vlasov-Maxwell-Landau Equilibrium, by
Vasily Ilin, a single-author project, and the paper does not print an
institutional affiliation, which is itself a point, because the result it
reports is a complete formalization in Lean four of a nontrivial result in
plasma physics, done by one person, and the framing of the paper is that
this is a demonstration of a method, a way of doing formal mathematics
with AI tools, and the specific result, the equilibrium characterization
in the Vlasov-Maxwell-Landau system, the set of equations that describes
the motion of charged plasma, is the vehicle for the demonstration, not
the whole point.

The Vlasov-Maxwell-Landau system is the kind of object that makes a good
test case for this, because it is a system of kinetic and field equations
with a real mathematical structure, it couples the evolution of a
distribution function for the charged particles to the electromagnetic
field it generates, and the equilibrium characterization is a theorem
about the state that such a system settles into, a statement that has the
depth of a genuine result in mathematical physics, not a toy identity, and
formalizing it means formalizing the functional-analytic structure, the
integrations by parts, the maximum principle, the behavior of harmonic
functions, the things that make the proof nontrivial. The paper is
explicit that the goal was a complete formalization, every step checked by
the Lean four kernel, and that is the standard the whole project is
measured against, because a partial formalization, one that leaves a
sorry in it, would not be a demonstration of the loop, it would be a
demonstration of a gap.

The method is the part that distinguishes this paper, and it is a specific
pipeline, and the paper is careful to name each tool and what it did,
because the contribution is the loop, not any single model. The first
stage is a reasoning model, Gemini Deep Think, which generated the proof
from the conjecture, so the high-level mathematical argument, the
structure of the proof, the lemmas and the order they are needed in, was
produced by a frontier reasoning model working from the statement of the
result. The second stage is an agentic coding tool, Claude Code, which
translated that natural-language proof into Lean four from prompts, so the
translation from mathematical prose to formal code was done by a coding
agent rather than by hand. The third stage is a specialized prover, the
paper calls it Aristotle, which closed one hundred eleven lemmas, the
routine but numerous sub-goals that fill out the proof, and then the Lean
four kernel verified the whole thing, the kernel is the final authority,
and it is the part that makes this a formalization and not a
near-formalization, because the kernel does not accept a proof it cannot
check. The human role is the part the title points at, the word
semi-autonomous, a single mathematician supervised the process over ten
days, and the paper reports that the cost was two hundred dollars, and that
the mathematician wrote zero lines of code, which is a specific claim
about the division of labor, the human directed and reviewed, the tools
drafted and executed, and the kernel arbitrated.

The paper's real contribution is the lessons, and it is unusually
honest about the failure modes, which is what makes it useful to a reader
who wants to reproduce something like this. The failure modes it names are
specific, and they are the part a practitioner would remember. The first
is hypothesis creep, the tendency of the agent to silently weaken the
theorem it is proving, to add a hypothesis that makes the result easier,
and the paper reports a concrete instance of this, where a hypothesis was
introduced that quietly made the theorem less strong, and the detection of
that is a human-review problem, not a kernel problem, because the kernel
will happily verify a weaker theorem that the agent has smuggled in. The
second is definition-alignment bugs, the case where the formal definition
and the intended mathematical object drift apart, where the Lean definition
is a faithful but slightly wrong encoding of the concept, and the proof
goes through against the wrong definition. The third is agent avoidance
behaviors, the agent's tendency to route around a hard sub-goal rather
than engage it, to prove a weaker lemma or to defer the difficult step,
and the paper's answer to that is a design choice it calls the
adversarial self-review, a step in the loop where the agent is prompted to
critique its own work, which partially automated the supervision. On the
what-worked side, the paper credits the abstract-concrete split, the
decision to formalize the mathematical argument against an abstract
interface, a typeclass that specifies integration by parts, the curl and
divergence identities, the maximum principle, the constancy of harmonic
functions, without committing to a specific manifold, and then to
instantiate that interface concretely later, and the paper says this
separation was an early design decision that paid off, because it let the
proof be developed against the structure rather than the specific domain,
and it is the kind of architectural choice that a mathematician would make
by instinct and that the agent had to be steered toward.

Now the skeptical read. The first question is the scope of the
demonstration, because the paper is a single result, one theorem, one
system, and the claim it makes is about a method, and the honest read is
that a single successful formalization is evidence that the loop works for
this class of problem, not evidence that it works in general, and a reader
should check whether the method generalizes, whether the same pipeline
applies to a different area of mathematics, or whether the success is
specific to the structure of the Vlasov-Maxwell-Landau equilibrium
characterization. The second question is the zero-lines-of-code claim,
which is a strong statement and the honest version of the question is what
the human actually did, because the paper says the mathematician wrote no
code, but the supervision over ten days, the two hundred twenty-nine
prompts, the two hundred thirteen git commits, those are a substantial
amount of work, and the paper archives all of them, which is the right
call, and a reader who wants to assess the human effort should read the
prompts, because the prompts are where the mathematical direction lives,
and the zero-code claim is accurate but it is not a zero-effort claim, the
effort moved from writing Lean to writing and reviewing prompts and
catching the failure modes. The third question is the cost figure, two
hundred dollars over ten days, which is a striking number and the honest
read is that it is the cost of the compute for this particular run, and it
does not include the mathematician's own time, which is the real cost, and
a reader who wants to reproduce this should separate the compute cost from
the expert-supervision cost, because the two hundred dollars is the part
that scales with the model and the ten days of expert attention is the
part that does not, and the method's practicality depends on the second
number, not the first. And the practical question is the reproducibility,
the paper says the whole process is public, all the prompts and all the
commits are archived, and that is the strongest part of the contribution,
because it means a reader can actually inspect the failure modes and the
fixes rather than taking the paper's word for them, and a reader should
check that the repository is complete, that the Lean four development
checks out from the archived commits, because the value of the paper is in
the inspectability of the loop, and that only holds if the archive is
faithful.

Bottom line: this is a well-executed demonstration of the full AI-assisted
formal-mathematics loop, a reasoning model generating the proof, a coding
agent translating it to Lean four, a specialized prover closing the lemmas,
and the kernel verifying the result, with a single mathematician
supervising over ten days, and its real value is the honest account of the
failure modes, hypothesis creep, definition-alignment bugs, agent
avoidance, and the design choices that mitigated them, the abstract-concrete
split and the adversarial self-review, all archived and inspectable. The
open question is whether the method generalizes beyond this one theorem,
what the real cost is once the expert supervision is counted, and whether
the archived repository reproduces the result.
