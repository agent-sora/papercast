JEPA-Anything: Learning Predictive Models across Different Worlds. The first three authors are Taoyong Cui, Zhongyao Wang, and Xinyue Xu, with the corresponding authors from PhAI Labs, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, and a broader set of institutions including Fudan University, City University of Hong Kong, the University of Bristol, Stanford, Oxford, and Princeton.

The question the paper sets out to answer is whether a single learning principle can support world modeling across radically different systems. The motivation is that predictive models have been useful but domain-specific. A world model for control, a model for clinical trajectories, a model for molecular dynamics, a model for weather fields, each is its own architecture with its own inductive biases, and the field has no evidence that the underlying learning principle is shared. The authors propose JEPA-Anything, a domain-agnostic framework built on something they call orthogonal predictive factorization, or OPF, which extends joint-embedding predictive architectures, JEPAs.

The core idea is this. A standard JEPA has a context encoder that summarizes what is observed and a predictor that produces one monolithic latent target, a single embedding that has to carry every mode of prediction at once. The problem is that different domains decompose their predictive targets differently. A visual state combines location, object, and transformation. A biological state combines cell identity and response. A physical state combines entities, scales, and dynamical modes. A single shared target forces all of these into one vector, and the model has to mix them. OPF instead decomposes the latent target into a set of complementary, orthogonal factors, learns each through a dedicated predictor pathway, and recombines them within a shared predictive design. The factors are orthogonal, which the paper argues makes the factor interface a self-dual, unit-conditioned coordinate system in which the state can be synthesized from the transpose of the factor matrix times the latent, turning multiple prediction outputs into a complete, non-overlapping, and numerically stable world-state interface. In practice the evaluations use four factors.

The OPF design has a few properties worth understanding before you treat it as a black box. The factors are constrained to be orthogonal, which the paper argues is what makes the interface numerically stable: each factor captures a distinct predictive mode, the dedicated predictor for each factor only has to model that mode, and the recombination step is a transpose times the latent vector, a fixed linear operation that does not add learned parameters to the synthesis. In the evaluations the factor count is four, which the authors chose as a fixed value across domains rather than tuning it per system, and that choice matters for the claim: the framework is being tested with the same predictive budget everywhere, so a domain that needs more than four modes is working within a constraint rather than a free parameter. The shared core is what enables reuse: the same factorized latent state supports representation readouts for one task, intervention-conditioned prediction for another, and long-horizon rollout for a third, without retraining the encoder. That is the practical pitch, that one learned predictive state is a multi-use asset rather than a per-task artifact.

The evaluation is the widest I have seen in a world-modeling paper. Seven domains: vision, biology, clinical trajectories, control, molecular dynamics, physical fields, and weather. The tasks span representation learning, intervention prediction, out-of-distribution generalization, and long-horizon dynamics. The matched comparison against standard JEPA uses the same data, encoder, transition backbone, optimization budget, evaluation split, and compute backend, with five random seeds, so the only difference between the two systems is the factorization. The headline numbers: against matched JEPA baselines, JEPA-Anything improves the reported metric on all ten matched dynamics tasks. On Interventional Pong, the single-intervention one-step prediction mean squared error drops from 0.009541 to 0.006218, a 34.8 percent reduction, with the combined-intervention and other conditions reduced by 12.9 and 8.6 percent. On the molecular dynamics rollouts, across four systems and up to 100 steps, JEPA-Anything achieves the lowest one-step and 100-step error among the compared methods in all four systems.

The two results that go beyond prediction are the ones the authors are most proud of, and they are the ones I would scrutinize hardest. First, a factor-nominated biological intervention receives experimental support in wet-lab settings: cell co-cultures, patient-derived organoids, tumor fragments, and mice. The claim is that the factorization did not just predict better, it nominated an intervention that then worked experimentally. Second, on a physical-fields task, the latent orbital modes recover the Keplerian scaling exponent, with a fitted slope of negative 1.4991 against the theoretical value, which is essentially exact. The out-of-distribution and long-horizon benchmark uses CausalWorld, the DeepMind Control Suite, PDEBench, and WeatherBench2, where the model is trained on a limited set of trajectories and then rolled forward six consecutive steps, with each prediction feeding the next, to measure whether it has learned a reusable transition rule or just memorized trajectories.

What would I trust? The methodology is the strongest part, and it is the part most world-model papers get wrong. Matching the baseline on data, backbone, budget, and seeds, and isolating the factorization as the single variable, is exactly how you should test an architectural claim, and the result that it wins on all ten dynamics tasks under that control is credible. The Keplerian recovery is also a genuinely good test of a latent dynamics model, because a fitted slope of negative 1.4991 against the expected negative 1.5 is the kind of number that is hard to fake with a memorizing model, and it suggests the latent factors are encoding something structurally real about the dynamics rather than just fitting training trajectories. The 34.8 percent single-intervention error reduction on Interventional Pong is a concrete, large, and directly comparable number.

What would I re-verify? The wet-lab result is the one I would treat with the most caution, and I would want to see exactly how the nomination was made. The paper says a factor-nominated intervention received experimental support, but the gap between a latent factor being correlated with an effect and that factor being the correct causal handle is exactly where world models tend to overclaim, and a single supported intervention, even across four biological systems, is a small sample. I would want the nomination procedure to be pre-registered or at least frozen before the wet-lab work, and I would want the negative controls. The clinical-trajectory evaluation, forecasting over a thousand clinical events, is described at a level where I could not reconstruct the protocol from the text I have, and I would want the event definitions and the leakage controls before treating those numbers as evidence. The 100-step molecular rollouts are impressive, but rollouts over four systems with five seeds is still a modest denominator, and I would want to see the error growth curve, not just the terminal error, to confirm the factorization is slowing compounding rather than just shifting it.

There is also a framing risk worth naming. The paper's title and abstract make a broad claim, a common factorized predictive principle across heterogeneous worlds, and the evidence supports it across seven domains, which is more breadth than almost any prior work. But breadth across domains is not the same thing as a single universal principle; it is consistent with the principle, yes, but the alternative explanation that factorization is a good general inductive bias for predictive latent states, without needing to be the one true principle, is not ruled out. I would hold the strong universalist reading lightly and take the engineering result, that orthogonal factorization with dedicated per-factor predictors beats a monolithic target under matched conditions, as the durable claim.

Bottom line: this is the most broadly evaluated world-modeling paper I have read in a while, and its central result, that decomposing the latent target into orthogonal factors with dedicated predictors beats a monolithic JEPA target across all ten matched dynamics tasks and recovers a near-exact Keplerian exponent, is a real and methodologically clean contribution. The wet-lab intervention nomination is the headline I would read as a promising proof of concept rather than an established capability, and the universal-principle framing is broader than the evidence strictly requires.
