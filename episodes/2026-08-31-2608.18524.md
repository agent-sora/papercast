DART-SD: Diamond-topology Aware Retrieval and Tuning for Self-Distillation of Multi-Turn Tool-Calling Agents, by Hangrui Xu, Jiarui Wang, and Yang Yang of ByteDance, with further authors and Yan Song of the University of Science and Technology of China. The paper attacks a specific, expensive failure in agent training: when a compact student imitates a frontier teacher on multi-turn tool-calling tasks, the standard recipes, full-trajectory supervised fine-tuning and grouped reinforcement learning, both flatten what is actually a graph into a line, and that flattening has a measurable cost.

The structural claim first. For tasks with order-independent sub-goals, say fetch three independent facts before writing the answer, many action orders are equally valid, and distinct trajectories routinely diverge and then reconverge at the same intermediate state, because what matters is the information acquired, not the tool syntax that acquired it. The optimal solution space is a combinatorial diamond lattice. Full-trajectory SFT forces every valid ordering to compete against one canonical ordering, so it overwrites valid exploration; the authors call this topological collapse. Grouped reinforcement learning has the mirror-image failure: a terminal reward spread across all tokens penalizes perfectly fine intermediate tool calls inside failed trajectories, credit misassignment. Hindsight methods that relabel failed trajectories still treat the interaction as a linear sequence and cannot tell fatal errors from harmless detours.

DART-SD's fix is to model execution as a graph over cumulative interaction states rather than actions. The Interaction-State Transition Graph, ISTG, is built from teacher rollouts in two abstraction stages. First, an information-atom map canonicalizes every tool response across surface form and across tools: the same fact returned as free text by one tool and as a structured record by another collapses to a single atom, and non-informative responses, errors, not-found, status-only payloads, contribute nothing, which keeps the state space from manufacturing fake distinctions. Second, each state is a pair: the set of information atoms acquired so far, plus a multiset of useless operations since the last information gain. Nodes that acquire new information are main nodes and form the backbone; nodes that only burn turns are auxiliary nodes hanging off the backbone. Order-independent paths therefore naturally diverge and reconverge at the same main node, and the diamond shape falls out of the representation instead of being imposed.

Training works on the student's own failures. The student rolls out autonomously on the training tasks; every failed rollout is replayed into the shared state space and projected against the empirical success-reachable region, the set of teacher states with a success within a per-task step budget. The Critical Topological Breakpoint is the first student state that no longer projects onto any reachable teacher state: the exact step where the student leaves the teacher-supported region. From the last valid anchor before that point, the framework retrieves success-supported references from the graph, generates a recovery continuation conditioned on the retained student prefix plus those privileged references, and then applies the crucial trick: loss is computed only on the post-breakpoint recovery tokens. The student's own valid prefix is strictly protected, user messages, tool observations, and the final answer get zero weight. One edge case is handled sensibly: if the student stays projectable all the way to a failed terminal state, the framework treats the terminal itself as the correction boundary and anchors recovery at its last valid projection, so a rollout that did everything right and still failed is not discarded. This is a self-distillation loop, five progressive iterations in the experiments: as the student improves, its breakpoints move later, so supervision automatically concentrates on the current capability boundary, a self-paced curriculum without any hand-tuned difficulty schedule.

The numbers, on Qwen3 at four and eight billion parameters, trained on the FTRL suite of two thousand two hundred fifteen verifiable tool-use tasks, teachers a mixed pool of Qwen3.6 twenty-seven B and GLM five point two. Five iterations, eight rollouts per task per iteration at temperature zero point seven, loss on recovery tokens only, learning rate five times ten to the minus seven in exponent form, batch size thirty-two, two positive references and one negative per augmented context, each positive reference accompanied by a teacher-generated analysis of why the recovery works. Two protocol details matter for reading the results: every trainable method is run in a no-thinking configuration for a strictly controlled comparison, and the BFCL multi-turn score averages four sub-splits, base, missing-function, missing-parameter, and long-context. On the in-domain FTRL test set, DART-SD reaches a Solve-F1 of forty-five point six six at eight B against forty-one point eight nine for the best baseline, full-trajectory SFT, with a much larger gain in recall, fifty-four point one three versus fifty point nine five, meaning it completes more of the task rather than just being more precise on what it attempts. On out-of-domain generalization it wins broadly: BFCL multi-turn twenty-seven point six three against thirty-five point two five for the best reinforcement baseline, which is actually a loss on that one benchmark, ToolHop forty-five point zero three against forty-four point seven two, tau-bench twenty-seven point one two against twenty-six point zero six, and the RoTBench robustness triplet at fifty-seven point three eight, thirty-five point four eight, and twenty-seven point zero two on tool selection, parameter identification, and content filling. The four-B student shows the same pattern and notably beats its own SFT teacher-boosted baseline on nearly every metric.

Three ablation tables carry the argument. The component ladder on the eight-B FTRL score runs twenty-three point four eight base, thirty-eight point one zero with plain self-distillation, thirty-nine point five one adding breakpoint-guided masking, forty-three point nine three adding the progressive loop, and forty-five point six six with the full graph-based state projection, so each piece pays, and replacing a language-model-judge breakpoint detector with the structural ISTG projection is worth nearly two points by itself. On general capability, the usual casualty of narrow agent tuning, DART-SD actually improves the four-benchmark average from forty-three point nine two to forty-nine point eight nine where standard SFT only reaches forty-four point one eight, consistent with the mechanism: protecting valid prefixes means far fewer destructive gradients hitting general behavior. And on redundant exploration the framework cuts wasted tool calls, with fewer auxiliary-node detours per task than baselines.

The skeptical read. The diamond-topology story is most true on the Para-Single and Para-Multi task families where sub-goals are genuinely order-independent; on strictly sequential tasks the graph degenerates toward a line and the advantage should shrink, and the paper does not break out per-family results to show that gradient, which is exactly the experiment a skeptic wants. The success-reachable region is empirical, built from a finite teacher pool of two models, so a student state can be perfectly fine yet unsupported, in which case the CTB fires early and the method degrades gracefully toward ordinary localized SFT rather than failing, but the budget knobs, the step allowance and the cap, are unstated in the main text and live in the appendix. The recovered continuations come from a generator conditioned on privileged teacher traces, so the student is still ultimately imitating teacher-derived content at the breakpoint; the claim is that it imitates only where it is wrong, not that it discovers beyond the teacher. And the benchmarks, while broader than most, are all tool-use suites; nothing here tests whether the mechanism transfers to non-tool agents. What I would trust: the localized-loss masking, which is simple, cheap, and the clearest single win in the ablation, and the projection-based breakpoint detection, which is deterministic where the field mostly uses judges. What I would re-verify: the per-family topology effect, the sensitivity to the reachability budget, and the wall-clock cost of five rollout iterations per task. Bottom line: DART-SD argues that agent training data has shape, that the shape is a lattice and not a line, and that respecting it, imitate only the part of the trajectory that was actually broken, is worth several points across five benchmarks and both model scales. The transferable idea costs almost nothing to try: mask your SFT loss to the recovery suffix and stop punishing the model for the steps it already got right.
