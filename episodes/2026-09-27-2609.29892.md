Qwen-Planner-Agent, a Closed-Loop AI-for-AI Framework for Real-World Mobile Planner Agents, by Tingyu Qu, Weigao Sun, and Yuecheng Liu. The byline is the MAI Team at Alibaba's Token Hub, which is the group behind the Qwen agent work.

The framing question is whether AI can be both the object of development and an active participant in building the next generation of AI systems. The paper's answer is a closed loop, and mobile device control is the test case, because mobile planning is demanding in two specific ways. Tasks are long-horizon and multi-step, which stresses agent reliability, and real device interaction is costly, which caps how much development data you can buy. The framework connects data production, model training, and deployment through a shared action-feedback-verification contract, and it has three components, each aimed at a different link in that loop.

The first is AI for Data, a human-gated agentic data flywheel. Specialized agents construct tasks, collect interaction trajectories, curate and balance the training data, and then use training feedback to guide what data to generate next. Human gating is the deliberate safety valve: humans review at the seams, but the volume is produced by agents. The second is AI for Training. It combines a supervised planning cold start with hybrid-environment online agentic reinforcement learning, where the hybrid part means some rollouts run in a cheaper simulated environment and the high-value ones run on real devices. The named technique here is CARE, Competence-Aware Reward-and-Advantage Engineering: a bounded LLM-based controller configures predefined reward schedules from training and validation feedback, adapting rewards and calibrating advantages so that the model spends its reasoning and tool-use budget where it is actually learning, which directly reduces per-task cost. The third is model-and-harness co-evolution. The planner model is paired with a harness that orchestrates memory, skills, and tools at runtime, and an execution-evidence-driven loop feeds structured action feedback and preserved failure traces back into coordinated adaptation of both the model and the harness. In other words, when the system fails, the failure is analyzed and used to improve the surrounding scaffolding, not just the weights.

The concrete system couples a trained planner model, called Qwen-Planner-Model, with a unified harness that supplies tool-conditioned skills, persistent memory, and execution feedback. The planner model comes in two sizes in the paper's evaluations: a 35B parameter, 3B-active mixture-of-experts variant and a 27B dense variant. The harness is the part that does a lot of the heavy lifting at inference, and the ablations are designed to measure exactly how much: the paper compares the model with and without the harness, with and without the memory component, and against a commercial memory system, Mem0, as a reference.

The benchmark is MobilePA-Bench, an evaluation of real-world mobile planning across four capabilities: tool use, memory, skills, and sub-agent coordination. On the overall score, Qwen-Planner-Agent reports 77.1, which the paper presents as the best among all the models and systems evaluated, and it improves over its own base model across all four capability dimensions. In the capability breakdown, the four sub-scores run 77.8 for tool use, 74.8 for memory, 86.3 for skills, and 59.6 for sub-agent coordination, with sub-agent coordination being the clearly weakest axis and the one that separates frontier systems from the rest. The comparison field is a serious one: the paper plots Qwen-Planner-Agent against Claude Opus 5, GLM 5.3, Qwen 3.8 Max, Kimi K3, Gemini 3.6 Flash, Seed 2.1 Pro, Claude Fable 5, GPT 6, and Astra, and it also reports a performance-versus-cost curve in US dollars per thousand tasks, which is an unusually useful framing for a system that runs real device loops.

The memory results are the part that most directly isolates the harness. The paper evaluates long-context memory across history lengths, from BEAM-100K up to BEAM-10M, and the finding is that explicit memory only starts to matter once the history is long enough to overflow direct context. On the direct-context side, even the strongest model degrades as history grows: Claude Opus 5 reaches 73.63 on BEAM-500K but drops to 54.84 on BEAM-1M, and under the truncation protocol no direct-context model exceeds 25.64 on BEAM-10M. Adding the harness flips that curve. Across the matched model pairs, the harness raises the mean BEAM score from 42.18 to 68.29 at 500K, from 40.41 to 70.27 at 1M, and from 23.42 to 61.66 at 10M, while on the shorter benchmarks the change is negligible, a few tenths of a point. For the 27B planner model specifically, the harness lifts BEAM-1M from 40.12 to 73.71 and BEAM-10M from 21.99 to 67.24. That is the cleanest evidence in the paper that the runtime scaffolding, not the weights, is carrying the long-history capability, and it is the result that justifies the model-and-harness co-evolution framing.

Beyond mobile, the paper reports that the planner model shows broad planning and tool-use capability on general agentic benchmarks, and that general capability is largely preserved, which is the standard catastrophic-forgetting check. The ablations are attributed specifically to model-and-harness co-evolution, meaning the gains are not all in the weights; a meaningful share comes from the runtime scaffolding adapting to the failures it observes.

Skeptical assessment. What I would trust: the cost curve. Reporting output cost in dollars per thousand tasks alongside performance is the right metric for a real-device agent, because the whole reason this is a research problem is that real device interaction is expensive, and a system that scores higher but costs five times as much per task may be the wrong trade. The four-capability decomposition on MobilePA-Bench is also a better diagnostic than a single aggregate, and the honest display that sub-agent coordination is the weak spot at 59.6 is a signal that the evaluation is not being massaged. The harness ablations, including the comparison against a commercial memory system as a reference point, are the kind of controlled comparison that lets you attribute gains to a specific component rather than to the model getting bigger.

What I would re-verify: the closed loop's actual contribution to the final number. The framework is described at the process level, data flywheel, CARE reward engineering, co-evolution, but the headline benchmark score is for the finished system, and the paper does not fully isolate how much of the 77.1 comes from each of the three framework components versus from the base planner model's raw capability. A reader who wants to reproduce the result needs to know whether the data flywheel or the CARE reward schedule is the load-bearing piece, and that attribution is thinner than the rest of the writeup. The human-gated data flywheel is also a real cost and throughput consideration: if human review at the seams is required, the "closed loop" is only as fast as its human bottleneck, and the paper should say how much of the data volume actually requires a human in the loop. The sub-agent coordination gap at 59.6 is also worth a closer look, because that is exactly the capability a multi-agent planner is supposed to be best at, and a number that low suggests the current architecture is not yet reliably orchestrating sub-agents.

Bottom line: Qwen-Planner-Agent is a strong demonstration that a mobile device planning agent can be developed inside a genuine closed loop where AI produces data, trains, and improves its own runtime scaffolding, and it lands at the top of a strong comparison field on MobilePA-Bench while reporting cost, not just accuracy. The framework's three components are the durable contribution, but the single number that matters most for adoption, how much each component contributes and where the human bottleneck sits, is the part I would want before trusting the closed loop as a scalable development method rather than a one-team achievement.
