What Makes Good Agentic Data? An ACE Lens on Data Generation for LLM Agents, by Xingshan Zeng of Huawei Technologies, Zishan Xu and Boju Zhang of Shanghai Jiao Tong University, with further authors from Northwestern University, Harbin Institute of Technology in Shenzhen, and the Shenzhen Loop Area Institute. This is a survey, not a system paper, but it earns its forty minutes of your attention by doing the two things a good survey should: it replaces a pile of domain-specific vocabulary with one abstraction that actually holds across domains, and it states the field's implicit objective function out loud. If you are building an agent data pipeline this quarter, this is the map.

The core object. Agentic data is not instruction-response pairs. The paper factorizes every agentic training example as four components: E, the environment specification, from bare tool schemas up to fully executable simulators with state, dynamics, policies, and observation interfaces; q, the task signal, which may be an explicit instruction, a hidden user intent, or something progressively revealed across turns; tau, the interaction realization, the actual trajectory of actions and observations; and v, an optional verifier, which can be a schema checker, executable test, state predicate, proof assistant, or model judge. The decomposition is conceptual rather than a serialization requirement, but it makes the real content of the field visible: pipelines generate not just conversations but tool ecosystems, executable environments, stateful tasks, and verification procedures, and the hard engineering is keeping those four factors consistent with each other. A sample can be fluent and still be garbage because its task is infeasible, a tool is implemented inconsistently, an observation does not follow from the preceding action, or the verifier rewards the wrong outcome.

On top of that factorization sits the A-C-E objective: Accuracy, Complexity, divErsity, treated as constrained distribution design with a deliberate asymmetry. Accuracy is an admission condition, a conjunction over all four factors: the environment must be internally consistent, the task feasible and grounded, the interaction causally valid, the verifier aligned with the intended outcome. Invalid data cannot be rescued by being difficult or different, so accuracy gates first. Within the valid set, Complexity is calibrated, not maximized: the useful difficulty region is relative to a declared learner and its execution configuration, its scaffolding, tools, and inference budget, and the goal is a learnable band near the model's current frontier, not maximum length. Diversity is a batch-level property rewarding coverage and non-redundancy across environments, tasks, and behaviors. The formal version is an optimization with an acceptance-rate constraint, and the paper is explicit that this is an analytical lens, not a governance checklist, since cost, safety, and efficiency remain separate constraints.

The taxonomy of generation paradigms is organized by which factor anchors the pipeline. Forward generation grounds everything in an existing environment, then synthesizes tasks and trajectories; the survey splits it by environment source, real or curated ones like ToolLLM and APIGen, model-synthesized ones, and programmatic or executable environments like EnvScaler and EnvFactory, noting the main risk is cascading dependence, where a narrow or unreliable environment constrains everything built on it. Reverse generation flips the anchor. Task-first pipelines specify a capability and construct the world around it, which gives direct difficulty control but needs strong feasibility checks. Trajectory-first pipelines explore or mine first and write the task afterward, as OS-Genesis does for graphical interfaces, improving executability but bounding the data by what the exploration policy happened to discover. Structure-first pipelines generate an intermediate scaffold, a tool graph or verified blueprint, before realization. And a growing cross-cutting family is adaptive and self-evolving generation, where accumulated experience, verification outcomes, and coverage gaps feed back into what gets generated next.

Three literature findings emerge from reading everything through this lens, and each is a trend worth internalizing. First, accuracy assurance is moving from plausibility judgments toward execution-grounded verification: layered rules, model checks, and human review; constraints built in before generation rather than filtered after; execution and state-based verification; and feedback-driven repair with selective admission. Where no complete oracle exists, pipelines combine partial rules with semantic review, and the survey's diagnostic question for any paper is simply: what does it verify, when, and which failures remain outside its verifier. Second, difficulty is shifting from static heuristics like input length toward model-relative calibration, including failure-driven generation that targets what the current learner gets wrong, and bidirectional schemes where the curriculum moves as the model moves. Third, diversity is shifting from surface variation, more paraphrases, bigger counts, toward behavioral coverage, with mechanisms including source expansion, compositional recombination, exploration-first discovery, perturbation and counterfactual variants, and coverage-guided balancing, and with measurement that separates genuine support expansion from denser repetition of saturated modes. One subtlety the accuracy-diversity tension exposes: verifier support can be narrower than the valid solution space, so optimizing admission against one fixed checker can silently discard legitimate strategies, which means verifier coverage and independent audits should expand alongside the diversity they are meant to police.

The discussion section is where the survey earns a second listen. On scaling: raw quantity is a weak proxy because samples contribute unevenly, accuracy determines how much of the pool is admissible at all, and diversity-oriented scaling has already beaten quantity scaling under comparable budgets, so the meaningful scaling variable is closer to effective support than to dataset size, and the strongest strategy maintains a growing region that is simultaneously valid, learnable, and behaviorally non-redundant. On real versus synthetic data: neither wins uniformly. Real data give authentic interfaces and long-tail behavior but are expensive, skewed toward frequent workflows, hard to reset, and not automatically correct; synthetic data give controllable difficulty and explicit verifiers but inherit their generator's fidelity limits, and shared teachers and templates can narrow behavioral support behind a large sample count. The recommendation is hybrid allocation, real data anchoring semantics and held-out audits, synthetic expanding and calibrating around those anchors, chosen by which deficiency is actually limiting. Two more directions round it out: moving agentic experience earlier into pre-training and mid-training, where the training unit need not be a full trajectory anymore but can be local state transitions, inverse-dynamics examples, or reachability objectives; and self-evolving agents, where data generation becomes part of the continual learning loop itself. The self-evolution section carries the sharpest warning in the paper: a generator, learner, and verifier that adapt together can confirm the same mistaken assumptions, so stable self-improvement requires fixed or independently updated anchors, held-out tasks, external execution, or periodic real-environment evaluation.

The skeptical assessment. Surveys get graded on selection and framing, not experiments, so the risks are different here: the A-C-E lens is persuasive partly because it is elastic, and elastic frameworks can rationalize almost any pipeline after the fact; the taxonomy's boundaries, especially between task-first and structure-first, are judgment calls; and the cited evidence for several trend claims is a handful of recent papers rather than a meta-analysis, so treat sentences like diversity scaling beats quantity scaling as directional, not established. There is also no quantitative benchmark of the lens itself, no demonstration that classifying pipelines by A-C-E predicts anything measurable. What I would trust is the factorized data object, which is immediately useful as a design checklist and a documentation standard for your own pipeline, and the accuracy-as-admission-gate asymmetry, which encodes a lesson most teams learn the expensive way. What I would act on directly: when reviewing or building an agentic data pipeline, ask what is verified, when in the pipeline, and which failures fall outside the verifier, and require complexity and diversity claims to be stated relative to a declared learner and configuration. Bottom line: the paper's central claim is that the field's bottleneck is not generating more experience but allocating valid, informative, non-redundant experience as the agent evolves, and the A-C-E lens is a serviceable instrument for that allocation. As a map of the agentic data landscape circa mid-2026 it is the best available, and its own closing warning, that generation and verification must not confirm each other's mistakes, applies to every self-improving system this podcast has covered this month.
