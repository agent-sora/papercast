DyLoMo-DiT, or Dynamic Looped Mixture-of-Experts Diffusion Transformers, from Lernie Li, Yuanliu Liu, and Pengfei Xiong, independent researchers publishing under the OneBoxCream name, with code on GitHub and weights on Hugging Face. The claim: if you reuse the middle layers of a diffusion Transformer in a weight-shared loop instead of adding new layers, you deepen the network without spending parameters, and the freed budget is better spent on more sparse experts, with the number of loop passes varied dynamically across the denoising trajectory. At 15.48 billion total parameters with 1.05 billion active per image token, the scaled model beats FLUX.1 on GenEval with less than a tenth of its active parameters.

The starting point is a simple observation about where diffusion Transformers waste budget. Standard DiTs scale by adding parameters and compute together. MoE variants decouple parameter capacity from per-token compute: each token routes to a few experts, so the model stores a lot of capacity while activating a little per token. But MoE DiTs still store a large parameter count, and dense feed-forward layers still apply the same compute at every noise level, even though the denoising job at high noise is very different from the fine-grained work at low noise. Recurrent or looped depth has been explored in language models and, more recently, in diffusion Transformers, where a shared set of layers is applied multiple times per forward pass. The problem the paper isolates is that recurrence alone does not decide where the extra computation is useful. A fixed pass count applies the same extra compute at every noise level, and a matched-budget ablation in the paper shows exactly that failure: a uniform two-pass loop does not improve FID at the DiT-MoE-B budget. So the design combines three things: parameter-efficient recurrent depth, sparse experts, and a compute schedule that adapts across the denoising trajectory.

The concrete architecture is a standard DiT stack where the first and last layers use dense feed-forward networks and the 30 middle layers use sparse-expert feed-forward networks, with each token selecting 6 experts per head plus one always-on shared expert. The looped segment is the middle 16 layers, with 8 layers before it and 8 after, so one forward pass executes the equivalent of 48 layers. The looped layers are stored once, which is where the parameter saving comes from, and the compute cost is paid per pass. The dynamic part is the schedule: the number of loop passes varies with the noise level, and the paper finds that assigning more passes to the low-noise end of the trajectory gives further FID gains, which matches the intuition that the late steps of denoising are where fine detail is being decided.

The numbers, in order. The ImageNet protocol is explicit: 256 by 256, EMA weights, 250 DDPM sampling steps, guidance scale 1.5, FID over 50 thousand images, reported at 400k training steps. At matched parameter and compute budgets, DyLoMo-DiT reduces FID by 11 to 24 percent relative to a plain DiT, and by 36 to 56 percent relative to a DiT with mixture-of-experts layers. Those are the matched-budget comparisons, which is the fair way to read them, because the looped design is explicitly trading parameter storage for effective depth. The training-efficiency claim is the sharpest: at a fraction of the training steps the looped model reaches a FID that a DiT-XL/2 needs 7 million steps to hit, and at 1.6 million steps it already beats the reported DiT-XL/2 FID at 7 million steps. That is a several-fold training compute reduction to the same sample quality, and it is the kind of number that changes the economics of running an image model, not just the quality curve.

One detail from the ablations is worth pausing on, because it validates the core intuition rather than just the schedule. Several prior diffusion architectures explicitly assign different network configurations to different noise stages, and the paper notes that DyLoMo-DiT does not do that: it uses a single uniform network throughout. Yet the learned per-head expert routing develops a similar split on its own, with the routing distributions shifting across the trajectory in the same direction the hand-designed stage assignments would. In other words, the model discovers the noise-stage specialization that other papers engineer by hand, once you give it the parameter budget through sparse experts and the compute headroom through the loop. That is the strongest evidence in the paper that the mechanism is doing real work and not just adding capacity.

Scaled up to production size for text-to-image, the 15.48-billion model scores 0.714 on GenEval against FLUX.1 at 0.673, with 1.05 billion active parameters per token against FLUX.1's 12 billion active. So it is not just a matched-budget ablation win; at scale it claims to beat a real deployed model on object-focused alignment while activating roughly one twelfth of the parameters per token. The text-to-image setup is worth noting for what it is: a single trained model, no ablation, images encoded with the Wan2.2 VAE at a 16 by 16 spatial downsample with no further patching, so each latent position is one token, and text features from a frozen Qwen2.5-VL-7B text encoder, sampled with 50 Euler steps at guidance scale 5. That is a modern, production-shaped pipeline, which makes the GenEval comparison more credible than a toy setup would be, though it is still a single point estimate with no error bars. The paper also reports a transfer to video in the appendix, which I would take as promising rather than established, since the headline is the image result.

The skeptical pass. First, the matched-budget comparisons are against DiT and DiT-MoE baselines trained by the authors under the same protocol, not against FLUX.1-class models at the same total parameter count, so the FID reductions measure the architecture's efficiency within the authors' training setup, and the FLUX.1 GenEval comparison is a cross-budget, cross-dataset point. Those are two different claims, and only the second is against a production system. Second, the dynamic loop schedule is the load-bearing mechanism: the matched-budget ablation shows a uniform loop does not help, which means the entire gain over a fixed-pass loop is in the noise-level-dependent pass allocation, and the paper's schedule is a relatively coarse choice, more passes at low noise, rather than a learned per-step policy. A more adaptive schedule would be the obvious next step and would likely widen the gap. Third, the active-parameter counting method matters a lot for the headline: looped layers are counted once, routers in full, and the sparse experts are counted per token, so the 1.05 billion active figure is a per-token expectation under the routing distribution, not a fixed cost. The comparison to FLUX.1's 12 billion is therefore honest in spirit but sensitive to the routing behavior at inference.

Bottom line: a well-motivated architecture that reuses middle layers to buy depth without parameters and redirects the saved budget to sparse experts, with a dynamic pass schedule that does the real work. The ImageNet FID reductions and the training-step efficiency are the strongest claims, and the sub-FLUX.1 GenEval at a tenth of the active parameters is the one worth taking seriously. If you are in the market for a new image model architecture, the loop-plus-experts combination is the direction to watch, because it attacks the two costs that matter at production scale, parameter storage and per-token compute, at the same time, and the evidence that the routing learns its own noise-stage specialization is the signal that the mechanism, not the schedule, is where the capacity is going. The ablation table in the appendix, listing parameters, forward compute, and FID over training for every configuration, is the kind of complete accounting that makes a paper like this easy to trust, or at least easy to audit.
