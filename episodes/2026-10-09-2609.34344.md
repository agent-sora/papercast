"Learning to steer, steering to see: unveiling the geometry of reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards in large language models via trainable vectors," by Yuchen Cai, Ding Cao, and Qixiang Yin from the University of Science and Technology of China, Tencent Hunyuan, and the Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications. This is squarely in the interpretability and mathematical-analysis space, so it is in scope on the standing rule. The question is a genuinely good one and it has been open for a while: we know reinforcement learning, specifically RLVR, the variant that uses verifiable rewards, gives large language models big reasoning gains, but we do not understand what those gains actually look like inside the model. The parameter updates are high-dimensional and noisy, so it is hard to separate the behaviorally meaningful changes from incidental variation. Their approach is to treat the whole thing as an intervention problem in activation space, and use a tool from the interpretability world, vector steering, as a probe.

The experimental setup is elegant. They freeze the base model and, instead of fine-tuning, they learn a small number of input-invariant vectors, one per selected layer, and add them to the layer outputs. Then they use an RL-trained model as a teacher and distill into the base model using only these vectors. The reason to do this is not efficiency, it is diagnosis. If a handful of fixed vectors can recover most of the gain that full fine-tuning produces, then the RL gain must admit a low-dimensional description, and the directions those vectors learn are a window into what the gain actually is. They run this across five models, DeepSeek R1 distilled Qwen at 1.5 and 7 billion and Qwen3 at 4, 8, and 14 billion, and six tasks with verifiable rewards, covering math, science, code, and instruction following.

The first finding is about compressibility, which they call the effective manifold capacity. A single steering vector per controlled layer recovers over 85 percent of the gain that full fine-tuning achieves, on most tasks, and this holds under both on-policy and off-policy distillation. That is a strikingly low-dimensional result, most of the RL gain is carried by a fixed additive offset per layer. But it is not infinitely compressible, and they pin down the two limits. The first is the teacher-student distribution gap. When the teacher and the student are close, a fixed offset is enough, but as the gap widens the required correction becomes more input-dependent, and a fixed offset can no longer express it. They show this with a clean ordering: under a large gap, full-parameter training does best, then LoRA, then vector steering worst, exactly matching their expressive capacity. The second limit is depth. Fixed-vector steering works well in the low and middle layers but degrades near the output, and they rule out the obvious explanation, that the gradient is weaker there, because the gradient norms are comparable across depths. The real cause is that near the output there are fewer downstream nonlinear transformations for a fixed offset to act through. They test the fix directly: replace the fixed offset with a low-rank input-dependent correction, and rank-1 gating recovers the high-layer performance, which confirms the diagnosis that the bottleneck is expressiveness, not optimization.

The second finding is the geometric one, the control manifold separation. They extend the probe to multiple mutually orthogonal vectors per layer, learned in sequence, each one constrained to be orthogonal to the ones before it. The leading direction is the strongest, but the later orthogonal directions still independently support the target behavior, so the leading direction is dominant but not unique. The more interesting result is where these directions live in activation space. They take the leading principal subspace of the base model's activations, the top ten percent of the variance, and measure how much of the steering directions sits in it. The effective directions put only about one to two percent of their energy in that principal subspace, well below the ten percent you would expect from an isotropic random direction. They then do the negative control: force the steering vectors into the principal subspace, and the gain does not come back. So the control lives in the low-variance complement, not in the high-variance space. And there is a striking consistency result: the same directions come back across different teacher parameterizations and different distillation regimes, and across different scientific topics, the alignment between the learned directions predicts the transfer gain, with a correlation of 0.91. So the geometry of the RL gain is reproducible and it tracks which capabilities transfer to which. There is a useful detail in the multi-vector result worth spelling out, because it is what turns the probe from a trick into a structural claim. They learn the vectors one at a time, each one constrained to be orthogonal to all the ones learned before it, and they freeze each vector once it is done. Even the eighth direction, which has been forced to avoid the first seven, still independently supports the target behavior, just a few points below the first. That means the gain is not carried by a single special direction, it is a low-dimensional subspace of control directions, and the first direction simply happens to be the most efficient one. Larger models also retain more of the higher-order directions than smaller models do, so the width of this effective subspace appears to grow with model scale, and that is a hint that the RL gain gets more structured as the model gets bigger.

They turn this into a practical tool, Alpha-Stabler, a plug-and-play framework for stabilizing RL training with no extra parameters and no changes to the optimizer or reward. The logic follows directly from the findings. During healthy training, the activation shifts stay in the low-variance complement, but when training is about to collapse, the shifts start intruding into the principal subspace, and they show that this intrusion, which they measure as a principal-subspace intrusion score, rises and becomes volatile before any visible score drop. So the Predictor watches that score and warns before collapse, and the Controller removes the principal-subspace component of the activation gradients during backprop, keeping only the orthogonal complement. They report that it stabilizes training over 2,000 steps and improves on gradient clipping and other projection methods, all from a probe that was built to measure the geometry rather than to control training, which is a nice demonstration that a descriptive analysis can double as a practical intervention.

The honest caveats. The analysis is on a fixed set of small models, up to 14 billion, and a handful of tasks, so the universality of the low-variance-complement claim is established within that scope rather than at frontier scale. The 85 percent recovery and the 0.91 transfer correlation are strong but they are on specific tasks and a specific principal-subspace definition, the top ten percent. And the Alpha-Stabler result is a stability win on a 2,000 step budget, which is a reasonable scale for the claim they are making but not a demonstration of frontier-length training. That said, the probe is a genuinely clean methodological contribution, and the negative controls, forcing vectors into the principal subspace, the parameter-matched static control for the gating, the gradient-norm check, are the kind of careful falsification that makes me trust the mechanism.

Here is the bottom line. This paper uses vector steering as a low-dimensional probe to show that RLVR-induced gains admit a structured description, a small number of reproducible control directions that live in the low-variance complement of the activation principal subspace, and that principal-subspace intrusion is a leading indicator of training collapse. If you are doing RLVR post-training, the activation-manifold lens and the Alpha-Stabler stabilization are both concrete and low-overhead, but treat the specific geometry as established within the tested model and task scope until it is confirmed at frontier scale.
