Nemotron 3.5 Super and Lightning: Open, Efficient Mixture-of-Experts Hybrid Mamba-Transformer Models for Agentic AI, from an NVIDIA team led on the byline by Aastha Jhunjhunwala, Aayushi Ashokkumar Srivastava, and Abhibha Gupta, with the full author list running to roughly five hundred and sixty people, is the complete training and deployment report for two open-weight models: Super, a 120 billion parameter, 12 billion active multimodal mixture of experts, and Lightning, a 30 billion parameter, 3 billion active text-only model. Both are hybrid Mamba-Transformer architectures, and the paper is organized around one theme, jointly optimizing capability and efficiency from continued pretraining through post-training to quantized serving.

The architecture and its initialization matter for anyone trying to reproduce this. Super is initialized from Nemotron 3 Super, the previous generation base, and Lightning from Nano 3 Base. The distinctive component is the multi-token prediction head used for native speculative decoding. In Super, two non-shared MTP heads are consolidated into a single recursively shared head, and the authors add hidden-state mixing to deal with a specific failure mode: the drafter is trained to condition on a particular input distribution but at inference time it sees different conditioning during recursive drafting, a train-inference mismatch that degrades acceptance. Lightning's MTP head is initialized from the Super 3 Base model through architecture-aware projection fusion and pruning, so the smaller model starts with a drafter that already knows the token distribution. Both models then undergo a dedicated 100 billion token MTP warm-up with the backbone frozen. After that, both are distilled for 1 trillion tokens using the next-larger Nemotron form factor as teacher: Ultra 3 Base distills into Super, and Super 3 Base distills into Lightning. That is the capability backbone, and it is followed by stages that diverge between the two models.

Super gets the longer treatment. First, 100 billion tokens of Indic continued pretraining, mixing 10 billion tokens of synthetic and natural Indian-language data with 90 billion from the preceding mixture. The synthetic side is produced with NeMo Data Designer, which generated approximately 2.83 trillion synthetic output tokens across 22 Indian languages and 34 language-script configurations. The reported effect is improvement on all five evaluated Indic benchmarks, with gains of up to 9.82 percentage points. Second, a 35 billion token stage extends the context to 1 million tokens. Third, 65.4 billion tokens of agentic software-engineering mid-training at 256 thousand context, with a mixture of whole repositories, issue and pull-request pairs, commit histories, build and continuous-integration logs, and agent trajectories. The mid-training ablations are the most informative numbers in the paper. With identical downstream supervised fine-tuning, mid-training raises DeepSWE pass@1 from 6.4 to 10.4, and SWE-bench Verified under the Codex harness from 48.4 to 61.8. But it also damages long context: RULER at 1 million tokens drops to 33.5, and a 9 billion token recovery stage is required to restore it to 81.0. If you are running agentic mid-training at long context, that recovery stage is the part to copy.

Post-training is the standard modern stack, executed comprehensively. A two-stage supervised fine-tuning pipeline converts the text-only base into a vision-language model while jointly developing reasoning, chat, safety, long-context, and agentic capabilities. The first stage establishes the vision-language interface on top of the text base, and the second layer on top of that with the full capability mix. Reinforcement learning spans diverse environments, including Olympiad-level competitive programming and a finance-agent task that requires SEC filing search, document parsing, evidence retrieval, and tool use. That finance environment is worth a sentence because it is the kind of agentic task that shows up in real deployments: multi-step, document-heavy, with a verifiable final answer, which is exactly the shape of task that RLVR handles. On top of the general RL, the authors train specialized teachers for video reasoning and tracking, vision tool use, and alignment, then consolidate those capabilities through multi-teacher on-policy distillation. The on-policy detail matters: the student generates its own trajectories and the teachers score them, so the distilled behavior stays in the student's own policy distribution rather than being imitated from off-distribution teacher rollouts, which is where multi-teacher distillation usually leaks quality. Lightning gets the lighter track: single-stage text-only supervised fine-tuning plus reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. The headline result of the whole post-training effort is 51.78 percent on SWE-bench Pro, which the paper highlights as exceeding Nemotron 3 Ultra despite using approximately 4.6 times fewer active parameters. The broader evaluation spans general knowledge suites like MMLU, MMLU-Pro, ARC-Challenge, and GSM8K, SuperGPQA and GPQA Diamond for graduate-level reasoning, LiveCodeBench for code generation, long-context reasoning, multimodal understanding, and a multilingual Global MMLU battery across German, Spanish, French, Italian, and other languages, so the comparison against the six leading open models in Figure 1 is not a single cherry-picked column. There is also a test-time scaling study: parallel agent execution plus self-verification lifts Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 62.7 to 71.9 and DeepSWE from 24.6 to 34.5. Those are large moves, but they are purchased with extra inference compute, not with model quality.

The efficiency half of the report is where the paper earns the word efficient in the title. Post-training MTP boosting improves the average speculative-decoding acceptance length from 4.287 to 4.718 under greedy decoding and from 3.787 to 4.612 under probabilistic draft sampling, measured on SPEED-Bench. On the quantization side, the team develops NVFP4 post-training quantization with mixed-precision recipes across weights, caches, and the MTP drafter, plus quantization-aware distillation, and reports 99.62 percent median accuracy recovery relative to BF16 across 36 benchmarks for the released quantized Super checkpoint. Serving optimizations include hybrid-state caching and GPU kernels for long-context and agentic workloads. On a single GB200 GPU, Super reaches up to 4,856 output tokens per second per GPU on a 20-turn agentic proxy workload while sustaining at least 50 tokens per second per user, and under the evaluated interactive serving conditions NVFP4 delivers 2.43 times the throughput of BF16. The throughput comparisons against Qwen3.5 at 122 billion with 10 billion active, and against Qwen3.8 Flash-Next, are 2.34 times and 1.90 times respectively. The release includes base, BF16 post-trained, and NVFP4 quantized checkpoints for both models, standalone parallel speculative drafters named LiLiCorr for Super and DFlash and DSpark for Lightning, model cards, the pretraining and post-training datasets, cookbooks, and training recipes.

So what should you trust, and what should you re-verify before adopting any of this? The mid-training ablations are the most honest content in the report, because they show both the gain and the damage, and the 256-thousand-token-context agentic mid-training degrading 1-million-token context performance to 33.5 before recovery is a real engineering hazard that most training reports never surface. The Indic result is strong but it is a 100 billion token stage, so the marginal cost per point is something you should compute against your own language requirements. The SWE-bench Pro headline, beating Ultra at 4.6 times fewer active parameters, is a comparison that favors the active-parameter framing. Ultra costs more to serve at every token, and the paper's own throughput section is where that trade becomes visible, so read the two sections together before drawing conclusions. The NVFP4 number is a median across 36 benchmarks, which means some benchmarks will sit below the recovery line; the per-benchmark distribution is in the tables and is worth checking for the capability you actually care about. And the test-time scaling gains, while large, are a compute-for-score exchange that your serving budget may not support. The drafters released separately are a genuinely useful artifact for low-concurrency, memory-bound deployments, since parallel speculative decoding helps most exactly when a single user stream is the bottleneck.

My verdict is that this is the kind of open report that functions less as a single result and more as a reference implementation for the whole pipeline, from MTP head design and distillation through agentic mid-training to NVFP4 serving, with ablations where it matters. If you are standing up an agentic serving stack on GB-class hardware and want an open model with a documented path from base checkpoint to quantized throughput, Nemotron 3.5 Super is the strongest current candidate, and the recipe around it is worth auditing stage by stage before you copy it.
