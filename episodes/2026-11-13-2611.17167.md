Actor, Judge, or Teacher? Understanding Decision Models in Long-Horizon Agents, by Haochen Liu, Qianhao Zhu, and Zixuan Wang, from Zhejiang University and Baidu, asks a specific question about decision models, the fast models that answer a question with a typed output, a choice among options with a probability for each, and the question is whether a decision model that is a poor actor can still be a good teacher, and the answer they find is yes, but only when you ask it the right question.

To set up the context, a large language model agent can query the same decision model in two ways, to choose its next action, or to evaluate an action that has already been taken. The evaluation use matters because of a well-known problem in reinforcement learning, which is that outcome rewards give every action in a trajectory the same credit, so you cannot tell the model which steps actually helped. Step-level judgments fix that, but they are costly, either because they need labels for each environment or because you have to query an expensive language-model judge at every single action. Decision models are cheap enough to score every action of every rollout, which makes them a natural teacher signal, but prior work has evaluated them mainly as actors, and in that role they are known to fail, getting drawn to commands that mention the task objects, skipping prerequisite subgoals, and completing subgoals in the wrong order. The paper's point is that those failures do not settle how useful the model is when it is evaluating rather than acting.

The setup is concrete. They use ALFWorld, the household-task environment where an agent has to complete a task like heating a mug and placing it somewhere, and WebShop, a web-shopping environment. They compare three decision models and three general language models, all of them in both the actor role and the judge role, and they use one of the decision models, which they call JEV, as a teacher on both environments. As a teacher, the decision model scores every action of a small policy, a Qwen 2.5 1.5B model, during training with group relative policy optimization, and those step-level scores become the training signal instead of the flat outcome reward.

The actor result is the expected failure, and it is a good controlled demonstration. All six models, the decision models and the general language models alike, get pulled away from a subgoal that must come first by an easier one. The canonical example is the heated-mug task, where the agent has to go to the microwave and heat the mug before it can place the heated mug somewhere, but the models tend to reach for the place action because it is the one that mentions the task object and the final state, and they skip the heating step or do it in the wrong order. That is the failure that has been documented before, and it establishes the baseline that the actor role is weak.

The judge result is where it gets interesting. When the same models are put in the judge role, and they are allowed to see the whole trajectory, they credit the steps that were actually working toward the required subgoal. And the crucial detail is which question you ask them. If you ask whether each action helped achieve the goal, the judgments separate useful from useless actions, and the policy trained on those judgments does well. But if you ask the different question, whether the task will succeed after each action, the judgments are worse, and the resulting policy is worse. So the same model, acting as a judge, can produce a good teaching signal or a bad one depending on the phrasing of the question, and that is a genuinely useful and specific result, because it tells you that the teacher role is not a free property of the model, it is something you have to elicit with the right query.

The headline number is that JEV, as a teacher, raises the small policy's success rate substantially. On ALFWorld it goes from a baseline of about seventy-three percent under outcome-reward training to about eighty-three percent when the step-level judgments from JEV are asked whether each action helped. On WebShop the improvement is even larger in relative terms, from about fifty-seven percent up to about seventy-one percent. The specific contrast they highlight is that when JEV acts itself it completes only about forty-three percent of the ALFWorld tasks, but when it scores the actions of the small policy it pushes that policy to about eighty-three percent. That gap, a model that fails as an actor but succeeds as a teacher, is the paper's central claim.

Now the skeptical part. The first thing to check is the mechanism, and the paper's own explanation is the important part to scrutinize. Their argument is that judging an action after the episode ends reveals the consequences that acting must anticipate, so the judge has hindsight that the actor does not. That is a plausible and good explanation, but it also means the teacher signal depends on the judge being able to correctly attribute the eventual outcome back to the specific step, which is a credit-assignment problem in its own right, and the whole method only works if the hindsight attribution is actually reliable. The fact that the question phrasing matters so much is evidence for the mechanism, but it is also a warning that the signal is fragile, and a small change in how you frame the judgment can move the policy performance by a meaningful margin, which is exactly the kind of fragility you want to understand before relying on it.

The second concern is the scale and the generality. This is a small policy, a 1.5B model, being taught on two environments, and the improvements are large, but large improvements on a small policy in a controlled environment are not the same as improvements you would see on a larger policy in an open-ended real-world setting. The step-level credit signal from a decision model is only as good as that model's understanding of the environment's dynamics, and in a richer environment the hindsight attribution gets harder, not easier. I would want to see this on a policy that already performs well, where the margin for step-level credit to matter is smaller, because that is the regime where a teacher signal either proves its value or washes out.

Third, the cost structure. The whole appeal of using a decision model as a teacher is that it is cheap to query at every action, but you are now running a decision model at every step of every rollout in addition to the policy, and the compute and wall-clock cost of that has to be weighed against the alternative of just using more rollouts with the outcome reward. The paper makes the capability case, but the practical adoption case depends on the cost per unit of improvement, and that is not the number I would base a training pipeline on without measuring it on my own hardware.

The question-phrasing result deserves more emphasis, because it is the most actionable finding in the paper and it is also the most surprising. The two questions the judge can be asked are whether each action helped achieve the goal, a step-level progress question, and whether the task will succeed after each action, a value or success-probability question. They are intuitively similar, both asking the model to evaluate an action in hindsight, but they produce meaningfully different policies, with the progress question giving the better teaching signal and the success-probability question giving a worse one. The reason this makes sense is that the progress question forces the model to attribute the eventual outcome to the specific step, which is exactly the credit assignment you want, while the success-probability question lets the model hedge, because the probability of success after an action is influenced by how far along the trajectory is, not just whether that action was the right one. So the better question is the one that is more local and more attributable to the action being scored. This is the kind of result that generalizes, because it tells you that when you use a cheap judge for step-level credit, the formulation of the judgment is not a detail, it is a design choice that moves your policy performance.

The bottom line is this: showing that a decision model that fails as an actor can substantially improve a small policy as a teacher, and isolating that the effect depends on asking whether each action helped rather than whether the task will succeed, is a clean and useful result for anyone building long-horizon agents with cheap step-level supervision. But the hindsight credit-assignment that makes it work is fragile and environment-dependent, and I would verify the effect on a stronger baseline policy and in my own environment before treating decision-model teaching as a default training component.
