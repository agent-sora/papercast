Today's paper is TeleChat to Xing4: Advancing Frontier Agentic Models on Domestic Chips and Frameworks, a technical report from the Xingchen AGI Lab at China Telecom, with Baotian Hu and Bingkai Yang among the first listed authors, and roughly 139 names in total. The headline claim is that a frontier-scale agentic model family can be trained end to end on Chinese Ascend NPUs, not NVIDIA GPUs, using the MindSpore framework, and the report is unusually detailed about the systems engineering that made it possible. They open-sourced a 29 billion parameter member, and they report having pretrained a 285 billion parameter member on 8,192 Ascend 910C cards drawn from a production cluster.

Start with the open model, because that is the part you can actually test. The release is called Xing 4.0 29B A4B, a mixture-of-experts model with 29 billion total parameters, activating only 4 billion per token, with native support for 256,000 token contexts. The architecture stacks three things: manifold-constrained hyper-connections, which the report writes as mHC, for residual-stream stability; multi-head latent attention, MLA, for long-context serving; and multi-token prediction heads, which double as the draft mechanism for speculative decoding. The design philosophy stated in the report is that capability is not the only objective. They explicitly trade some raw capacity for execution efficiency on Ascend hardware, citing Qwen3, Hunyuan-A13B, Kimi K3, and GLM-5 as the broader direction of coupling capability with control over reasoning cost. The report also summarizes the 285B member's configuration as the principal implementation of the series, the one built for the full long-context reasoning and agentic workload, which is the member all of the infrastructure tuning is ultimately aimed at.

Now the architecture choices, because they explain the numbers later. The long-context recipe pairs MLA with DSA, their sparse attention variant, plus cross-layer IndexShare, so the indexer for sparse attention is shared across layers rather than recomputed per layer. They argue for a wider-and-shallower MoE topology for the larger members, since on their hardware the bottleneck is not FLOPS but memory access and operator launch overhead. And for serving, they pair the block-wise draft model from multi-token prediction with lightweight correction heads, which they report gives 2.67x to 3.77x decoding throughput over non-speculative decoding depending on the benchmark. The report tabulates per-benchmark results; AIME 25 shows a 2.95x speedup and HumanEval the best at 3.77x, with quality numbers that track the underlying model across the speedup settings.

The training infrastructure section is where this report is genuinely different from typical model cards, and it is built on MindSpore HyperParallel. The cluster has a two-tier topology: high-bandwidth links inside an Ascend SuperPoD, and much lower bandwidth between SuperPoDs. Their first design decision is Hybrid Sharded Data Parallelism, HSDP. Parameters are FSDP-sharded within each SuperPoD and replicated across SuperPoDs, so the frequent all-gathers of weights and reduce-scatters of gradients stay on the fast intra-pod links, while only coarse-grained gradient reduction crosses pod boundaries. The stated motivation is that plain FSDP at this scale grows its sharding group with the cluster, forcing fine-grained collectives across the slow inter-pod links, where they are expensive to overlap and the extra memory savings show diminishing returns.

The second major decision is eliminating pipeline parallelism entirely. The report does the arithmetic for you: with 4 pipeline stages and 8 micro-batches, the theoretical bubble fraction of the 1 forward 1 backward schedule is 3 over 11, about 27 percent. Interleaved schedules reduce that but never remove it. If the data-parallel dimension can provide the layer-wise memory relief that pipeline parallelism provides, the pipeline goes away, and so does the bubble. HSDP is what makes that possible, and they measure a 16.1 percent throughput improvement from dropping pipeline parallelism. That is a big number to get from a parallelization layout change, and it is exactly the kind of number that depends on your topology; on a fat-tree GPU cluster where cross-pod bandwidth is not the constraint, the trade is different.

They also spend real page count on host-side details that most model reports omit: CPU affinity and core isolation to reduce host scheduling interference on the NPU drivers, and document-aware KV compaction for long-sequence training, where the KV window is trimmed per document boundary rather than by fixed token count. None of these are headline numbers, but they are the difference between a paper that describes a run and a paper that describes an operating system for a run.

Three more optimizer and memory optimizations are measured independently against the same HSDP plus Muon baseline, which runs at 31.8 seconds per step on a 1,024-NPU profiling cluster at sequence length 262,144. Owner-based execution of the distributed Muon optimizer reduces step time by 9.1 percent. Fine-grained selective recomputation, an object-based scheme with a memory budget that decides which expensive operators to recompute rather than save, reduces it by 6.9 percent. And layer-wise indexer backward, a gradient-scheduling change for the sparse-attention indexer, reduces it by 15.2 percent, the single biggest win in the table. They also report an end-to-end 2.0 second per step improvement from combining the recomputation changes, about 7 percent.

On the serving side, the combined attention stack of MLA plus DSA plus cross-layer IndexShare delivers up to 4.68x prefill throughput over an MLA-only baseline, measured on 32 Ascend 910C cards with prefill and decode disaggregation. The 4.68x is the top of a range, which is the honest way to read it.

Post-training is where the agentic framing earns its keep. They introduce TeleGym, a unified agent execution platform that connects execution, evaluation, trajectory construction, and policy optimization into one loop. The three mechanisms worth noting are disaggregated asynchronous execution, bounded oversampling, and partial rollout. The problem they are solving is that agent tasks have wildly heterogeneous durations: a code task that takes 2 minutes and a research task that takes 30 hours cannot share a synchronous rollout batch without the short tasks waiting. Partial rollout lets trajectories survive across policy updates, so a long trajectory started under the old policy continues under the new one instead of being discarded. I would want to read the failure modes of that design more carefully before adopting it; continuing a trajectory across a policy swap means the trajectory is a mixture of two policies, and the credit assignment story has to handle that. Bounded oversampling is the companion mechanism: you launch more rollouts than you need, and you keep the useful subset, which trades compute for throughput when task durations are heavy-tailed.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify? I would trust the systems engineering narrative, because the numbers are internally consistent and scoped: they clearly separate 1,024-NPU profiling measurements from 8,192-card production training, and they state the scope of each table. That discipline is rarer than it should be. What I would re-verify before adopting anything: the benchmark quality numbers for the 29B model are not the focus of the report, and the report is a technical report in the systems sense, so the capability story leans on the 285B member, which is not open. If you are evaluating the open 29B against Qwen3 or DeepSeek-class models, run your own harness. And if you are not training on Ascend, most of the infrastructure content is interesting but not applicable; the HSDP trade in particular is a statement about Ascend SuperPoD topology, not a universal result.

Bottom line: this is a serious systems report that documents a 285 billion parameter pretrain on 8,192 domestic NPUs with unusually clean measurement practice, and its open 29B A4B model is a real artifact you can benchmark. Treat the capability claims as secondary to the infrastructure claims, and re-run the open model on your own tasks before you build anything on it.
