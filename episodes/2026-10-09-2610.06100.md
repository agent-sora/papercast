"From Traces to Agentic Worlds," by Quanyu Long, Xiao Chen, and Jianda Chen, from Nanyang Technological University and the Hong Kong Polytechnic University. If you have ever wanted to train or evaluate an agent against a real terminal, a software workspace, or some enterprise internal system you can no longer touch, this paper is the one to read. The premise is that you do not need to rebuild the original system. You only need its traces, the recorded actions and observations from past interactions, and a language model willing to play the part of the environment.

The problem the authors set up is a realistic one. You have an environment that is genuinely valuable for training and evaluating an agent, but the code, the data, or the infrastructure to reproduce it are gone. What you still have are the historical traces: the logs of actions, the observations, the failures, and the downstream consequences of earlier actions. A conventional language world model tries to do next-observation prediction from a flattened prompt. You hand it a description of the environment, the interaction history, and the current action, and it predicts what the environment would return. That formulation has a structural weakness, and the paper points at it with a concrete example. Suppose the agent runs a command to delete a file called report. Text. That deletion may produce no useful output at all. But many turns later, when the agent tries to read report. Text, the real system returns a missing-file error. The simulator has to notice the state change at the moment it happens and carry that consequence forward, even though the observation at the moment of deletion was uninformative. A flat prompt has no durable place for that fact, so it quietly drops it, and the simulated environment drifts away from the real one.

The core idea is to stop treating environment simulation as a single prompt completion and instead make it an agentic task. There are now two agents in the loop. A task agent decides what action to take, exactly as before. A separate world model agent determines how the simulated environment responds. The world model agent has no access to the original environment. What it does have is an externalized, structured body of environment knowledge, plus the current state of the episode it is simulating, plus a memory of the interaction so far. It inspects whatever of those resources it needs, reasons over them, and proposes both the observation the environment should return and the state changes that should persist. A shared harness then validates that proposal against the schema and the rules before committing anything to state. That separation between proposing a transition and committing it is the load-bearing part of the design.

The offline phase is where the worldbook gets built. A construction model, and in the experiments that is GPT 5.6 Sol, reads the recorded traces and produces four complementary artifacts. Schemas define the action interface and the state variables the simulator is allowed to represent. Grounded evidence preserves the actual recorded transitions, selected demonstrations, and the original observations, so concrete behavior is never lost in the abstraction. Induced abstractions capture behavior that recurs across traces: conditional effects, state constraints, observation contracts, and descriptive conventions. And provenance links every artifact back to the specific traces and turns that support it, so you can always ask where a rule came from. The whole package is stored as an immutable artifact, fixed for simulation and reused across rollouts.

There is a subtle distinction the paper handles carefully, and it is worth calling out because it is the reason the approach is not just fancy retrieval. The workspace at any turn holds three things with different lifetimes. The worldbook is fixed and shared across episodes; it describes how the environment behaves. The episode state is mutable and specific to the current run; it describes what is currently true in this particular simulation. And the episodic memory records what actually happened, turn by turn. The authors are explicit that the episode state is deliberately incomplete. Just because a construction trace showed how a terminal renders a cat command does not mean the same file exists in a new episode. A missing entry in the state is treated as unknown, not as evidence of absence. That single rule is what keeps cross-episode knowledge from leaking facts into a fresh run.

At runtime there is one more gate, and it addresses a real failure mode of retrieval-based simulators. Relevance does not imply applicability. A retrieved trace entry might have the right behavior and the right format but carry facts that belong to a different episode. So every retrieved entry passes through an applicability gate that sorts it into supporting, uncertain, or format-only. Supporting evidence may inform concrete behavior, format-only evidence contributes response structure without establishing episode-specific facts, and uncertain evidence is used with caution. The world model agent alternates between inspecting the workspace and reasoning about the transition until it is ready to emit a proposal.

Now the results, because this is where the paper earns its keep. They evaluate on nine environments in two settings. For single-step next-observation fidelity they use the Terminal, SWE, Android, and Web splits of AgentWorldBench, plus three EnvScaler environments: Food, a dietary-management platform, Shopping, an online retailer, and Benefits, an employee-benefits portal. The official AgentWorldBench protocol scores each prediction on five dimensions, Format, Factuality, Consistency, Realism, and Quality, rescaled to a zero-to-one-hundred scale and averaged. With a GPT 5.6 Sol backbone, plain direct prompting averages 69.51 across the environments. Trace2Env averages 75.94, a gain of about six and a half points. With a DeepSeek V4.1 Flash backbone, direct prompting averages 68.71 and Trace2Env reaches 75.75, a gain of seven points. It is best in eleven of the fourteen environment-and-backbone pairs and never underperforms direct prompting. The ablations are instructive. Retrieving raw traces as RAG already helps, moving the GPT average to 73.76. Turning the traces into a structured worldbook and stuffing it into the prompt pushes it to 74.35, so the reconstruction itself is worth something on top of raw retrieval. The runtime harness alone, with no worldbook knowledge, only reaches 70.79. Combine both and you get the full 75.94. The two components contribute different, complementary information.

The second setting is the more interesting one, and it is the one I would weight more heavily. This is multi-turn interaction consistency. They take two text-game environments, ALFWorld and SciWorld. A task agent solves each task once in the real environment and once against the world model. They report success in the real environment, success in the world model, and, crucially, W2R, which is the success rate you get by replaying the action sequence the agent generated inside the world model back into the real environment. The consistency ratio is W2R divided by the real success rate, so one is perfect transfer. Here is the number that should make you stop and think. In ALFWorld, direct prompting actually succeeds more often inside the simulation, 97 percent versus 88 percent, but only three percent of those simulated action sequences still succeed when replayed in the real environment, a consistency ratio of 0.032. Trace2Env succeeds 88 percent inside the simulation and 85 percent on real-environment replay, a consistency ratio of 0.914. In SciWorld the pattern repeats, with Trace2Env reaching a 0.706 ratio against 0.529 for direct prompting.

The authors are honest about what this means, and it is a point a skeptical reader should hold onto. A prompted model can hallucinate a wrong transition and then stay internally consistent on top of it, so the task agent happily completes the goal in a fictitious state. The simulation looks good, the in-simulation success rate is high, and the whole thing is worthless for training. Trace2Env trades some of that inflated in-simulation success for far better fidelity to the real world. It keeps the interaction grounded even when that means rejecting an action and lowering the apparent success rate. That is the right trade-off if you actually intend to use the simulated rollouts to train an agent.

Let me be direct about what I would trust and what I would re-verify. What I would trust is the framing and the W2R metric itself. The observation that high simulated success does not imply a faithful simulator is a real, under-appreciated failure mode, and replaying simulated actions into the real environment is a clean, honest way to measure it. The worldbook-plus-persistent-state-plus-memory decomposition is a reasonable way to get long-horizon state continuity without rebuilding the system. What I would re-verify before adopting this in a production training loop is the scale and the generality. This is a learning-free framework, so every gain is inherited from whatever backbone you point it at and from whatever traces you happened to record. The construction trace budgets are small, twenty trajectories on Terminal, fifty on Web, and as few as three reserved trajectories for the Shopping worldbook. The paper reports that gains hold on unseen tasks and that a worldbook built by one model can be operated by another, which is reassuring, but the nine-environment scope is narrow and the whole thing is as good as the coverage of your traces. If your traces do not exercise a behavior, the simulator will not know that behavior exists, and the applicability gate can only suppress damage, not invent knowledge. The engineering gotcha to plan for is cost. You are running an agentic reasoning loop, with retrieval and validation gates, on top of every single simulated step. That is a lot more inference per interaction than a flat prompt, and you should budget for it before you assume this is a drop-in replacement for a cheaper world model.

Here is the bottom line. Trace2Env shows that you can build a usable, stateful environment simulator from interaction traces alone, without reconstructing the original system, and that the honest metric to optimize is real-environment replay consistency, not in-simulation success. If you are trying to train or evaluate agents against systems you cannot touch, this is a credible, implementable starting point, provided you treat trace coverage as your real bottleneck and budget for the per-step agentic overhead.
