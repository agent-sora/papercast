Sharpening Tax in Post-Training. The first three authors are Changdae Oh, Qi Zeng, and Qi Qi, working across Meta Superintelligence Labs, the University of Wisconsin Madison, New York University, and Stanford.

The question is whether reinforcement-learning post-training does something specific to the base model's behavior distribution, and whether that something has a cost. The dominant observation in the literature is that an RL-trained policy gains accuracy, measured as pass at one, at the expense of solution coverage, measured as pass at K, meaning the number of distinct correct solutions you can recover if you sample many times. The hypothesis the paper tests is that this is distribution sharpening. RL concentrates the model's behavior on the solutions it already found to be reliable, so it pushes tasks toward either almost always solved or almost never solved. The base model with a light inference harness, despite a much lower pass at one, often recovers more distinct solutions given a big enough test-time budget, because its distribution is flatter and still spreads across many ways to reach the answer.

To measure the cost they introduce the Sharpening Tax, a diagnostic that quantifies how much coverage post-training has burned in exchange for the accuracy it gained. Concretely, the tax compares the post-trained model's pass at K to what the base model achieved at the same K, normalized so that a model that simply inherited the base's coverage pays no tax and a model that collapsed its diversity pays a large one. They evaluate it across fourteen base and post-trained model pairs, drawn from four model families, on three agentic benchmarks, for forty-two cases in total. The agentic setting is deliberate. The sharpening hypothesis is most likely to show up where the model has to reason and act over multiple steps, because those are the tasks where the base distribution spreads its attempts across many plausible action sequences and post-training has the most to collapse. The headline is that the tax is real and it is not avoided by task-specific tuning. Even when you do reinforcement-learning fine-tuning on a single domain, the post-trained model still pays the sharpening tax relative to its base, so this is a property of the post-training procedure, not of a particular dataset.

The metric is also designed to be cheap to estimate, which matters for how it would actually be used. The paper argues the tax can be estimated from a modest number of samples without running the full pass at K sweep, so it is practical as a diagnostic you could run on a new post-trained checkpoint before deciding whether its behavior is worth keeping. The theoretical section is where the paper earns its place in the interpretability and analysis lanes: it defines what the tax measures, explains why sharpening charges it, and shows why the proposed sampler reduces it, rather than leaving the metric as a purely empirical curiosity.

The practical workflow the diagnostic enables is worth spelling out, because it is different from how coverage is usually tracked. Normally pass at K is reported as a single number for a model, and the comparison between base and post-trained is implicit, you have to subtract them yourself and then decide whether the drop matters. The Sharpening Tax instead makes the trade a single named quantity that you can threshold on. A post-trained checkpoint that triples pass at one but collapses pass at K carries a large tax and is flagged as over-sharpened even though its headline accuracy looks great. A checkpoint that keeps most of the base coverage while improving accuracy carries a small tax and is the one you would want to ship if your deployment can afford test-time sampling. The difficulty dependence of the tax is what makes PTGS the right shape of fix rather than a global one: because the tax is concentrated on the hard slice of tasks where the base distribution was still broad, the mitigation only needs to preserve sampling breadth on that slice, which is cheaper than preserving it everywhere. That is why a dynamic, difficulty-conditioned temperature can reduce the tax without giving up the accuracy gain on the easy slice. A concrete consequence is that the diagnostic and the fix share the same difficulty estimate. PTGS needs a per-task difficulty signal to decide when to broaden the sampling, and the Sharpening Tax is concentrated on exactly the tasks where that signal says the base model was uncertain. So a deployment that computes the tax to decide whether a checkpoint is over-sharpened is also computing the difficulty profile that tells PTGS where to intervene, and the two uses of the same statistic are not coincidental. The diagnostic is what tells you whether the tax is worth paying, and the sampler is what tells you where you can avoid paying it, and both hinge on the same measurement of how broad the base model's distribution is on each task.

The mitigation is called Posterior-Tempered Group Sampling, or PTGS, and it is a plug-and-play sampler rather than a new objective. The idea is to adjust the sampling temperature dynamically based on task difficulty, estimated from the model's own success statistics on a task. On hard tasks, where coverage still matters and the model is uncertain, it samples more broadly. On easy tasks, where the model is confident, it sharpens to lock in the reliable solution. This is the intuition of a tax break: sharpen the sampling for the easy cases where sharpening is free, but do not collapse coverage on the hard cases where it is expensive. They plug it into standard algorithms, PPO and GRPO, and it improves both pass at one and pass at K while reducing the measured tax.

The clearest numbers are the Sokoban runs with a seven billion parameter model, averaged over five seeds. The base model has a pass at one of 20.7 and a pass at 128 of 76.6, so it is accurate on about a fifth of trials but can recover a solution on three quarters if given 128 samples. Plain PPO raises pass at one to 46.5, nearly triple, but pass at 128 collapses to 55.0, a drop of 21 points, and the tax is 0.094. PPO with PTGS gets pass at one to 61.1, even higher than plain PPO, while keeping pass at 128 at 69.7, and the tax drops to 0.081. The GRPO case is sharper still: plain GRPO has a pass at 128 of 55.3 with a tax of 0.081, while GRPO with PTGS holds pass at 128 to 72.5 and cuts the tax to 0.025, so PTGS recovers almost all of the base model's coverage while still beating the base on accuracy. FrozenLake shows the same pattern with even larger base coverage, and the tax is reduced there too, so the effect is not an artifact of one environment.

Skeptically, the mitigation experiments run on two grid-world environments, Sokoban and FrozenLake, which are clean and well understood but a far cry from the open-ended agentic tasks the diagnostic itself is motivated by, so the generalization of PTGS beyond toy RL is the main open gap. The diagnostic is a measurement of a well-motivated phenomenon, but the tax is defined relative to the base model's coverage, so part of what it captures is how much diversity the base model had to begin with, which varies a lot across families. And improving pass at one on Sokoban from 46 to 61 while holding coverage is a good result, but the absolute coverage, 69 out of 128, is still below the base, so the tax is reduced, not eliminated. The honest read is a useful and well-posed diagnostic for a real and consistent tradeoff, plus a simple sampler that meaningfully softens it, with the caveat that the evidence for the fix lives in simple environments.
