The paper is called "Beneath the Surface: What We Overlook When We Talk About Agent Evolution." The first three authors are Xiaobo Wang, Zimu Wang, and Siyu Ye. The work is a collaboration across the University of Science and Technology of China, the University of California, Berkeley, and Simple Agent Lab, with co-authors from the Beijing Institute for General Artificial Intelligence and Shanghai Jiaotong University.

The setting is recursive self-improvement, the idea of an agent that keeps revising its own design based on experience. The authors focus on one concrete form of it: agent harness evolution. In harness evolution you hold the model weights completely fixed, and you change only the surrounding system that shapes what the model sees and turns its outputs into actions. Depending on the harness, those changes can target the prompts, the skills, the tool interfaces, the orchestration logic, or even the harness source code. A whole line of recent frameworks closes the loop between execution, diagnosis, mutation, and evaluation, and they report gains on standard benchmarks. The authors' thesis is that a higher overall benchmark score does not mean a more useful agent.

The core problem is that a single aggregate score averages over tasks and trials. A version of the agent that newly solves some tasks can look better even while it quietly breaks other tasks that it used to handle. The score does not show you deployment cost, and it does not show you performance on tasks that were not the ones guiding the evolution. So if you rely on that score alone, you can select a brittle or expensive agent version and mistake "it adapted to the tasks we evolved on" for "it got generally better." The whole paper is organized around three research questions. First, phenomenology: does evolution give reliable and efficient improvements, or does it trade off coverage against regressions and hide costs? Second, generalization: do the gains survive when you deploy the evolved agent on tasks or environments it was not evolved on? Third, mechanisms: which specific evolutionary operators actually create the useful changes, and how are those changes preserved?

To answer these they evaluate four existing methods, AHE, HyperAgents, A-Evolve, and GEPA, using two harnesses, MiniSWE and Codex, on two kinds of tasks. General tasks come from a benchmark they call tau-3-Banking, and coding tasks come from Terminal-Bench 2. Their experimental toolkit is where the paper is most useful if you want to reproduce the style of analysis: repeated evaluations to measure reliability, controlled expansion of the agent, interventions on individual components, and what they call admission replays, where you replay the selection and gating decisions to see what gets kept and what gets discarded.

The four-stage loop that all of these methods share is worth laying out, because the paper's mechanism analysis is organized around it. Each iteration records the current agent's behavior, selects a set of candidate changes to try, rolls them out on the evaluation tasks, analyzes the results to decide which changes helped and which hurt, and then gates the survivors into the next generation. The mutation step is where the actual edits to prompts, skills, tool interfaces, or code happen, and the gate is where the selection decision is made. The authors' point is that the interesting behavior lives at the gate and in the mutation, not at the aggregate score, and their intervention methods let them hold all the other stages fixed while they perturb one.

The headline phenomenology finding is uncomfortable and consistent. Across all sixteen evolved agents they study, every single one gains previously uncovered tasks while simultaneously losing previously covered ones. So the evolution is redistributing task success, not uniformly adding it. In one general-task example, measured reliability rises by 5.2 points even though the agent loses ten tasks it had previously covered. And most of those reliability gains come with an increase in deployment cost, so the improvement is not free. Specific, targeted changes tend to outperform generic reminders like "be careful," but their value can flip when you combine two changes that each helped on their own.

The generalization findings are selective. The highest-scoring evolved agent does not produce the largest mean improvement in any of their four evaluation pools, and a stricter admission rule, one that is more selective about which candidate changes to keep, can actually discard useful alternatives rather than improve them. When they transfer an evolved agent to a new task set, coverage usually falls on the general-task target across both harness types and both paradigms, but it rises on the coding target when you use what they call the outer-layer-only harness. So the value of the change depends heavily on where and how you deploy it.

There is a second, quieter point that I think is the one people will reuse. The paper keeps separating three things that the field tends to collapse into a single number. Aggregate performance is the mean score across tasks. Coverage is how many distinct tasks the agent can handle at all, and it is tracked per task, not averaged away. And reliability, which they frame as a cost-weighted notion, folds in how much the agent spends to get its results. The reason all three matter is that a single evolving agent can move these in opposite directions at the same time, which is precisely what the gain-while-losing pattern shows. A method that optimizes the mean while silently shrinking the task set it can handle, or that holds accuracy while doubling its cost, will look fine on the headline and quietly degrade in deployment. That is why the authors argue that reporting a single evolved-agent score is insufficient, and it is a methodological position that does not depend on any of their specific numbers being right.

The constructive follow-up is a system they call EvoAudit, which they also refer to as Audit-Guided Evolution, AGE. It takes the diagnostics from the three research questions and turns them into concrete revisions to the four stages of the evolution loop, select, analyze, mutate, and gate. Each proposed revision comes with the supporting evidence, the expected effect, and the applicability conditions under which it should hold. In their tests, this audit-guided version achieves the best performance of the methods they study. The notable thing about the design is that it does not add a new evolutionary operator. It changes how the existing stages make their decisions, so that the selection and gating steps explicitly account for coverage preservation and cost, rather than just for the mean score. That is a smaller and more auditable change than swapping in a new mutation strategy, which is probably why it is robust across the task families they test.

Now the skeptical read. This is largely an analysis paper rather than a systems paper, and its value depends on how far you can generalize from four methods and two task families. The claim that all sixteen evolved agents show the gain-while-losing pattern is a strong empirical regularity, but it is established on a specific set of benchmarks and harnesses; whether it holds for other agent designs is exactly the open question. The numbers are directionally interesting, the 5.2-point reliability gain alongside ten lost tasks is a vivid illustration, but the effect sizes are small and the cost accounting is described rather than fully itemized. The EvoAudit result is a positive, but it is also measured by the same group with the same evaluation stack, so it is not an independent confirmation that the audit loop beats the baselines. What I would trust is the methodological point, that aggregate benchmark scores are insufficient for evaluating self-modifying agents, and that you need task-level coverage, regression tracking, cost, and transfer to judge a change. What I would re-verify before building on EvoAudit is whether its gains survive outside the authors' own evaluation pools, and how sensitive the selection and gating revisions are to the particular admission thresholds they chose.

The bottom line: this paper makes a well-evidenced case that agent-harness evolution improves average scores by reshuffling which tasks get solved, not by cleanly adding capability, and that cost and transfer are where the real trade-offs live. Its practical takeaway is the evaluation discipline, not any single method, and the audit-guided variant is a reasonable but still in-house demonstration that that discipline can be turned into a better loop.
