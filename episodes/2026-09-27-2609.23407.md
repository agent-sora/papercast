OmniEcho, Audio-Visual Spatial Understanding for Omni-Modal Embodied Agents, by Ruixun Liu, Yuxuan Wang, and Jiacheng Xie, from Peking University, Alibaba Group, and Tsinghua University. The work sits at the intersection of Peking University's School of Intelligence Science and Technology and its State Key Laboratory of General Artificial Intelligence, with the model side coming out of Alibaba's Token Hub, and part of the work done during an internship at Alibaba. The project page and the benchmark are released, which is the part that makes this a usable contribution rather than a closed demonstration.

Humans localize sound effortlessly. You can identify where a sound came from, triangulate it against what you see, and reason over the combined signal without thinking about it. A dog barking behind a wall, a phone vibrating in a back pocket, a car passing on your left, all of it is a spatial signal you use continuously to build a model of the scene. Embodied agents cannot do this well, and the paper's opening claim is that the field does not even have a clean way to evaluate spatial audio understanding in embodied settings. Most audio benchmarks reduce the sound field to stereo or mono, which throws away exactly the directional information the task requires, and most navigation benchmarks give the agent a text instruction to follow, which is a fundamentally easier problem than navigating from a sound. So OmniEcho delivers two things: a benchmark, OmniEchoBench, that keeps the sound field intact, and a model that is built to pass it.

The benchmark has a specific shape. Six tasks, built over 197 real-world spatial audio-visual scenes, with 2,972 question-answer pairs and 900 navigation samples. The audio is first-order ambisonics, or FOA, which is the four-channel format that captures the full sound field around a listener rather than a stereo pair. The scenes were collected from 30 real-world environments, which matters because synthetic spatial audio datasets are plentiful and real captured sound fields are not. The six tasks span perception and reasoning on the audio-visual scene plus audio-vision-language navigation, where the agent must actually move toward or away from a sound source described in language. To make the benchmark usable for training and not just evaluation, the authors also build a controllable rendering pipeline for spatial audio that preserves geometric consistency among the sound sources, the visual observations, and the agent trajectories. That pipeline is what makes it possible to generate matched training supervision from the same scene geometry.

The model side is OmniEcho, built on Qwen3-Omni, specifically the 30B parameter, 3B active, mixture-of-experts variant. That is a deliberately small active footprint for an omni-modal model, which matters because the model has to run in a loop on a robot, where inference latency and power budget are real constraints, not afterthoughts, and because a 3B active count is the kind of number that can run on edge hardware rather than only in the cloud. The design decision that does most of the work is a split audio pathway. The model keeps the pretrained semantic audio pathway from Qwen3-Omni untouched, and adds a new spatial pathway on top of a purpose-built FOA encoder that ingests the four-channel ambisonics signal. A cross-attention localization head attends to the encoder tokens to produce spatial outputs. The reasoning behind the split is that the base model already has a well-tuned sense of what sounds mean, the semantic pathway, and you do not want to perturb that alignment while teaching it a new sense of where sounds are. Training is a three-stage procedure whose explicit goal is to align the semantic and spatial representations while keeping the semantic pathway stable, so the model learns the spatial pathway on top rather than re-learning audio understanding from scratch. The practical consequence is that the model can reason about what a sound is, where it is, and what the agent should do about it, in a single forward pass, with the FOA audio as a first-class input modality rather than something decoded to mono first. The controllable rendering pipeline I described earlier is what feeds this training: because it keeps sound sources, visual observations, and agent trajectories geometrically consistent, the supervision signal is clean, the model is not being asked to infer spatial relationships from mismatched audio and video.

The evaluation has two halves. On spatial question answering, the paper's central comparative claim is that existing omni-modal models, including the base Qwen3-Omni without the spatial pathway, struggle on the benchmark, while OmniEcho reaches state-of-the-art performance on the spatial perception tasks. This is the benchmark doing its job: it is designed to be hard enough that current omni-modal models, which were never trained to reason over a full sound field, expose a real gap, and the model the authors pair with it is the one that closes that gap. The benchmark and the model are presented as a matched pair, which is the honest way to introduce a new capability axis, you define the axis and then show a model that can use it. On sound-guided navigation, the agent receives the FOA audio stream and a goal description, and must decide autonomously when to terminate, with no route-level text guidance and no goal image. OmniEcho achieves a 16.2 percent success rate and 11.5 percent on the step-level success metric, with a path length ratio of 4.09. That is a strong result against the conventional baselines: Seq2Seq at 9.8 percent and CMA at 8.5 percent, and it beats the binaural-audio SoundSpaces baseline, which manages 5.4 percent, by a wide margin. The honest comparison is against InternVLA-N1, a visual-language navigation agent using Qwen2.5-VL-7B with text instructions: it scores 17.8 percent success and 16.7 percent on the step metric, with a tighter path length ratio of 2.87. So OmniEcho is lower by 1.6 points on success rate and 5.2 points on the step metric, despite navigating from a much harder input, pure spatial audio rather than a text instruction. The authors frame this as sound-guided navigation in the real world still being substantially harder than text-guided VLN, and the gap to the visual baseline is the size of that difficulty.

Skeptical assessment. What I would trust: the benchmark's data provenance. Real-world captured scenes with genuine first-order ambisonics, geometric consistency between audio and vision, and a controlled rendering pipeline for generating training supervision is a real contribution to the field, because the alternative is a pile of synthetic datasets that models can overfit to without learning anything transferable about real sound fields. The pathway-split design is also the right engineering call for the problem: freezing the semantic audio pathway and grafting a spatial one is cheaper to train, easier to debug, and preserves the base model's audio-linguistic alignment by construction.

What I would re-verify: the navigation numbers in context. A 16.2 percent success rate in absolute terms is low, and the paper's framing, that this is close to VLN performance on a harder task, is defensible but not self-evident. On the real-world benchmark the trajectory-length ratio is 4.09 for OmniEcho versus 2.87 for the visual baseline, and on the simulated VLN-CE cross-model comparison the trajectory length climbs to 7.02, which is the kind of inefficiency that matters for deployment cost and safety. I would also want to see the per-task breakdown on the six benchmark tasks, because a single aggregate over perception and navigation tasks can hide that one or two tasks are driving the result.

Bottom line: OmniEcho is a competent two-part contribution, a genuinely useful real-world spatial audio benchmark and a model that is the first to treat first-order ambisonics as a native input modality for embodied agents, with honest reporting that it still trails visual-language navigation on success rate. For anyone building audio-visual embodied systems, the benchmark and the FOA encoder plus pathway-split recipe are the parts worth taking.
