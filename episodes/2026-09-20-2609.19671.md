When2Think: Learning Difficulty-Aware Length Control for Efficient Hybrid Reasoning Models. The first three authors are Jaejun Shim, HyunJin Kim, and Young Jin Kim, from Sungkyunkwan University and Microsoft, with the final author JinYeong Bak at Sungkyunkwan University.

The paper addresses a well-documented inefficiency in large reasoning models, the systems that generate long chains of thought before answering. These models are strong on hard problems, but they do not adapt their effort to the problem in front of them. They overthink easy problems, burning hundreds of reasoning tokens on a question that needed a one-line answer, and they underthink hard ones, terminating the chain too early or producing a fragmented argument. The existing fixes come in two flavors, and both have a cost. A uniform length penalty, a reward that just punishes long outputs, cuts the token budget everywhere, including on the hard problems where the length is doing real work. Rigid routing, deciding in advance whether a problem gets the fast path or the slow path, commits before the model has actually seen how hard the instance is. The authors' formulation is that efficient reasoning is an instance-adaptive computation allocation problem: the right amount of thinking depends on the specific instance, and the model should learn to allocate it.

Their method is When2Think, a post-training framework for hybrid reasoning. The core mechanism is called IDAC, Instance-level Difficulty-Aware Control. It is a reward-shaping mechanism, and the key ingredient is a set of pre-computed reference statistics. For each problem in the training set, before training begins, the authors compute two numbers from a reference model: the accuracy, how often the reference gets the problem right, and the token usage, how many reasoning tokens the reference typically spends. Those two numbers define a difficulty profile for the instance. During training, IDAC uses that profile to shape the reward: on easy instances, the reward favors short direct answers; on hard instances, it favors the extended reasoning the problem actually needs. Combined with verifier-based rewards and batch-wise standardized advantages, the optimization is critic-free: it does not require a learned reward model or online queries to a reference model during training. The result is a model that learns when to use what the authors call system one, a direct no-think answer, versus system two, extended reasoning.

The headline results are on mathematical benchmarks, and the numbers are the point of the paper. On AIME24, Pass at three, the success rate when the model is given three attempts, increases by 10.0 percent relative to the base model, while token usage drops by 27.9 percent. Those two numbers moving in the same favorable direction is the whole argument: the model got better and cheaper at the same time, which is exactly what a uniform length penalty cannot give you, because a uniform penalty trades accuracy on hard instances for savings on easy ones. On AIME25, When2Think reaches a Pass at three of 40.0 percent, outperforming compression baselines and routing-only baselines. The comparison set matters here, because the alternative approaches, pure compression and pure routing, are precisely the two things the paper argues have the efficiency tax, and the result is that adapting per-instance beats both.

The mechanism is worth examining because it sidesteps a common trap in reward-shaping for reasoning length. The naive move is to add a penalty term that grows with output length and tune its coefficient. That works on easy problems and quietly sabotages hard ones, because the coefficient is global. IDAC's move is to make the control instance-conditional: the reference accuracy and token usage for that specific problem determine how the reward is shaped for that specific problem. A problem the reference solves easily gets a reward that rewards brevity; a problem the reference struggles with gets a reward that tolerates or even encourages the longer chain. The pre-computation is what makes this tractable at training time: the difficulty signal is looked up, not estimated online, so the critic-free optimization stays stable. The batch-wise standardized advantages handle the scaling of the reward across a batch of instances with different difficulty, which is a non-trivial detail because a raw reward difference between a one-token answer and a thousand-token answer would otherwise dominate the gradient.

What would I trust? The problem framing and the core mechanism. The observation that reasoning models allocate computation uniformly when they should allocate it adaptively is well-established, and the efficiency tax of the two standard fixes, uniform penalties and rigid routing, is a fair characterization. The pre-computed reference statistics are a clean way to get an instance-level difficulty signal without an online critic, and the fact that it is critic-free makes it easier to reproduce. The result of improved accuracy and reduced tokens on the same benchmark, with both numbers moving favorably, is the strongest kind of evidence for this kind of method, because it rules out the explanation that the model is just trading away accuracy to save tokens.

What would I re-verify before building on it? First, the reference statistics are computed from a single reference model, and the whole method inherits that reference's notion of difficulty. If the reference model has an atypical difficulty profile, problems it finds easy that a deployment model finds hard, or vice versa, the difficulty signal is wrong for the student. I would want to see how sensitive the results are to which reference model computed the statistics, because that is a hidden dependency. Second, the evaluation is on AIME, a mathematical benchmark, and the difficulty-adaptive behavior is demonstrated there. I would want to see whether the same instance-conditional allocation transfers to domains where difficulty is less cleanly separable, because the reference accuracy and token usage that define difficulty are cleanest on problems with a verifiable answer. In open-ended reasoning, where there is no verifier to score the answer, the verifier-based reward that anchors the optimization is harder to define, and the difficulty signal becomes less reliable. Third, the 27.9 percent token reduction is measured against the base model on the same benchmark, but I would want to know the distribution of the savings, whether it comes almost entirely from the easy instances, as it should, and whether the hard instances actually get more tokens rather than fewer, because a method that cuts tokens everywhere but keeps accuracy stable on the easy set is not doing the adaptive allocation it claims.

One more point on the system one versus system two framing. The paper describes the learned behavior as learning when to answer directly versus when to reason, and that is the right way to think about it, but it is worth noting that the decision is being made by the model itself at inference time, conditioned on the input, not by an external router. That is a meaningful difference from the rigid-routing baselines, which make the fast-or-slow decision with a fixed rule. The model-internal decision is more flexible, but it also means the failure mode is a misjudgment of difficulty at inference time on an unseen problem, and the pre-computed training-time statistics do not directly protect against that.

There is a second practical consideration that the benchmark numbers do not fully capture. The 27.9 percent token reduction is an average over the benchmark distribution, and the value of the method in deployment depends on the difficulty mix of the actual workload. If a production workload is mostly hard problems, the savings will be smaller than the benchmark average, because the model is spending its tokens where the method tells it to spend them. If the workload is mostly easy problems, the savings will be larger. The method's economics are therefore workload-conditional in a way that the AIME numbers do not reveal, and anyone estimating the cost savings of adopting this approach should measure the difficulty distribution of their own workload rather than importing the benchmark percentage. The same logic applies to the accuracy side: the plus 10.0 percent Pass at three is measured on problems where the base model's uniform allocation is miscalibrated, and on a workload where the base model is already allocating reasonably well, the room for the adaptive allocation to help is smaller. The method is a correction to miscalibration, and its value is proportional to how miscalibrated the deployment actually is.

Bottom line: When2Think is a well-motivated answer to the overthink-and-underthink problem in reasoning models, and its headline result, plus 10.0 percent Pass at three on AIME24 while cutting tokens by 27.9 percent, is exactly the accuracy-plus-efficiency gain that uniform length penalties cannot produce. The mechanism, pre-computed per-instance difficulty statistics shaping a critic-free reward, is clean and reproducible; the open question is how dependent the difficulty signal is on the particular reference model that computed it, and whether the adaptive allocation holds up outside mathematical benchmarks.
