Paper five is Discovery Foundation Models: Toward Open-Ended Discovery Intelligence, from the DFM Scientist Collaboration Program, with Ling Yang, Zhenfei Yin, and Yingcheng Wu among the listed authors. This is a position-and-system paper rather than a single-model release, and its ambition is to define a new class of model. The argument is that the useful next capability for a foundation model is not a bigger benchmark score on existing tasks, but the ability to discover: to propose a hypothesis about an unexplored region of some scientific domain, design an experiment or search to test it, interpret the result, and update the hypothesis, repeatedly, without a human deciding in advance what counts as a discovery. They call this open-ended discovery intelligence, and they argue that it is a distinct capability from reasoning, from tool use, and from the open-ended generation that earlier "agent" systems have demoed, because the loop must close on something that was not in the training data and that no benchmark can enumerate. Their core objection is to the standard task abstraction, where the problem, its representation, the objective, the tools, and the evaluator are all supplied as fixed inputs: in that setup the model can be brilliant and still have done zero discovery, because the discovery was already done by whoever framed the task. A discovery foundation model, in their formulation, operates over a revisable research state in which even the problem and the representation are things the model can change.

The paper formalizes this with a set of seven coupled capabilities, named in the paper as problem discovery, problem formulation, representation construction, hypothesis formation, intervention, evidence-grounded revision, and continual discovery improvement. Translated into plain language: the model must find the problem worth working on, not just solve one it is handed; it must build the representation, which in science means the variables and the model, before it can reason; it must generate candidate hypotheses that are both novel and falsifiable; it must be able to design and execute an intervention that could actually distinguish between the candidates, which in practice means calling wet-lab equipment, a simulation, or a literature search; it must interpret the outcome, including a negative one, and ground the revision in evidence rather than in its own confidence; and it must improve at the discovery process over time, carrying validated experience forward into the next investigation. The last capability is the one most agent papers skip, and it is the one that separates discovery from a very long chain of tool calls, because a system that never abandons a dead end is just a very persistent search procedure, not a scientist. The authors are explicit that the definition is capability-based rather than tied to a particular architecture, model checkpoint, or level of autonomy, which means a DFM can in principle be a single neural net or a human-supervised pipeline, as long as all seven capabilities are present and integrated.

They then describe Zetema, a reference instantiation of a discovery foundation model. Zetema is deliberately not one monolithic model; it is a system built around one constraint, namely that any scientifically consequential operation must be gated. It maintains an explicit research state, which is the model's current belief about what is known, what is being tested, and what has been ruled out; it supports branching and rollback within an investigation, so a dead end can be set aside without losing the rest of the program; it gates consequential actions through verification and a Research World Model before they are executed; it connects what the paper calls Dry-Lab reasoning to external computational or physical evidence; and it converts validated cross-task experience into what it terms Discovery Skills, reusable procedures distilled from past investigations so that the system gets better at the discovery process itself, not just the current target.

They ground the framework with GALILEO, a real dry-lab and wet-lab case in the therapeutic space. GALILEO couples multi-omics-informed target nomination and peptide design with robotic synthesis, multimodal phenotyping, and orthogonal hands-on assays, with repeated evidence-driven revision. The two validated branches are LRRC8C and SLC25A1, and across five optimization rounds, the physical measurements from each round altered the system's subsequent target beliefs, assay choices, mechanism hypotheses, and molecular-design policies, with the accumulated feedback consolidated into a transferable design rule the authors call the Amphiphilic Balance Grammar, a recurring structural pattern in the top-scoring peptides that standard structure-based design pipelines had not surfaced, which they argue is the kind of finding that only shows up if the model is allowed to keep going past the first obviously good hit and keep asking why the good hits are good.

The training formulation follows the same state-centered view. Instead of training only on final answers, the paper targets the intermediate decisions that actually change a research program: which hypothesis to keep, which assay to run next, where to allocate limited resources across formulation, representation, hypothesis construction, intervention, falsification, and verification. It uses trajectories, interactive environments, process supervision, and scientific feedback, which is a deliberate departure from the end-of-episode reward that drives most reinforcement-learning agent work, because in a discovery setting the end of an episode is rarely a clean success or failure, and rewarding only the terminal state teaches the model to be cautious rather than curious.

The numbers in this paper are different in kind from the other four on today's show. There is no benchmark table with a state-of-the-art cell, because the claim is not that Zetema wins a leaderboard, it is that the loop produced a structurally coherent family of binders that a non-discovery pipeline would not have produced, and that the model's written rationale for each round of choices is at least partially right about why those peptides worked. The evaluation formulation is explicit about this: validation is not reduced to a universal scalar reward, and instead logical checks, held-out execution, simulation, replication, independent reviewers, and physical measurement are each treated as supporting different claims, with the system required to preserve which validator supported which state transition and to abstain or escalate when the available evidence does not justify promotion. Evaluation itself measures two things: externally validated progress in the current episode, and improvement in future discovery behavior under matched resources and retrieval controls, which is an honest way of saying that a discovery system should be judged on whether it gets better, not just on whether it got one answer right. The authors are careful to say that this is one domain, two proteins, five rounds, and that the generalization claim is a hypothesis, not a result.

My skeptical read: the seven-capability definition is useful as a checklist and thin as a scientific theory, because it does not say what a discovery is, in the way that a philosopher of science would demand, and "novel and falsifiable" is doing a lot of work that the paper never operationalizes beyond taste. The Zetema system is a wrapper around a general LLM plus a fixed set of tools, so the "foundation model" in the name is aspirational, and the real contribution is the GALILEO loop design and the five-round dataset, which is a small but real artifact that other groups can now use. The Amphiphilic Balance Grammar result is the strongest claim in the paper and the one worth checking, because a structural pattern that standard design pipelines missed, found and then consolidated by the loop, is a genuinely new kind of evidence for what these systems can do, even if the word "discovery" is still doing more work than the evidence supports. Bottom line: this is the most conceptually ambitious paper of the day and the least numerically supported of the six, the seven-capability framing will probably survive even if this specific instantiation does not, and the five-round GALILEO loop is the concrete thing to cite.
