Axion 1.0: Open Agent Foundation Models, Simplified, by Yuqi Jia, Yunjie Tian, and Jiayu Zheng, from Duke University, ByteDance, the University of Maryland College Park, and the University at Buffalo, is a release of two fully open agent foundation models, a 35-billion-parameter model with 3 billion active and a 122-billion-parameter model with 10 billion active, both initialized from Qwen3.5 mixture-of-experts checkpoints. The pitch is in the title, simplified. The training recipe is only three stages: two lightweight supervised fine-tuning stages followed by reinforcement learning, with no dedicated mid-training, no direct preference optimization, and no on-policy distillation. The second claim, and the one that matters more for the open ecosystem, is that they release everything, the complete recipe including data construction, generation, filtering, verification, prompts, and training code, so the whole development process is reproducible. That combination, an open agent foundation model with a deliberately short pipeline, is the actual contribution, and the rest of the paper is the evidence that the short pipeline is not a shortcut.

The first stage develops self-contained reasoning through what they call model-aware data construction. The process is adversarial in a specific sense: they synthesize difficult problems, then check whether the base model can solve them. A problem the base model answers incorrectly goes straight into the candidate bank, because it is already hard enough. A problem it answers correctly gets strengthened using one of four transformations, and the strengthened version is re-tested. The base model is allowed to use Python execution during this phase but has search and webpage visits disabled, so the reasoning it must produce is self-contained rather than delegated to a tool. They then have a separate model, GPT-5.5, independently solve each accepted problem three times, and the problem is only kept if it survives that independent verification, which is a guard against the data pipeline quietly injecting unsolvable or ambiguous problems. The effect is a curriculum that is hard by construction relative to the specific base model being trained, which is a different thing from a static difficulty-graded dataset. The second SFT stage extends the model to tool-dependent reasoning. Here the data pipeline is a scalable construction of diverse tasks grounded in real-world data sources and deterministic computation, which gives them automatically verifiable outcomes at scale, because the answer is computed rather than judged. The design intent is a progression: independent reasoning first, grounded interaction second, and then reinforcement learning on top.

The reinforcement learning setup is worth describing because it is the part most likely to be re-implemented. The infrastructure builds on verl's fully asynchronous policy module, and it separates rollout generation, policy optimization, and auxiliary services onto distinct GPU pools. Rollout workers use vLLM, and for the 122-billion model they run ten tensor-parallel-4 rollout engines on 40 GPUs. The trainer starts an update once it has a batch of 24 prompts with non-degenerate rewards, and every two optimizer steps the updated weights are transferred to the rollout engines through NCCL, taking 45 to 60 seconds for the 122-billion model. In-flight rollouts continue with the updated weights, and any trajectory more than 11 parameter versions old is discarded, which bounds how stale the rollouts can get relative to the policy being trained. For open-ended tasks they use judge models and six 9-billion instances for webpage summarization and context condensation. They also adopt rollout routing replay, a technique that re-routes rollouts to keep the sequence used for training consistent with the one used for rollout, and they report it improves rollout-trainer consistency, measured by the mean sequence-average log-probability gap, from 0.044 to 0.042, while lifting the expert-level exam from 72.3 to 74.6 percent at the 122-billion scale, at the cost of a 4.5 percent drop on SEAL-0. That trade-off is exactly the kind of detail a re-implementation would need.

On results, the headline is that the compact recipe lands competitive with leading commercial models on several agentic benchmarks, and the ablations are the more interesting part because they show what each stage is actually doing. The 130-question text-only subset of Humanity's Last Exam is their clearest progress bar. For the 35-billion model it climbs from 47.7 percent at the base to 59.2 percent after the first stage, 66.2 percent after the second, and 70.0 percent after reinforcement learning. For the 122-billion model it goes from 40.8 percent to 68.5 percent after the first stage and 74.6 percent after reinforcement learning. The first stage is doing the heavy lifting on reasoning; the second stage, which is about tools and external data, actually drops the 35-billion model's BioMysteryBench score from 23.5 to 17.6 percent before the later stages recover it. That dip is an honest detail. It means the tool stage is not free, it temporarily taxes pure reasoning before the full pipeline pays it back. The same second stage then lifts the 35-billion model's BioMysteryBench from 17.6 to 35.3 percent and FrontierScience Research from 48.3 to 58.3 percent, which is where the tool-dependent data starts to compound rather than compete with the reasoning gains.

Against similarly sized open models the comparison is favorable. Against the comparable open Agents-A1 model, Axion 1.0-35B improves by 4.6 percent on the expert-level exam, 5.8 percent on SEAL-0, and 7.0 percent on the FrontierScience Olympiad. On FrontierScience Research the two models score 56.7 and 66.7 percent, against 40.0 percent for Agents-A1 and 26.7 percent for GPT-5.5, and the 122-billion model also exceeds GPT-5.6 Sol by 3.4 points. On BioMysteryBench the 122-billion model reaches 41.7 percent, against 47.1 for GPT-5.6 Sol and 46.1 for Claude Fable 5, so it is close but not ahead on that one. The transfer study is useful: they train 4-billion and 9-billion models with the same two SFT stages but no reinforcement learning, and the 9-billion beats the 4-billion on all six benchmarks, with the expert-level exam moving from 53.1 to 63.1 percent and BioMysteryBench from 11.8 to 23.5 percent. That is evidence the recipe scales down, not just up, which is exactly what an open release needs, because the people most likely to adopt the recipe are the ones who cannot afford the 122-billion run. One more detail worth keeping: after the supervised stages, the gap between the two model sizes on the expert-level exam collapses from 9.2 percent to 0.8 percent, which suggests the recipe, not the parameter count, is what sets the ceiling on reasoning in this setup, at least up to the scale they tested.

The skeptical read. The benchmark suite is heavily science-reasoning and search-heavy, with Humanity's Last Exam, HiPhO, FrontierScience, and BioMysteryBench, so the numbers say a lot about scientific agentic reasoning and less about software engineering or general tool use, which is where most deployed agents live. The self-reported scores are theirs to compute, and while they note which baselines were evaluated by whom, the comparison set mixes provider-reported and independently evaluated numbers. The reinforcement learning gains are real but uneven: on the 35-billion model reinforcement learning hurts full HiPhO by 1.1 percent and FrontierScience Research by 1.6 percent, so it is not a free lunch at every size. And the model-aware data construction leans on GPT-5.5 as a verification oracle, which is fine for a one-time recipe but means the data quality is tied to a proprietary frontier model that the open community cannot rerun identically.

Bottom line: Axion 1.0 is the rare open agent release where the pipeline is genuinely short and fully disclosed, and the staged ablations show each step doing distinct, checkable work. The science-reasoning numbers are strong and the scaling-down results are encouraging, but the evaluation is narrow and the data oracle is proprietary, so treat the benchmark lead as a real but bounded signal, and re-run the recipe's data construction on your own hardware before trusting the exact figures.
