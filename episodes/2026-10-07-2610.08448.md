Rethinking Cross-Tokenizer On-Policy Distillation is from Bingxi Hou, Guochao Jiang, and Guofeng Quan, all at Alibaba Cloud Computing, with the full team of seven authors and Yuewei Zhang as the corresponding author. The paper takes a narrow, well-scoped question that a lot of people are quietly asking right now: when you distill a student model from a teacher model that uses a completely different tokenizer, how much of the alignment machinery actually matters? The answer they land on is blunt: less than you think. The strict one-to-one alignment positions already carry nearly all of the signal, and the elaborate machinery people build to cover the rest of the vocabulary is, in their experiments, actively harmful.

On-policy distillation trains a student on its own sampled generations, using the teacher's predictions as the supervision target. That works cleanly when both models share a tokenizer. Cross-tokenizer distillation is where it gets ugly, because the same decoded text breaks into different token boundaries on the student and teacher sides, and the next-token distributions live over two different vocabularies. The paper partitions a student response into aligned token groups, intervals between shared boundaries. Most of those intervals are strict one-to-one groups, where exactly one student token and one teacher token span the same text. The rest are mismatch groups, where you need multiple tokens on at least one side to cover the same span. The baseline loss is a reverse KL divergence computed only at the strict positions, with both distributions restricted and renormalized to the shared vocabulary.

The first thing worth knowing is how much strict alignment there actually is. Static vocabulary Jaccard overlap across their three teacher-student pairs ranges from about 39 to 65 percent, which on its face looks like a big gap. But dynamic coverage on the student's own rollouts is much higher: 85.6 to 97 percent of student tokens fall into strict one-to-one groups, and 83 to 97 percent on the teacher side. The most extreme case is Granite 4.1 8B distilling into Phi 4 mini, where static Jaccard overlap is the lowest at 39.5 percent, yet strict token coverage is the highest of the three pairs, nearly 97 percent on both sides. The paper's framing for this is that static overlap counts vocabulary entries equally, but in practice the probability mass on a student's own trajectories is concentrated on the shared entries.

That concentration is the second diagnostic. On responses sampled from the students before distillation, at strictly aligned positions, the shared vocabulary retains nearly all predictive mass in both models. In their Qwen 2.5 7B to Llama 3.2 3B pair, the shared vocabulary holds 99.7 percent of mass on both sides, and a student-selected top-16 subset of the shared vocabulary still retains about 94 percent on each side. Only about 0.3 percent of mass sits outside the shared vocabulary at all. So the "uncovered" vocabulary that all the fancier methods try to recover is, mass-wise, a rounding error.

The experiments run three heterogeneous pairs: Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct into Llama 3.2 3B Instruct, Granite 4.1 8B into Phi 4 mini Instruct, and Granite 4.1 8B into Qwen 2.5 7B Base. Training uses 20,000 prompts, half mathematics from the DAPO math set and half code from CodeForces. They evaluate math on MATH500, GSM8K, the three AIME years, AMC 23, and Minerva Math, averaging over 32 samples per problem, and code on HumanEval, MBPP, and LiveCodeBench at 8 samples. The first result is the weight sweep. They add a mean squared error loss on span log-probabilities for the mismatch groups, with a weight lambda from zero to 1.5. Every positive lambda gives complete structural coverage of the response, and every one of the 18 positive settings is worse than lambda zero, by 0.27 to 1.20 percentage points on the full average. In the headline pair, math drops from 39.11 at lambda zero down to about 37.9 at 1.5, and code from 26.60 to about 25.4.

The second result is the top-k support. Restricting the reverse KL at each strict position to the student's top-16 shared-vocabulary entries, instead of the full shared vocabulary, preserves at least 96 percent of the full-average improvement over the undistilled student. In the Granite to Qwen pair the strict top-16 run even edges out the full-strict baseline on some benchmarks, with MATH500 at 86.08 against 85.91, and AIME 2024 at 43.02 against 37.40. Both strict variants beat the cross-tokenizer baselines they compare against, including SimCT and the ULD variants, on all three pairs.

The third diagnostic explains why the span loss hurts. At checkpoints trained with only the strict loss, they compare the gradients from the span objective against the strict gradients, and the directional agreement is weak, sometimes negative. Their control is simple: split the strict positions into two random subsets and measure agreement, and the span-to-strict agreement is consistently below that reference. On top of that, the span gradient norms grow relative to the strict ones as training proceeds. So the extra supervision is not just uninformative, it is a growing, weakly-aligned competing signal, and that is the mechanism behind the accuracy drop.

A skeptical read: this is a clean, well-controlled negative result, and the diagnostics are the real contribution. The gradient agreement test against a position-splitting reference is the kind of control that separates a careful paper from a vibes paper. What I would re-verify before adopting this: the coverage numbers come from student rollouts, which is the right regime for on-policy distillation, but the top-16 subset is student-selected, so part of the gain is that you are training on the positions where the student already has concentrated mass. I would want to see this on longer-horizon rollouts, where mismatch groups are more common and the mass concentration could decay, and I would not generalize the "skip the mismatch groups entirely" conclusion to settings with very aggressive vocabulary mismatch, like byte-level teachers versus word-piece students. The 32-sample averaging is solid, but these are small students, 3B to 8B, and distillation dynamics can change at scale.

Bottom line: if you are distilling across tokenizers, strict-position reverse KL on the shared vocabulary, optionally truncated to the student's top-16 entries, is where the value is, and the mismatch-group machinery costs you accuracy. The paper's broader message is a useful one for the field: alignment coverage is the wrong metric, supervision reliability is the right one.

One practical note for anyone implementing this. The strict-position loss is almost free to set up relative to the baselines. You decode the student response once, tokenize it with both tokenizers, keep the shared boundary offsets, and at each one-to-one interval you renormalize both next-token distributions over the shared vocabulary and take the reverse KL. The shared vocabulary is identified by the underlying text of the tokens, excluding special tokens and ambiguous matches, which is the part worth reading the appendix for, because the edge cases in how you match multi-byte tokens are exactly where silent bugs hide. The top-16 variant adds one forward pass bookkeeping step: you take the student's own probabilities at that position, restrict to the shared entries, and keep the 16 with the highest mass. There is no learned component in any of this, no alignment network, no byte-level interface, which is part of why the result is interesting. The competing methods in this space, rank-based distribution matching, learned vocabulary mappings, byte-prefix marginalization, all add machinery to recover supervision on the positions this paper shows you can mostly ignore.

There is also a training-dynamics detail that is easy to miss. The alignment ratio logged during the strict run stays high throughout training, it does not drift, which means the coverage argument holds over the course of the run and is not a first-epoch artifact. The coverage ranges across the three training windows, steps one to twenty, forty-one to sixty, and eighty-one to one hundred, are within a couple of points of each other, so the concentration of mass on shared vocabulary is stable as the student improves. That stability is what makes the top-16 truncation defensible: the subset you would use at step one is roughly the subset you would use at the end.
