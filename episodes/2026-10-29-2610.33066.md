OvisOCR3, from ATH-MaaS at Alibaba Group, is a technical report for a 0.8 billion parameter model that unifies five OCR-related task families in a single model, document parsing, general OCR, key information extraction, document figure captioning, and cross-page table reconstruction. The claim is that a compact sub-billion model can cover all five of those tasks and still lead the public benchmarks in most of them, and the more interesting question for this audience is the training recipe, because that is the part you can copy. The recipe is a four-stage pipeline that extends the staged paradigm from OvisOCR2, and it is the part worth reading carefully.

The five task families are defined by their prediction targets and output representations. Document parsing is the full-page structure, text plus layout as a structured document representation. General OCR is text recognition over arbitrary regions. Key information extraction is pulling out a set of named fields according to a schema, which is closer to structured generation than to recognition. Document figure captioning is describing figures that carry information the surrounding text does not, which is the vision-language part. And cross-page table reconstruction is recovering a table that spans multiple pages as a single complete structure, which is the hardest of the five because it requires reasoning across page boundaries. These tasks share a foundation in text recognition and structure, but they differ in data volume and response length, and that difference is the whole reason the training pipeline has the shape it does.

Stage one is continued pretraining. The model starts from Qwen 3.5 at 0.8 billion, and the vision encoder, the multimodal connector, and the language model are updated jointly on a broad mixture of visual-text tasks to build a common foundation for all five domains. The joint update matters, because the five tasks sit at different points in the vision-to-language pipeline, table reconstruction needs strong structural vision, figure captioning needs open-ended visual understanding, and key information extraction needs precise region-to-field association, and a stage that co-adapts the encoder and the language model is what lets one 0.8 billion backbone serve all of them. Stage two is domain-balanced mid-training, and this is where the recipe is specific. The five task families have very different data volumes and response lengths, so if you just mix them, the high-volume, short-response tasks dominate the gradient and the long-response, low-volume tasks like table reconstruction get starved. Their fix is to rebalance the task mixture and train two branches, one with token-level loss normalization and one with sample-level loss normalization, and then fuse the two sets of weights at the end of the stage. The token-level branch weights every token equally, which favors the long outputs, and the sample-level branch weights every sample equally, which favors the numerous short ones, and the weight fusion is a way of combining both views without committing to one normalization. That is a concrete, reproducible answer to a problem that comes up in any multi-task training, and it is the kind of detail that usually ends up in an appendix and does not.

Stage three is multi-teacher on-policy distillation, or MOPD. They train five domain-specific teachers, one per task family, each at 4 billion parameters, and then distill all five into the shared 0.8 billion student. The distillation is on-policy, which means the student generates its own response and the teacher provides token-level supervision conditioned on the same input and the student-generated prefix, so the student is being corrected on its own outputs rather than being forced to imitate teacher outputs it would never produce. Each example is supervised only by its own domain teacher, while all five domains update the same shared student. They compare two implementations, a policy-gradient objective that uses the teacher-student log-probability difference on each sampled token as feedback with a clipped objective, and top-K distillation that matches the teacher and student distributions over the top candidate tokens at each prefix. They report that top-K distillation gives better overall performance, particularly on document parsing and general OCR, and their explanation is that the relative preferences over multiple candidates help the capacity-constrained 0.8 billion student absorb the teacher knowledge better than a single-sample policy gradient does, which is a plausible mechanism and a useful result for anyone distilling into a small model. The teachers are only used during training, so at inference time you run a single 0.8 billion model with no task-specific components, which is the deployment property that makes this a real unified model rather than a router.

Stage four is mixed-task reinforcement learning, or MixRL, which jointly refines the student with domain-specific rewards across the task mixture, so each task family has its own reward function and the RL stage is what polishes the structured outputs, the field extraction and the table reconstruction, where the reward signal is cleaner than the supervised one. The point of putting RL after distillation, rather than instead of it, is that the distillation gives the 0.8 billion model a strong prior on the output format of each domain, and the RL then improves on that prior with rewards that are cheap to verify but hard to get by imitating. That is the standard argument for the distill-then-RL order, and it is the order that makes the small model competitive with much larger generalists on the verifiable tasks, because the RL can chase structural correctness directly instead of hoping that imitating the teacher's distributions happens to land on well-formed output.

On the benchmarks, the numbers back up the unification claim. On OmniDocBench version 1.6, the overall parsing score is ninety six point seven four, and it takes the highest overall scores on PureDocBench, Real5-OmniDocBench, and MDPBench. On OCRBench v2 it ranks first, scoring sixty five point nine on the English split and sixty one point three on the Chinese split. It outperforms the baselines on the original OCRBench and on all four key information extraction benchmarks, which is the task family where the schema-driven field extraction is hardest, because a single wrong field name or a missing region breaks the downstream parsing even when the text itself is read correctly. On DocFigCapBench it gets the highest localization F1 and a caption score close to that of Qwen 3.8 Max, which is a much larger general-purpose vision-language model, and that is the comparison that matters for the figure captioning task, because it suggests the 0.8 billion specialist is doing real vision-language work, not just pattern matching. On cross-page tables it leads in relation F1 on CrossTabBench and in truncated-table TEDS on MPDocBench-Parse, which is the task family that was hardest to train and the one where the domain-balanced mid-training was doing the most work. The weights are released on Hugging Face, which makes this a reproducible release.

What I would trust is the recipe, specifically the two-branch loss normalization with weight fusion for multi-task imbalance, and the top-K over policy-gradient finding for on-policy distillation into a small student, because those are the two results that transfer to other multi-task training problems. The ablations also do the work of separating the contributions, and the pattern is consistent, each stage adds on top of the previous one rather than replacing it, which is the signature of a recipe where the stages are doing genuinely different jobs, the pretraining building the shared vision-language base, the mid-training fixing the task imbalance, the distillation importing the teacher knowledge, and the RL polishing the verifiable structure. What I would re-verify is the in-house benchmarks, because several of the comparisons are against internal evaluation sets whose difficulty and contamination profile you cannot check from outside, and the DocFigCapBench caption score being close to Qwen 3.8 Max is the claim I would want to see on a fully public, held-out set before I treated the figure captioning capability as production-grade. The engineering takeaway is that a 0.8 billion unified document model is now a realistic deployment target for a whole document-processing stack, and the four-stage recipe, continued pretraining, balanced mid-training, multi-teacher on-policy distillation, and mixed-task RL, is the template for how to get there.
