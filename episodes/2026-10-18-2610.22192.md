The paper is Terminal-RSI, from Junyao Yang, Zongxia Li, and Yucheng Shi, from Tencent, with Shuicheng Yan as a senior author. The system is a recursive self-improving training loop for terminal agents, and the result it produces is Terminal-RSI-27B, a model built from the Qwen 3.8 27 billion base that reaches about twenty percent on Terminal-Bench 4.0, where the base model scored zero, and eighty-six and a half percent on Terminal-Bench 2.1. The central claim is that if you recursively rewrite your own verified trajectories and then refine the policy against an inherited critic, each round of the loop consolidates what the agent discovered and moves it from partial local fixes toward tasks that pass a strict, protected verifier. That is recursive self-improvement in the narrow, verifiable sense: the model gets better at terminal tasks by training on its own successful runs, repeatedly.

The problem the paper attacks is the credit-assignment problem of terminal agents, and the authors state it well. A terminal task, in the Terminal-Bench sense, is a long interaction in a sandbox. You inspect a repository, repair dependencies, run things, and you need to reach a state that passes a hidden verifier. Two clocks are running at different speeds, and the mismatch is the core difficulty. On the token clock, one shell action can take hundreds of tokens to express, and the observation that comes back can be thousands of tokens that contain no new decision at all. Standard advantage estimation walks the token chain and discounts credit by textual length, so two runs with identical decisions and identical outcomes can get different credit simply because one produced more verbose logs. The first fix is Turn-Boundary Skip-Observation GAE, which applies the discount at turn boundaries, the real decision points, while keeping token-specific baselines within a turn. That removes the verbosity bias from the credit signal, which is a small thing to say about and a big thing to do, because it is the difference between teaching the model what decisions work and teaching it to be terse.

The training loop itself alternates two operations. The first is Recursive Self-Rewrite. The agent discovers trajectories, and a trajectory is retained only if it passes the protected verifier, not an agent self-declaration and not a partial check. The retained trajectories are then rewritten, cleaned and restructured by a planner that sees the public task and the runbook, so the next round trains on cleaner demonstrations of what actually worked. The second operation is Recursive Policy-Refine, which is where the critic comes in. A warm-up critic model is prepared, and each refinement stage inherits the critic from the previous stage, so the value baseline does not have to be rebuilt from scratch every round. The refinement updates the actor on a slower schedule than the critic, with stage-specific learning rates, so the policy moves carefully while the inherited value network recalibrates around it. Two reward designs round out the loop: Family Dense Reward calibrates partial progress across task families so that meaningful subtask completion gets credit even when the full verifier is not passed, and Success-Prioritized Reward separates strict success from bounded partial credit, so the model is never confused about which is which. The five-stage sequence runs from the base model through rewrite one, refine one, rewrite two, refine two.

The results deserve the details, because they are a ladder rather than a single number. On Terminal-Bench 2.1, the base model gets about fifty-two percent, and the loop climbs through the stages to eighty-six and a half percent, which is a very large gain on an already decent baseline. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, the harder current benchmark, the base scores zero, the first rewrite stage gets to about six, the first refine to about seventeen, and the final model lands just under twenty. On Terminal-Bench Hard, the base is at thirty-one and the final model is at seventy-nine, which is the most striking number in the paper, because it is the benchmark where the task is to recover from long dependency chains, and the model went from barely solving anything to solving most of them. On the long-horizon benchmark, strict completion went from zero of forty-six tasks to five of forty-six, and the process reward roughly doubled. These are honest numbers, including the zeros in the base column, and the trajectory of improvement across stages is the real evidence that the loop is doing something cumulative rather than one-shot.

There is a reason the loop is recursive rather than one-shot, and it is the structure of the data. Each round of rewriting only retains trajectories that passed the strict verifier, so the corpus the next round trains on is strictly cleaner than the one before, and the inherited critic is strictly more calibrated, because it has been refined against a harder set of tasks. The two stages interleave for a reason. The rewrite stage changes what the model is shown, the demonstrations, and the refine stage changes how the model is scored, the value baseline, and neither alone is enough. A rewrite without refinement just gives the model cleaner data to fine-tune on, and a refinement without rewrite keeps training against noisy trajectories. The family dense reward is doing something subtle and important, which is keeping the partial progress signal meaningful across task families that have very different scales of progress, so that a task where you can make five percent of the subtasks counts the same in training as one where you can make ninety. Without that calibration, the reward signal would be dominated by the easiest families.

What I would trust is the decomposition of the credit problem, and specifically the turn-boundary advantage estimation, because it identifies a real, measurable flaw in how long-horizon agent training assigns credit, and the fix is clean. The critic inheritance is also a sound design choice. Rebuilding the value network from zero at every round of a self-improvement loop would be slow and would throw away the most valuable asset the previous round produced, and the warm-up plus handoff pattern is the right way to keep the baseline stable while the policy moves. The strict verifier requirement, retaining only trajectories that fully pass, is the guardrail that keeps recursive self-improvement from optimizing against its own noisy self-reports, and that matters more than it looks, because it is the difference between compounding real gains and compounding self-deception.

What I would re-verify is the magnitude on the hardest benchmarks and the reproducibility of the loop. A base model at zero on Terminal-Bench 4.0 means the absolute numbers there, even at twenty percent, are on a benchmark where the task bar is extremely high, and I would want the per-task breakdown to see whether the gains are broad or concentrated in a few task families. The recursive loop is also the kind of procedure where small implementation choices, the rewriting prompt, the critic warm-up length, the stage learning rates, can account for a lot of the result, and I would want a second run of the loop with a different random seed or a different rewrite model to see how much of the ladder is robust versus seed-specific. The long-horizon numbers, five of forty-six strict completions, are encouraging but small in count, and the doubling of process reward is a denser, more forgiving signal than strict completion. The whole result sits on the Qwen 3.8 27 billion base, and the interesting open question, which the paper does not answer, is whether the loop scales up to a larger base, because that is where recursive self-improvement either becomes interesting or stays a niche training trick.

Two-sentence verdict: Terminal-RSI is a well-engineered recursive self-improvement loop for terminal agents, and its real contributions are the turn-boundary advantage estimation that removes verbosity bias from credit assignment and the critic-inheriting refinement that makes each round cumulative, producing a large, verifiable gain from a base that scored zero on the current Terminal-Bench. The numbers are honest and the ladder across stages is convincing, but the small absolute counts on the hardest benchmarks and the seed-sensitivity of recursive loops are the things I would want replicated before treating twenty percent on Terminal-Bench 4.0 as more than a strong first round.
