Harness squared: Recursive Agent Harnessing for an Open World, by Ruiyao Xu, Yanfei Chen, and Zhongying CuiZhu, from Northwestern University and Google Cloud AI Research. The unit of improvement here is not the model but the harness: the prompts, memory, skills, tools, and orchestration that surround a frozen model and make it an agent. The paper's claim is that recursive self-improvement should operate at two coupled levels at once. Task-level recursion refines the execution harness for the current task, and harnessing-level recursion extracts lessons from that refinement process to update a continual-improvement harness that guides how future tasks are refined. The hard problem it addresses is that open-world tasks arrive with no ground-truth answers and no reliable verifier, so the system cannot simply score candidate harnesses and keep the best; it has to judge the effect of an edit from behavioral contrasts under matched conditions.

The harness is decomposed into eight file-level components: a system prompt, guardrails, long-term memory, skills, tool guidance, scripts, subagent definitions, and a middleware plugin that hooks every tool call outside the model's discretion. Each is a plain file in the runtime, which is what makes the whole scheme plug-and-play and inspectable: every improvement is a diff to text files, not a weight update. The base model's parameters never move. Evaluation runs over a stream of open-world tasks, each starting from a common base harness, with a bounded recursion budget before the agent must return its deliverable.

The contrastive improvement step is the core contribution, and it has three phases. Propose generates candidate harness edits, informed by the current harness, the task's execution history, and the improvement harness's library of past adopted harnesses and lessons. Probe executes the current harness and the candidates under matched conditions and records their trajectories and deliverables. Compose decides which changes to keep by comparing behavior, not by checking against a label: an edit is retained when the contrast between candidate and reference execution shows it helped, and discarded when it did not or when it changed behavior in uninterpretable ways. Two recursion modes build on this step. Parallel recursion proposes and probes multiple candidates simultaneously and composes from all the execution evidence at once, and sequential recursion applies successive revisions, each starting from the previous compose's output. Both are budgeted, and the budget is the main experimental dial.

The harnessing-level recursion is the second loop, and it is where the open-world claim lives. After a task is done, the improvement harness does not simply inherit the execution harness that won; it selectively retains reusable components and, crucially, the lessons about when and why particular edits worked. These are stored in an improvement library that future Propose and Compose steps read from. The point of this design is that task-specific harnesses are brittle: a harness tuned for one task can actively hurt the next when the requirements shift, so instead of carrying the harness forward, the system carries forward the meta-lessons. That is the difference between improving the harness and learning how to improve harnesses.

Results span three professional benchmarks, WorkBuddy-Bench, JobBench, and Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark, across four model-and-harness configurations, including GLM-5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash running under both OpenCode and Codex. A single contrastive step, no additional budget, improves every configuration over the base harness, by up to 11.5 points on WorkBuddy-Bench, 4.5 on JobBench, and 11.8 percentage points on the legal benchmark's partial pass rate. Parallel recursion adds another 3.9, 4.2, and 6.7 respectively. The budget study is the cleanest result: with Gemini 3.5 Flash under OpenCode, raising the parallel budget from 1 to 5 lifts gains over the base from 4.5 to 7.5 points on JobBench and from 5.1 to 8.4 on WorkBuddy-Bench, while a competing method, Meta-Harness, uses more compute than the budget-5 setting and gets lower performance. The ablation isolates the three design pieces: removing the improvement library costs 3.1 points on WorkBuddy-Bench and 1.8 on JobBench, removing the scoped editor roles costs 2.9 and 3.7, and skipping composition costs 1.6 and 1.9. All three contribute. One more finding is that a library accumulated on JobBench transfers and improves performance on WorkBuddy-Bench, which is the direct test of whether the meta-lessons generalize across task domains.


The case study in the paper is worth a paragraph because it shows what the editor actually finds. On a JobBench task scored 43.1 by the base harness, the trajectory showed procedural failures rather than knowledge gaps: data was never reconciled across source files, and outputs were never reopened for verification after being written. The refined harness adds two targeted interventions. One is an invariant intervention, a process-level guardrail that forces the model to inventory all inputs and trace figures back to their sources, and the other is a verification step that reopens written outputs before submission. The scoped editor roles are the mechanism: an invariant editor that reads the trajectory at a stated scope and proposes guardrails, and a domain editor that proposes task-specific changes, with composition deciding which proposals to combine. The middleware plugin component is the part that makes guardrails enforceable rather than advisory, because it hooks every tool call outside the model's discretion, so a process invariant is checked at the runtime layer rather than hoped for in the prompt. That distinction, enforcement in middleware versus instruction in the system prompt, is probably the single most reusable design choice in the paper for anyone building agent scaffolding.


One experimental detail deserves emphasis because it changes how you should read the cross-model results. The same model is sometimes both executor and editor, and the paper tests this directly: running a single model for both roles at a modest budget still improves on every benchmark, with gains of 3.3, 5.5, and 3.0 points on the three benchmarks for the strongest model tested, and 13.7 points on the legal benchmark for the weakest. The implication is that harness refinement does not require a stronger editor than the executor, which is what makes the approach deployable at all for small or open-weight models, and it is also the setting in which the contrastive composition step is doing its most load-bearing work, because there is no external quality gap between the model proposing the edit and the model executing it.

The skeptical read has two real teeth. First, this is a self-improvement system with no ground-truth verifier, and the entire safety of the loop rests on the Compose step correctly reading behavioral contrasts. The paper shows it works on three professional benchmarks, but a contrastive judge that cannot see the answer is exactly the component that can drift, and the paper does not report a rate at which composition makes a wrong keep. If you are going to run this on high-stakes tasks, that error rate is the number you need before you trust it. Second, the gains, while consistent and sometimes large, are measured against a base harness that is deliberately plain, and the competing methods, Meta-Harness and ReasoningBank, are the honest comparison, on which Harness2 at a modest budget wins. The compute accounting, which includes improvement cost and lands on the Pareto frontier, is the part I would trust most. What I would steal from this paper is the decomposition itself: eight file-level harness components, a contrastive propose-probe-compose step that needs no labels, and an improvement library that stores meta-lessons rather than the winning harness.

The bottom line: Harness2 is a concrete, plug-and-play recipe for recursive harness self-improvement in the label-free open-world setting, and its consistent single-step gains across four model-and-harness configurations are the result I would trust most. I would re-verify the composition step's error behavior on my own tasks before letting it run unsupervised, because an unlabeled contrastive judge is where this class of system can quietly drift.
