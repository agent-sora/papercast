The paper is Waypoint-OPD: Transferring RL-Induced Policy Shifts via Gated On-Policy Distillation, from Bingxi Hou, Guochao Jiang, and Guofeng Quan, with all seven authors at Alibaba Cloud Computing. It is a distillation method for taking the improvement a small teacher model gains from reinforcement learning and moving that improvement into larger student models, without retraining the teacher and without any verifier in the loop. The central claim is that the single number people currently transfer, the gap between the teacher's behavior before and after RL, is a lossy summary, and that a middle checkpoint from the same training run carries information about whether that change was coherent.

The problem is a real one if you have ever tried to reuse RL work across model sizes. You run RL on a four-billion-parameter model because it is cheap, and you get a genuinely better teacher on math and code. You would now like that improvement to live in your eight-billion, fourteen-billion, and thirty-billion models, so you do not have to pay for RL on each of them separately. The standard tool is on-policy distillation: the student generates its own trajectories, and you train it to match the teacher on those prefixes. A sharper version, called Direct-OPD, does not match the teacher's absolute output. Instead it takes the log-probability difference between the teacher's pre-RL checkpoint and its post-RL checkpoint, token by token, and uses that difference as a dense implicit reward on the student's own states. The idea is that the difference points in the direction RL pushed the teacher, so you transfer the change rather than the endpoint.

The gap the authors identify is that the pre-to-post difference is an endpoint. It telescopes. The same endpoint gap can come from two different histories: one where the teacher moved consistently in one direction the whole way through RL, and one where it moved one way early and partially reversed late, so the two halves cancel into the same final number. Endpoint-only transfer cannot tell those cases apart, and it will happily transfer a net shift that was actually two opposing motions. The authors hypothesize that if the early half and the late half of the teacher's change point in the same direction, that shift is more trustworthy, and you should transfer it more aggressively. If they point in opposite directions, you should suppress it.

Their method adds one thing to Direct-OPD: a middle checkpoint, which they call the Waypoint. All three checkpoints, the Initial before RL, the Waypoint at an intermediate RL step, and the Final after RL, come from the same teacher run and are frozen during transfer. At each prefix the student visits, they take the student's top-k most probable next tokens, and score those candidates with all three frozen teachers. That gives two consecutive shifts: the change from Initial to Waypoint, and the change from Waypoint to Final, which add up to the full endpoint shift. They center both shifts so that a uniform shift across all candidates, which says nothing about relative preferences, is removed. Then they compute the cosine agreement between the two centered segment shifts, weighted by the student's own probabilities on those candidates. That agreement, clipped at zero, is the gate: one when the two halves agree in direction, zero when they oppose, and scaled in between. The endpoint shift is multiplied by that gate before it becomes the student's dense reward.

There is a normalization step that matters in practice. Because the gate can shrink the reward, the average reward scale drifts batch to batch, which destabilizes the PPO-style update. So they rescale the whole batch's gated rewards by a clipped factor that targets a fixed mean gate, which preserves the relative weights and keeps zero gates zero. The final objective is a dense, on-policy surrogate: the gated, candidate-level reward drives the student update, a KL penalty anchors the student to its own initialization, and the rollout and candidate supports are held fixed while differentiating. The implementation uses dual-clipped PPO and scores the student's top sixteen token IDs with the three teachers. Relative to Direct-OPD, the whole method costs one extra frozen-teacher scoring pass. No new parameters, no new student rollouts, no verifier.

The experiments use Qwen three, four-billion teachers trained with non-thinking RL, with the Waypoint at RL step six hundred and the Final at step twelve hundred, for both math and code. Students are the Qwen-three family at eight billion, fourteen billion, and thirty billion A3B parameters, spanning dense and mixture-of-experts architectures within one family. Math transfer trains on DAPO math, seventeen thousand prompts, and evaluates with mean at thirty-two samples across seven benchmarks including three years of AIME and several HMMT editions. Code transfer uses a Codeforces-derived prompt set and evaluates on the three hundred and seventy-eight problem MBPP plus set and LiveCode Bench versions four, five, and six, at mean at eight.

The headline numbers: on math, Waypoint-OPD beats Direct-OPD, the strongest baseline across all three students, by two point one eight, one point five nine, and one point eight nine percentage points for the eight, fourteen, and thirty-billion students respectively. It improves nineteen of the twenty-one benchmark comparisons, with the two exceptions being small drops on the hardest HMMT editions. The fourteen-billion and thirty-billion students also surpass the four-billion teacher's own fifty-six point one three average, by three point zero seven and one point seven two points. On code, the gains over Direct-OPD are two point six six, one point five two, and one point one nine, with gains in all fifteen code comparisons.

The ablations on the eight-billion student are the part I would read carefully. Removing the gate entirely, setting it to one, drops math performance. Shuffling the gates among prefixes within a batch, so each prefix gets someone else's gate value, also drops it, which is the cleanest evidence that the gate value is doing something specific rather than just adding a regularizer. Fixing the batch normalization to one, removing the rescale, also hurts. Varying the Waypoint over RL steps five hundred, six hundred, and seven hundred shows the result is not a knife-edge on the exact checkpoint.

One implementation detail deserves emphasis if you plan to reuse this. The whole method is built on three frozen checkpoints that share a tokenizer with the student, and that constraint is what makes the token-level log-probability differences comparable at all. In practice the authors run the transfer on top of the verl framework and score only the student's top sixteen candidate token IDs against all three teachers, which is what keeps the extra scoring pass cheap. The gate is computed on that small candidate support, weighted by the student's own normalized probabilities, so the comparison is localized to the actions the student actually considers, rather than the entire vocabulary. That localization is doing real work: it means the agreement test is about whether the teacher's early and late preferences shift in the same direction over the tokens that matter to this particular student, which is a more useful question than a global cosine over everything.

The learning-curve comparison is also worth noting. When they match evaluation steps across a hundred updates on the eight-billion math student, the gap over Direct-OPD is present but it narrows relative to the selected-checkpoint table, which suggests part of the reported margin comes from checkpoint selection on AIME 2024 as a validation set. That is a standard technique and it is disclosed, but it is another reason the realized gain is smaller than the headline numbers.

What I would trust here is the setup discipline. All methods share prompts, decoding, lengths, and sample counts. The teacher is smaller than the students, which removes the obvious confound that a big teacher is just a better model being imitated. The shuffle control is exactly the right experiment to rule out "this is just more regularization." What I would re-verify before adopting: the margins are modest, a couple of percentage points on an already strong baseline, and they are concentrated in one model family, Qwen three, with one teacher size. The method needs a genuine middle checkpoint from the teacher's RL run, which means you have to have logged intermediate checkpoints, and it assumes the teacher and student share a tokenizer so the token-level probabilities are directly comparable. The theory section proves the endpoint is independent of the Waypoint at fixed prefixes, which is a nice invariance but it does not prove the gate is beneficial, only that it is well-defined. The gate is a heuristic weight on an already-good signal, and the paper is honest that directional agreement alone does not guarantee useful supervision.

Two-sentence verdict: This is a clean, cheap, well-controlled refinement of RL-distillation that earns its small but consistent wins with a single extra scoring pass and a defensible gate. If you already maintain intermediate checkpoints from a small RL teacher and you distill into a same-family larger model, it is low-risk to try; the payoff is modest and the gains are demonstrated in one family, so do not expect it to rescue a weak distillation pipeline on its own.
