Rufus-Air: An Open LLM Post-Training Recipe. The first three authors are Chia-Yuan Chang, Renyuan Cheng, and Rui Feng, and the full author list is all from Amazon. The paper is deliberately listed alphabetically, because the contribution is a shared recipe rather than any one person's result.

Here is the pitch in one sentence, and it is the reason this earns a slot: post-training sections in model cards usually read like a system card, not a recipe, and this one is trying to close that gap. The team took GLM-4.5-Air-Base, a public mixture-of-experts checkpoint with 106 billion total parameters and 12 billion active, and ran it through a single serial pipeline of eight stages, documenting the data, the reward design, the stage order, the infrastructure, and the per-stage results well enough that a team outside a frontier lab could attempt the whole thing. The compute claim is specific: the RL stages ran on 8 to 32 nodes, which is small by the standards of the models being compared against.

The pipeline order is the thing I would actually want to steal, so let me lay it out. It goes: supervised fine-tuning, then reasoning RL, then coding RL, then instruction-following RL, then three agentic stages in sequence, general agent, coding agent, and search agent, and finally RLHF. Each stage trains on the checkpoint the previous one produced, so nothing is a from-scratch rerun. The order is set by two axes at once. On capability, it goes basic to advanced, so each stage builds on what the one before it seeded. On reward type, it goes from hard, verifiable rewards toward softer judge-based or environment-mediated signals. The reasoning and coding stages, whose rewards are the least gameable, run first; the harder-to-verify stages run later. The authors are explicit about what this buys and what it does not. It limits how long any given gameable reward is under optimization pressure, because the model spends its early, most-plastic RL budget on signals it cannot easily fool. But they are honest that this order does not protect earlier gains, because every later stage updates the same parameters. So the stage order is a story about exposure to reward hacking, not a guarantee of monotonic improvement.
The SFT stage is the load-bearing wall. They use 9 million samples and 27 billion tokens, and they are blunt about the conclusion: a strong SFT checkpoint establishes a capability floor that the RL stages refine rather than build from scratch. SFT is a capability-building stage, not a warm-up. The infrastructure note that I would flag for anyone reproducing this: SFT runs on Slime with the Megatron backend across 64 eight-H200 nodes, 512 GPUs total, with a batch size of 4096 sequences. That is the part of the recipe that is least reproducible for most teams, and it is the part that the rest of the pipeline quietly depends on.

The RL stages use a difficulty filter that the authors call an automatic curriculum, and I think it is the most transferable idea in the paper. They drop prompts the policy already solves, and in most stages they also drop prompts it never solves. That leaves a productive band of examples that are genuinely learnable, and the filter advances that frontier on its own without manual staging. The reasoning RL stage, measured against its own SFT checkpoint, raises GPQA by 5.3 points, from 68.2 to 73.5, and leaves both years of AIME 2.6 to 2.8 points lower. The framing matters: they tuned the stage against GPQA and treated AIME as a guardrail. The SFT base already sits at 90 to 91 pass-at-one on AIME, and with 30 problems per year a change under three points is less than one problem. They even note that same-condition reruns of AIME 25 on the SFT checkpoint differ by 4.6 points at a sample size of 8, so the AIME dip is inside the noise they measured. The takeaway they carry forward is that on a strong SFT base, verifier-based reasoning RL is reliable and largely curation-bound, meaning the gains track the quality of the learnability and correctness filtering more than the details of the policy-gradient objective. That is a genuinely useful engineering prior.
The three agentic stages are worth a closer look, because they are the part of the pipeline most likely to be the differentiator for a team that does not care about raw benchmark leaderboards. General agent adds tool use and planning in its own environment, measured on Tau2 retail, airline, and telecom, where Rufus-Air reaches 85.3, 84.0, and 93.0 respectively, with telecom being the standout at 93.0 against the baseline 32.7. That gap is the largest single-stage improvement in the table and the one I would be most suspicious of, because telecom is a domain where the agent's tool-calling and multi-turn state management dominate the score. Coding agent is measured on software engineering, and search agent on open-web search, where the BrowseComp and Seal-0 numbers I cited earlier come from. The final RLHF stage then shapes open-ended quality where no hard verifier exists, and the authors are explicit that this is where reward-hacking risk is real, which is precisely why it runs last, after the model has already spent its budget on the signals it cannot easily fool.

Now the numbers that matter. Against the official GLM-4.5-Air release, which is the vendor's own post-trained model and not one of theirs, Rufus-Air leads on every reported benchmark except Arena-Hard v2 creative writing. The headline wins are large in a few places. On instruction following, IFBench prompt-strict goes from 33.6 to 76.9, and IFEval prompt-strict from 83.0 to 95.4, which is a jump so large that I would want to look at exactly what the instruction-following RL stage did to get it. On the search agent side, BrowseComp moves from 22.7 to 37.1 and Seal-0 from 33.3 to 51.4. On coding, SWE-bench Verified goes from 50.6 to 65.6, and Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 24.7 to 42.7. On reasoning, AIME 25 lands at 88.3 against the baseline 84.2, and GPQA at 75.6 against 73.9. It is competitive with other similarly sized open models such as INTELLECT-3 and Nemotron-3, though it is not the leader on every row. GPT-OSS and Qwen3.5 sit above it on some reasoning benchmarks, which is the honest picture: this is a competitive open recipe, not a frontier-beater.

The infrastructure section is where I would spend my time, because the authors argue, correctly, that engineering choices are part of the recipe and not an implementation detail. They name specific ones: token-in, token-out rollouts for on-policy multi-turn RL, consistent chat-template handling carried from SFT through the agentic stages, a sandbox service reliable enough for long agentic runs, and large batches with Rollout Routing Replay for stable RL. These are the details that a system card would never disclose and that a team trying to reproduce the pipeline would hit first. The agentic stages each have their own environment, and the search agent in particular depends on a live web loop, which is where the sandbox reliability matters most.

So what would I trust, and what would I re-verify before adopting this? I would trust the structure. The four stated principles, SFT as a capability stage, difficulty filtering as an automatic curriculum, reward reliability setting the stage order, and infrastructure as part of the recipe, are well-motivated and each is tied to a concrete measurement rather than an assertion. The stage-order argument about reward-hacking exposure is the kind of thing you cannot get from reading a paper abstract, and it is exactly the kind of prior a post-training team needs. What I would re-verify: the instruction-following gains are large enough that I would want the per-stage delta table, not just the final comparison, to confirm they came from the IF RL stage and not from drift in the agentic stages that follow it. And the AIME guardrail framing is defensible but it means the recipe is explicitly allowed to trade a little math for a lot of instruction following and agentic capability, so if your workload is math-heavy you would want to run that trade yourself. The compute for SFT is the real barrier to reproduction, and the rest of the pipeline is the part most teams can actually run.

Bottom line: this is one of the more genuinely reproducible post-training writeups I have seen, and its four ordering principles are worth stealing even if you never run the full pipeline. Treat the final benchmark table as a competitive-with-open-models result, not a frontier result, and budget your reproduction around the SFT stage, which is where the real cost lives.
