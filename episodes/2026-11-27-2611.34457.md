ProxyGenBench: Benchmarking Proxy-Guided Video Generation, from Zhenchen Tang, Haolin Lu, and Songlin Yang, at the New Laboratory of Pattern Recognition in the Institute of Automation of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, the University of California at San Diego, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, and Tencent. The claim: giving a video model a coarse structural proxy, like an animated 3D blockout, improves control over how a scene unfolds, but the same proxy introduces artifacts the model must not copy, and existing benchmarks do not measure whether a model can preserve the intended structure, complete the missing content, and bind the other conditions all at once.

The problem is a control problem. Video generators have gotten very good at producing realistic clips, but controlling how a scene unfolds remains hard. Generated videos can contain wrong interactions, inconsistent spatial relationships, or discontinuities across shots, even when every individual frame looks plausible. A text prompt and reference images give you semantic and appearance guidance, but they leave object trajectories, camera behavior, and event timing implicit. A structural proxy fills that gap: it is a coarse video, usually rendered from a 3D representation, that specifies scene layout, object placement, motion, camera behavior, and event order without defining the final result. The catch is that these proxies are coarse by design. They leave details unspecified, and they often contain low-fidelity geometry and motion, placeholder shapes, incomplete interactions, that should not be reproduced in the output. So successful proxy-guided generation has to do three distinct things at once. Preserve the intended structure. Complete the missing content and correct the proxy artifacts. And bind the text, image, and audio conditions to the right entities and events. The central question the authors ask is how a generator can preserve the useful constraints in a proxy without inheriting its artifacts, and without ignoring the other conditions the user gave it.

The benchmark is built to probe exactly those three abilities. It has 312 evaluation cases from 60 scenes across three scene tasks: dynamic action, which tests motion, interaction, and state transitions; multi-shot storytelling, which tests event order and continuity across cuts; and multi-view consistency, which tests stable scene and object states across camera viewpoints. The proxies come from four source groups with different construction workflows. Enhanced reference-to-video adds proxy guidance to existing image- or audio-conditioned tasks. Native code-to-proxy uses an LLM-based agent to animate 3D scenes from scripts or reference clips. Game proxy uses game footage and 3D game assets. And studio 3D proxy uses unfinished production assets. Each source has a different flavor of artifact, which is what lets them study completion across genuinely different failure modes. The four source groups also map to four intended applications, and that mapping is part of the benchmark's design: enhanced reference-to-video targets workflows that add proxy guidance to an existing image- or audio-conditioned task, native code-to-proxy targets an LLM agent animating 3D scenes from a script, game proxy targets game-like world modeling, and studio 3D proxy targets 3D animation completion. Covering all four means the preservation-completion trade-off is being measured across the situations where proxy-guided generation is actually going to be used, not just in a single toy setting.

They also vary the inputs systematically. There are three proxy-fidelity levels, P-r-zero is a blockout, P-r-one is a figure, and P-r-two is detail, with increasing geometric and motion detail. There are three text-detail levels, T-zero through T-two. And there are optional image and audio references. The key design point is that they vary text detail at a fixed proxy level and proxy fidelity at a fixed text level, so the two effects are studied separately rather than testing every combination. Out of the 312 cases, 294 are proxy-conditioned and 18 are no-proxy counterparts, plus a set of no-image, no-audio, and targeted ablation cases.

Evaluation is where this differs from a typical video benchmark. They do not just compare the output to the proxy by similarity, because matching the proxy is not the goal, and a high proxy-resemblance score would actually reward a model that copies the low-poly donuts rather than refining them. Instead they build verified, case-specific checklists. Thirty-two fine-grained criteria are organized into eight capabilities under the three dimensions, Preserve, Complete, and Bind, and the applicable criteria are instantiated as checklist questions that are grounded in the inputs the model actually received and fixed across models for the same case. Vision-language-model judgments use visual or audio evidence to assess structural adherence, completion, and binding beyond mere proxy resemblance. They report the three dimension scores plus a structure-sensitive strict score that gives extra emphasis to the critical Preserve constraints, because structural control is the basic requirement of proxy-guided generation. The whole checklist approach matters because a single scalar like CLIP similarity cannot tell you whether the model preserved the seating arrangement while also fixing a low-poly prop.

The results across five video generators are the part I would read first. Proxies improve spatiotemporal control, particularly action transitions and cross-shot spatial consistency, which is the expected win. But there is a tension. In the scene-matched comparisons, moving from a coarse proxy to a detailed one increases the Preserve score but lowers the Complete score for all five models. More proxy detail helps the model follow the structure but gives it more artifacts to inherit, so it completes less cleanly. And on the studio 3D proxies, strong structural adherence can coexist with incorrect condition binding, meaning a model can nail the layout while grounding the wrong voice to the wrong character or dropping the clothing reference entirely. The representative scores put the top models, Seedance 2.5, Kling 4.0, and Wan 3.0, in a narrow band, with the strict structure-aware score sitting in the mid-forties even for the best.

Where I would push back. First, the judges are vision-language models scoring against checklists, and the paper reports that the rubric judgments agree closely with human annotations, but the agreement statistic and the number of human-verified items are what I would want to see before trusting the absolute scores. Checklist-based VLM evaluation is defensible, but it inherits the judge model's blind spots, and for subtle binding errors a VLM can miss exactly the thing the checklist is trying to catch. Second, five generators is a small model panel, and these are all strong closed or commercial systems, so the benchmark is a snapshot of the current frontier rather than a tool that will keep meaning the same thing as new models ship. Third, the 312 cases from 60 scenes is a modest corpus; the per-scene-task breakdowns will have limited statistical power, and the strict score's weighting of Preserve over Complete and Bind is a modeling choice that flatters structure-following at the expense of completion.

What I would trust: the framing and the checklist methodology, because separating preserve, complete, and bind, and deriving the checklists only from what the model actually received, is the right way to evaluate a task where matching the input is not the goal. The preservation-completion tension is a genuinely useful negative finding, and it is the kind of thing a similarity-only benchmark would never surface. What I would re-verify: the human agreement numbers, the judge model used for the VLM scoring, and whether the strict-score weighting changes any model's rank.

The bottom line: ProxyGenBench is a well-constructed benchmark for a control problem that current video models clearly do not solve, and its preserve-complete-bind decomposition is the most reusable piece. It documents a real tension, namely that more proxy detail helps structure but hurts completion, and it shows that strong structural adherence does not guarantee correct condition binding. It is not a tool for ranking which video model is best overall, but it is a solid instrument for the specific question of whether a generator can use a coarse structural guide without copying its flaws.
