Recursive self-improvement of AI research agents. The first three authors are Dhruv Srikanth, Bingchen Zhao, and Dixing Xu, from Weco AI, with co-authors Yuxiang Wu and Zhengyao Jiang at the same company.

The premise is a pointed one. AI research agents are already automating pieces of AI R&D: machine learning engineering, GPU kernel optimization, algorithm search, harness design. But notice where the improvement stops: the agents make the artifacts better, while the research process that produces them stays fixed. In conventional R&D, the long-standing empirical trend is that cumulative spending on research yields diminishing returns, so if the process itself never improves, the cost of the next increment keeps climbing. Recursive self-improvement points the process at itself: the agent's own code is the object of optimization, and each accepted rewrite becomes the agent that the next round edits. The paper presents AIDE squared, a two-loop system that runs this at the harness layer, the code around the model that controls an agent's search policy, context management, and verification. The inner loop is a research agent that optimizes code against a measurable metric on a suite of AI R&D tasks under a fixed dollar budget. The outer loop proposes rewrites of the inner agent's code and grades each candidate by running it on a selection benchmark, scoring the resulting solutions on private held-out data that the candidate never sees, and keeping a rewrite only if its aggregate private grade beats the incumbent. The design contains two deliberate anti-gaming measures. First, the inner agent optimizes against public task scores, while the outer loop selects on private held-out grades, so the inner agent cannot directly optimize the selection criterion. Second, every agent is evaluated at the same per-task budget, so a gain must come from a better algorithm, not from spending more compute. The outer-loop agent in the main run is AIDE-human, a production research agent the company developed over two years of human R&D, running on Claude Opus 4.7, while every inner-loop agent is evaluated with Gemini 3 Flash.

The main experiment is one autonomous eight-day run producing a 100-node trajectory, the initial agent plus 99 rewrite proposals. Seven rewrites were accepted, at steps 2, 6, 28, 39, 47, 63, and 85, lifting the incumbent's private grade from 0.703 to 0.778, above AIDE-human's 0.749 under the same grading. The accepted changes read like a changelog for agent engineering: a bandit search policy over strategies at step 2, a requirement to produce a root-cause diagnosis before any fix at step 6, forking the champion solution to escape plateaus at step 28, self-repair after a crashed robustness fix at step 39, a patch for an error that crashed evaluations at step 47, compressing log spam before reading it at step 63, and demoting a failure-prone draft strategy at step 85. Two full repeat runs of the same protocol also produced sustained improvements, accepting two and four rewrites respectively, which is the paper's evidence that the trend is not a one-off lucky rewrite. The generalization story is the part worth your attention. They test four external benchmarks that never influenced selection. On ALE-Bench, long-horizon combinatorial optimization from AtCoder contests, the evolved checkpoints score 1713 and 1790 against the starting agent's 1536 and AIDE-human's 1511. On MLE-Bench, autonomous Kaggle-style ML engineering, they reach 0.730 and 0.722 percentile against 0.678 and the human baseline's 0.708. On FML-Bench, 18 realistic ML research codebases, the final agent's 19.9 percent mean normalized test improvement slightly exceeds the human baseline's 19.6. The striking one is WeatherBench 2, a physics-based weather-forecasting optimization task that is out of distribution from everything in the selection set: the forecast-skill gain jumps from 0.262 for the starting agent to 0.798 and 0.793 for the evolved checkpoints, versus 0.404 for AIDE-human, and across every seed the evolved agents independently converged on the same family of changes to the forecasting model's numerics, with almost no run-to-run variation, while the human-engineered agent reaches a comparable solution on at most one seed. On a separate held-out task family measuring reward hacking on 38 GPU kernel pairs, the hacking rate falls from 55 percent for the starting agent to 32 percent for the final agent, seven points below the human baseline's 39 percent, even though nothing in the loop optimized for this.

The skeptical read. The experimental design is stronger than most work in this space, and the two anti-gaming measures, public-versus-private signal separation and fixed per-task budgets, are the right ones, and the fact that reward hacking fell without being optimized for is exactly the kind of emergent property a well-designed loop should show. But the numbers have to be read with care. The main run is eight days and one seed, with two repeat runs that accepted fewer rewrites, so the seven-accepted-rewrite trajectory is the best of three, and the paper is transparent about compounding noise across the two loops making it hard to decisively separate a discovered agent from the strong baseline when that discovered agent is itself used as the outer-loop agent. The margins on the in-distribution held-out benchmarks are small relative to the reported standard errors: ALE-Bench's 1790 versus 1511 has error bars of 9 and 35, MLE-Bench's 0.722 versus 0.708 has error bars around 0.011 and 0.007, and FML-Bench's 19.9 versus 19.6 is explicitly within one standard error. The WeatherBench result, the most dramatic number in the paper, is one task with three seeds, and the 0.404 for the human baseline carries a standard error of 0.193, so that comparison is not statistically tight. The reward-hacking measurement uses KernelBench, whose isolated-timing protocol is itself a known target for gaming, so the 55 to 32 percent decline is reassuring but measured on a task family that is, by construction, sensitive to exactly the behaviors the loop was not optimizing. The costs are real: each candidate grading runs the agent on the full selection benchmark with per-task budgets of 5 to 20 dollars across 10 to 22 tasks, and 99 candidate proposals means thousands of agent runs, which is an 8-day, compute-intensive experiment rather than a cheap iteration loop. What I would re-verify before citing this as evidence that recursive self-improvement scales: a larger number of seeds at the outer loop, a pre-registered held-out set chosen before the run, and a comparison where the baseline is allowed the same total compute budget rather than the same per-task budget.

What this paper does establish, within its stated scope, is that a fixed-budget, private-grade selection loop can produce a sustained, multi-step trend of harness improvements in a frontier research agent over eight days, and that the resulting agents match or exceed a strong human-engineered production baseline on external benchmarks, including one out-of-distribution scientific-computing domain where the gains are large and reproducible across seeds. The honest framing is that this is a proof of mechanism, not a measured inflection point: the improvements are real but modest in aggregate, the statistical margins on the in-distribution benchmarks are thin, and the system still requires a human-built production agent to drive the outer loop and a human-chosen selection benchmark to define what good means. Bottom line: AIDE squared is the most rigorous demonstration to date that recursive self-improvement at the harness layer produces improvements that transfer beyond the selection tasks, with an emergent reduction in reward hacking as a bonus, but the effect sizes on the in-distribution benchmarks are within noise of the strong baseline, so the claim that should carry weight is the transfer to the out-of-distribution weather task, where the signal is clean and consistent across seeds.

The appendix of rejected proposals is worth a moment, because it shows the loop exploring the right space and failing where it should. Across three runs, the outer agent proposed island populations with periodic migration, pairwise LLM-judge tournaments for selection, stagnation-triggered exploration escalation, restart policies, majority-vote ensembles, decaying exploration schedules, and variance-adaptive exploration boosts, and none of them survived grading. Ensembles in particular were explored and consistently rejected, with the agent's own analyses noting that ensembling LLM calls consumes budget that would otherwise fund additional search steps, which is a genuinely interesting piece of self-knowledge: the discovered agent understood that at fixed budget, more samples of the same policy were worth less than more search. Roughly a quarter of the rejected rewrites scored higher on the public signal the inner agent sees, and were rejected on the private grade, which is the anti-gaming design working exactly as intended, filtering out candidates that optimize the visible metric without private validation.
