What a Forecasting Leaderboard Can Establish: Resolution Reporting for Zero-Shot Time-Series Models, from Arya Sikder and Sriram Pillutla, at CastStar. The claim: every measurement has a resolution, the smallest difference it can separate from noise, and no public forecasting leaderboard states the resolution of the datasets it uses, so the authors built a tool, Vernier, that computes that number from the files a board already publishes, and they use it to show that the boards people cite as evidence of state-of-the-art accuracy cannot actually resolve the gaps they are displaying.

This is a methods paper, and a short one, but it is aimed at something real. A foundation model for time series is pretrained once on a large heterogeneous corpus and then asked to forecast series it has never seen. The whole point of that design is transfer to datasets nobody chose in advance, so the only real evidence is the model's score on held-out data. But independent public forecasting datasets are scarce, so comparisons run through a few shared leaderboards, and the one with the most entries is GIFT-Eval. GIFT-Eval runs every submission on the same 97 configurations, each one a dataset at one sampling frequency and forecast horizon, displays a strict order over 128 entries, and publishes its per-configuration scores. Papers cite a position on GIFT-Eval as evidence of state-of-the-art accuracy. The problem the authors identify is that GIFT-Eval's 97 configurations come from only 28 datasets, four of which contribute eight configurations each, and the configurations of one dataset rise and fall together. So the effective evidence is 28 independent draws, not 97, and nobody has ever said how small a difference those 28 draws can actually detect.

The method is clean and it is the part worth stealing. Vernier reads a single file, one score for every entry on every evaluation unit, and answers three questions. First, how small a difference can the board detect? Because units from one data source are not independent, the source is the unit of sampling, not the configuration. They estimate the standard error of the mean difference between two entries with C-R-squared, a cluster-robust variance estimator corrected for having few clusters, with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. The resolution of a pair is then the minimum detectable effect, the smallest true difference that a five-percent test detects with 80 percent power, which is the sum of the two relevant t-statistic multipliers times the standard error. The board's resolution is the median of that over the neighbouring pairs of its top ten. Second, which entries cannot be separated from the leader? They run a cluster-robust t-test on each pair, plus a wild cluster bootstrap and its exact form as a robustness check, and the entries that do not separate from the leader form its tier. Third, does the order depend on who else submitted? They replay the board's version history and count the top-ten pairs among unchanged entries that reverse, which they call the bystander test.

The numbers are uncomfortable for the field. GIFT-Eval resolves 6.8 rank places, but the median gap between displayed neighbours is 0.91. So the board is displaying gaps that are an order of magnitude smaller than it can actually resolve, and its top ten spans 11.0 rank places, which is fewer than two of the steps its datasets can resolve. Of the nine neighbouring pairs in the top ten, the cluster-robust test separates exactly one before a Holm correction, and after the correction no neighbouring pair separates, under the cluster-robust test, the wild bootstrap, or the exact test. Each of the nine displayed gaps is smaller than its own pair's resolution. Closing that median gap would take about 48 times as many datasets. The test itself holds its level with only 28 datasets, declaring a false difference 5.2 percent of the time, which is right at the nominal five-percent level, against 6 to 10 percent for a percentile bootstrap. So the statistic is not the problem; the data is.

They run the same report on five more boards that publish per-entry, per-unit scores: fev-bench, its 20-task mini subset, Chronos Benchmark II, TIME, and BOOM. On every board built from 15 to 39 public data sources, the resolution is 3.6 to 19 times the median gap between displayed neighbours, and at most one of nine neighbouring pairs separates. BOOM is the exception, and it is instructive. BOOM treats each of its 2,554 series as an independent source, and with that many sources its resolution is 0.63 of its median gap, and all nine neighbours separate. Group BOOM's series into its 23 domains and only five of nine separate. The lesson is that independent sources, not evaluation units, set the resolution.

The pool-dependence finding is the second result and it is just as pointed. A mean-rank rule scores an entry against every other entry on each configuration, so it is pool-dependent, and a new arrival can displace entries that nobody reran. Replaying the history of four boards, pool-dependent rules reversed 43 untouched top-ten pairs, in 31 of 226 updates, with neither entry in any reversed pair having changed. On GIFT-Eval alone, in the week to early September 2026, three submissions from three unrelated organizations changed nothing else, and the order of three entries reversed completely. A pool-independent rule, the geometric mean of scaled error, uses only an entry's own scores, so no arrival can reverse an untouched pair, which is the recommendation they draw out.

There is a fourth finding that is easy to overlook. No board records how much history a model receives, and that is an evaluation setting that is invisible in the published scores because the score files record the numbers but not the configuration that produced them. They ran four open models at two history lengths, inference only, across 70 GIFT-Eval configurations, and shortening the history to 512 steps raised the error of three of the four models, two of them by 15.3 percent, all three separating from zero after a Holm correction. That is larger than the 11.8 percent spread of the displayed top ten, which means the unrecorded history-length setting can move a model more than the entire gap between the top entries.

Where I would push back. This is a two-author paper presented at a workshop, and the scope is deliberately narrow: it is about zero-shot forecasting boards that publish per-unit scores, and the conclusions do not automatically extend to boards that hide their per-configuration scores or to other model families. The 48-times-more-datasets estimate is a useful order-of-magnitude, but it is derived from GIFT-Eval's specific layout. And the bystander test depends on being able to replay the board's version history, which only some boards expose. The practical tool, Vernier, runs with one command on any leaderboard that publishes per-dataset scores, and that is the genuinely reusable artifact.

What I would trust: the core argument, that a board's resolution is set by its number of independent data sources and that the popular forecasting boards are displaying orders finer than their data can support. The cluster-robust resolution is a well-motivated statistic and the level-holding check is honest. What I would re-verify before acting on it: whether the specific board you care about publishes the per-unit scores Vernier needs, and whether the pool-dependence reverses are still happening in the current version of that board.

The bottom line: this is a small, rigorous paper that should change how you read a time-series leaderboard. A displayed position is only as meaningful as the board's resolution, and for most forecasting boards that resolution is far coarser than the gaps being celebrated. The fix is not more submissions to the same datasets; it is more independent data, and reporting the resolution, using a pool-independent rule, showing tiers, and recording how each model was run.
