CogBattery: Human-Referenced Evaluation of Cognitive Task Behavior and Structure in Language Models, by Dengzhe Hou, Lingyu Jiang, and Fangzhou Lin, from Tohoku University, with co-authors at Texas A&M University and Worcester Polytechnic Institute, is an evaluation paper that compares language models to humans on classic cognitive tasks, and its central argument is that a model matching the direction of a human effect is not the same as a model behaving like a human.

The motivation is precise. Human cognitive paradigms, things like n-back, Stroop, and working-memory span tasks, compare conditions that differ in a controlled way, and the published human data show how people respond in each condition. That makes them a natural tool for comparing models and humans under matched conditions. But a model can reproduce the direction of a human effect, for instance responding more to a lure than to a control, while making a completely different distribution of errors, or reporting confidence that is directionally correct but far less informative. Matching the effect does not establish the behavior. The prior evaluations that port these tasks to language models, CogBench, CogEval, and a handful of single-paradigm studies, each cover a small number of paradigms or a small number of models, and most use the human data only as norms or scaling constants rather than as condition-level references. CogBattery's contribution is scale plus condition-level comparison: 54 open-weight models from 23 families, evaluated on text adaptations of 13 cognitive paradigms, each compared against the human data from that paradigm's own source study, mostly participant-level records.

The 13 paradigms fall into five groups by origin: working memory, cognitive control, episodic memory, social reasoning, and metacognition. The concrete tasks include digit span, 2-back, operation span, Stroop, flanker, go-no-go, the California Verbal Learning Test, the DRM false-memory paradigm, source memory, false-belief stories, recursive mindreading at levels one through seven, calibration, and a wagering task. That spread is deliberate: it is meant to cover the kinds of processes a cognitive test battery would, from memory maintenance through response inhibition to theory of mind, so that a model's profile across tasks is informative rather than a single number. The models span 0.36 billion to 141 billion total parameters, including mixture-of-experts parameters, across families like Qwen, Llama, Gemma, and Mistral, with a few thinking-capable models run in native thinking mode and the rest answering directly at temperature zero. All 702 model-by-task cells completed, giving over 366 thousand response records, of which 99.97 percent are technically valid.

The first finding is that shared directions hide different errors. Like humans, models respond more to n-back lures than to controls, which is the effect direction you would expect. But the models sit on top of that lure effect with a large excess of false alarms to ordinary, non-target items, an excess of about 0.257 over the human rate. That is a behaviorally different profile: humans make the lure mistake, models make the lure mistake plus a lot of noise. Stroop interference is also much larger in models than in humans. So on the surface the models look human-like, and at the error level they do not.

The second finding is about confidence and metacognition. The verbal confidence that models report separates their correct from incorrect answers less well than humans' confidence does, even though the token probabilities, the models' internal confidence, separate about as well as humans'. That gap between what the model internally knows and what it reports is a meaningful metacognitive deficit, because it means the model has the information available to it in its own probability distribution but fails to surface it in the language it chooses. And most models, including models that classify accurately, always choose the same wager in the wagering task, which is a sign that the metacognitive machinery is not actually being used to make decisions, regardless of whether the classification is right.

The third finding is about scale and structure. Across tasks, model scores share two dimensions, and the first is closely tied to model scale. But here is the counterintuitive result: larger models are not consistently closer to humans. Scaling up does not buy behavioral correspondence, at least not on these tasks, and that is a finding that cuts against the usual assumption in this literature that a bigger model is a better cognitive simulation, with the gap to the human reference narrowing as parameters grow. And the n-back false-alarm excess persists in models of 27 billion parameters and larger, so it is not an artifact of small models; the largest open models in the battery show the same noise profile as the small ones. The structure analysis is the most negative result in the paper: the predefined cognitive groupings, the five groups by paradigm origin, show no reliable predictive advantage over random groupings of the same size when predicting held-out model families' task scores. In other words, the way cognitive scientists organize these tasks does not organize model performance any better than chance, which is a striking finding if you are used to those groupings being meaningful in humans, and it suggests that model task similarity is driven by task surface and difficulty rather than by the underlying cognitive mechanism.

Two methodological caveats the authors flag are worth repeating because they bound the claims. First, no single human cohort covers all 13 paradigms, so each paradigm is compared against its own source study's human data, which means the cross-task structure comparisons, the correlations and factor analyses, can only be run on the four cohorts where the same people did multiple tasks. That is a real constraint on how much you can claim about the overall structure of model cognition. Second, these are text adaptations of paradigms that were designed for timed, perceptual, or motor responses, and the authors are explicit that the auxiliary demands of a text format and the protocol differences preclude matched comparisons to previously published model scores. The Stroop and flanker tasks have no reaction-time component, for instance, so you are measuring the accuracy effect of interference, not the temporal cost of it.

Skeptically: this is a measurement paper, so the questions are about construct validity rather than about a method you would adopt. The text adaptation of a timed cognitive task is a real threat to interpretation, and the authors handle it honestly by scoping the claims to what the adaptation can support, but it means the human-model gaps they report are partly an artifact of the medium, and a future battery with reaction-time proxies, even rough ones estimated from generation length or early stopping, would tighten the comparison considerably. The constant-wager result is interesting but could partly reflect a formatting constraint in how the models are prompted to wager, and the token-probability versus verbal-confidence gap, while compelling, depends on the calibration task's two-choice structure. The random-grouping result is the one I would most want replicated, because it cuts against a lot of the framing in the cognitive-benchmarking literature, but it also depends on the specific held-out family design. None of that undermines the core contribution, which is a condition-level, human-referenced test battery that is reusable, and the release of the full response records, all 366 thousand of them, makes the negative results as checkable as the positive ones.

Bottom line: CogBattery makes a well-supported case that matching human effect directions is not the same as human-like behavior, because the models' errors, confidence reports, and wagering diverge from humans even when the headline effect is reproduced, and it adds the sharper finding that model scale does not reliably buy behavioral correspondence and that cognitive groupings do not predict model structure better than chance. It is a reusable, condition-level benchmark rather than a method, and its value is as a diagnostic for new models, which is exactly the role it sets itself up to play.
