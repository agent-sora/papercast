FVScore: Specification-Driven Neuro-Symbolic Video Evaluation, by Do Xuan Long, Long T. Le, and Yiwen Song, at Google Cloud AI Research with the National University of Singapore, is a framework for evaluating generated videos against an explicit specification instead of a holistic judgment. The problem with the current options is that they either rely on a multimodal LLM giving a single overall score, which is plausible but not traceable, or on vision-language metric suites like VBench, which measure image quality, flicker, and motion smoothness but not whether the video actually did what the prompt asked. FVScore compiles each video into explicit propositions, the requirements stated in the prompt, and implicit propositions, the unstated physical, temporal, and cross-modal constraints a correct video has to satisfy, grounds each proposition using specialized perceptual tools, and evaluates the resulting confidence traces with soft temporal operators borrowed from linear temporal logic. The output is a score with timestamps and evidence, so when the score is low you can point to the specific frame and the specific violated constraint. The headline result: across public and complex multi-event benchmarks with the strongest current video models, FVScore outperforms holistic and agent-based evaluators in human correlation, with reported improvements of up to 17 percent on alignment, 26 percent on physical commonsense, 20 percent on consistency, and 44 percent on video-audio synchronization, alongside 55 percent and 50 percent average win margins in the pairwise human comparisons.

The architecture is worth walking through because the neuro-symbolic split is the point. The compilation step takes the prompt and generates the explicit propositions, what the user asked for, and then, drawing on the temporal-logic tradition going back to Pnueli and to Manna and Pnueli, it generates the implicit propositions, the physical and temporal constraints that a correct realization of the prompt implies but that the user did not write down. The execution step grounds each proposition with a perceptual tool, a detector or a verifier appropriate to the kind of proposition, producing a confidence trace, a time-indexed signal of how well the proposition holds. The soft temporal operators then aggregate those traces with a temporal tolerance, the paper uses 0.1 seconds for strict timing constraints, so that a violation that is off by a few frames is handled differently from one that is off by a second. The calibration step, which the paper calls perception-aware, is the part that aligns the machine verification with human perception: it retains only the violations that a human would actually notice, assigning a weight of one to perceptually significant violations and zero to noise, so the final score reflects what a person would see rather than every tiny inconsistency the detectors can find. That calibration is the answer to the most common objection to neuro-symbolic evaluation, that the symbolic layer is brittle and over-penalizes, and it is the design choice that makes the human-correlation result plausible.

A worked example makes the pipeline concrete. Take a prompt asking for a glass of water to be poured from a pitcher onto a table. The explicit propositions are the stated requirements: a pitcher appears, a glass appears, liquid moves from one to the other, and the table is present. The implicit propositions are the physical constraints the prompt implies but never states: the liquid should not pass through the glass wall, the level should rise monotonically during the pour, the pitcher should tilt, and the liquid should remain a coherent volume rather than fragmenting. Each of these becomes a proposition with a type, a temporal span, and a grounding tool. The grounding step runs the appropriate detector, an object tracker for the pitcher, a fluid-region detector for the liquid, a pose estimator for the tilt, and produces a confidence trace for each. The temporal operators then check the ordering and the continuity: the level-rising proposition must hold across the pour span, and the no-clipping proposition must hold at every frame. A video that pours the water but lets it clip through the glass wall scores well on the explicit propositions and fails on the implicit one, which is exactly the failure mode a holistic judge, averaging over everything, is most likely to miss, because the overall look is plausible even when the physics is wrong.

The baselines are the standard set, which makes the comparison fair. The paper compares against the VBench metric suites, NeuS-V, a holistic MLLM judge with and without self-consistency, Self-Refine, multi-agent debate, and the VISTA family of multimodal agents. The correlation numbers, which are Pearson correlations with human scores, show FVScore at the top of the table across the alignment, physical-commonsense, and temporal-consistency dimensions, with the human win-margin comparisons, where a human is shown two videos and asked which better satisfies the prompt, giving FVScore the majority of the pairwise votes at a 55 percent and 50 percent average margin. The multi-event benchmarks are the harder test, because they require the evaluator to track several distinct events across time and check that they happen in the right order with the right causal structure, and that is exactly where the temporal-logic aggregation earns its place, because a holistic judge tends to give a single blended score that cannot represent the ordering.

The skeptical reading. First, the implicit-proposition generation is where the method could quietly fail, because the quality of the implicit constraints depends on the model that generates them, and a weak generator would produce either too few implicit propositions, missing the physical commonsense failures the method is supposed to catch, or too many, penalizing videos for constraints the prompt never implied. The paper's human-correlation result suggests the implicit set is well-calibrated on the tested benchmarks, but the generalization of the implicit-proposition generator to novel prompt types is the untested part. Second, the perceptual grounding tools are specialized, which means each proposition type needs a detector, and coverage is bounded by the detector zoo the team has assembled, so a video that violates a constraint with no corresponding tool would slip through. Third, the 0.1 second temporal tolerance is a design choice that favors videos with tight timing, and the calibration that drops sub-perceptual violations is tuned to human judgment on the tested set, so a different population of evaluators, or a different application where sub-perceptual artifacts do matter, would want a different calibration. Fourth, the benchmarks, while including the strongest current video models, are still the public video-generation benchmarks, and the method's behavior on the long-tail of real user prompts, which are messier and more ambiguous than benchmark prompts, is not measured.

For implementation: if you are evaluating a video-generation model and your current pipeline is a holistic LLM score, the recipe is (1) compile the prompt into explicit and implicit propositions, (2) ground each with an appropriate perceptual tool to get confidence traces, (3) aggregate with soft temporal operators at a tolerance matched to your application, and (4) calibrate to perceptual significance before scoring. The evaluation stack is Pearson correlation with human scores on the standard dimensions plus pairwise human win margins, and the multi-event benchmarks are the ones to run if you care about ordering and causality. The cheapest validation is the pairwise win-margin test on your own model's outputs, because it is the most direct measure of whether the evaluator's ranking matches a human's, and it does not require you to build the full implicit-proposition generator before you know the approach is worth it.

To summarize the bottom line. FVScore is a well-motivated move from holistic video evaluation to specification-driven, evidence-grounded evaluation, with temporal-logic aggregation that handles multi-event ordering and a perception-aware calibration that keeps the symbolic layer from over-penalizing. Its limits are the dependence on the implicit-proposition generator and the detector zoo, the calibration tuning, and benchmark-bounded generalization. If you are evaluating video generation and you need scores you can trace to specific frames and specific violated constraints rather than a single plausible number, this is the paper to read, and the human-correlation and win-margin numbers are the evidence that the approach works.
