Title: Navier-Stokes lost in translation: Why Lean verification of AI autoformalisation does not guarantee correct natural language proofs
Authors: Alexander Bastounis, Fabian Circelli, Anders C. Hansen

Navier-Stokes lost in translation is from Alexander Bastounis, Fabian Circelli, and Anders Hansen, with Bastounis in the mathematics department at King's College London and Circelli and Hansen in the Department of Applied Mathematics and Theoretical Physics at the University of Cambridge. This is a formal-methods and logic paper, and it is the kind of work that sits exactly at the intersection of LLMs and mathematics that is worth covering. The setting is autoformalisation, the process of using an AI system to translate a natural-language mathematical statement or proof into a formal language such as Lean, so that a proof assistant can check it. It is increasingly used to verify mathematical texts, including ones generated by AI, the clearest example being OpenAI's announced proof of blow-up of solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations. The paper's claim is that this pipeline does not do what people think it does: a Lean proof that compiles and verifies is not a verification of the natural-language proof it was translated from, because the translation itself is where the error enters.

The core theoretical result is a hardness statement. They show that resolving the ambiguities in a mathematical natural-language text, the step you need in order to produce a semantically faithful translation, is arbitrarily high in the solvability complexity index hierarchy. In the informal version of their result, providing semantically faithful autoformalisation is harder than any computational problem in the arithmetical hierarchy, including the halting problem, which sits at solvability complexity index 1, while the faithful-translation problem they are studying sits at the top, essentially infinity. The way to read that is not as a practical engineering warning but as a statement of kind: there is no algorithm that will, in general, decide whether a given natural-language statement has been faithfully translated, so the gap between the natural-language claim and the formal Lean proof can never be closed by the formal checker alone. The formal checker verifies the Lean code; it has no access to the natural language it is supposed to represent, so it cannot detect a mistranslation.

The reason the hardness result holds, in brief, is that faithful translation requires resolving a family of ambiguities in the natural-language text that are independently undecidable. A mathematical sentence in natural language is underspecified in several distinct ways at once: which quantifier scopes over which, what the definite article is picking out, what is being held fixed versus what is being asserted about an arbitrary object, and so on. The paper's argument is that deciding whether a particular resolution of those ambiguities is the one the author intended is not a single computable problem but a problem that sits at the top of the solvability complexity index, because it requires deciding the truth of statements of unbounded complexity about the author's intent and the surrounding mathematical context. The practical consequence, which they state plainly, is that back-translation, the idea that if you can translate back from the Lean proof to natural language you have checked the correspondence, does not escape the problem either, because the back-translation has the same ambiguity-resolution requirement in the reverse direction, and the paper shows it is as hard as the forward direction.

They also organize the types of autoformalisation, which is worth a line because it scopes the problem precisely. There are two main kinds. In one, the theorem statement has already been faithfully translated into Lean, and the AI's job is only to translate the alleged natural-language proof into a formal proof, so the statement is fixed and correct and the question is whether the proof's reasoning transfers. In the other, both the statement and the proof are in natural language and the AI has to translate the statement as well, which is the harder and more common setting, and it is this setting where the semantic-fidelity gap is widest, because a mistranslated statement can be proven perfectly correctly in Lean while saying something the original author never claimed. The Navier-Stokes example is in the second category, and it is the one that matters for how the community currently uses autoformalisation, because the announced proof is being checked by formalizing the paper's own text, not a separately verified statement.

The examples make the point concrete, and they are the part of the paper I would put on a slide. They take real AI-generated translations and show that the Lean proof compiles and is accepted, while the natural-language proof it is supposed to represent says something different. Their sharpest example is again OpenAI's Navier-Stokes announcement. They look at a specific pressure-flux bound, one of the key estimates in the announced proof, and show that the Lean formalization establishes a different estimate than the one in the natural-language paper. The proof methods diverge: the natural-language proof relies on the boundedness of the Riesz transform as a map from L to the power three halves to L to the power three halves, while the Lean proof does not use that boundedness and instead applies Holder's inequality with different exponents, which changes the structure of the bound. So the Lean proof is a correct proof of a related but distinct statement, and it compiles cleanly, and none of that machinery flags that it is not a faithful translation of the published natural-language argument. They note, pointedly, that when they ask a frontier model directly about the concern, the model itself shares the worry that the bound is not semantically faithful, which is a nice data point that the failure is detectable by a careful human or model reading the pair side by side, but it is not detectable by the Lean checker.

A skeptical read. The theoretical result is the strongest and most important piece, because it reframes what autoformalisation can promise: it is not a verifier of the natural-language proof, it is a verifier of a formal artifact whose fidelity to the source is a separate, generally undecidable question. I would want to see the solvability-complexity argument in full detail before treating the infinity claim as settled, because hierarchy-membership results of this kind have to be careful about exactly which quantifier structure they are encoding, and the informal statement, that it is harder than any problem in the hierarchy, is the kind of claim that can be stated more strongly than the theorem actually proves. The second thing I would re-verify is the scope of the examples. They show that specific estimates in the Navier-Stokes proof diverge, which is a real finding, but a careful reader would want to know whether this is a systematic property of the translation pipeline or a few local divergences, and the paper, being a logic-and-analysis paper, is not positioned to give that frequency, so the examples establish that the failure is possible and real, not how often it happens. The third: the practical implication is a bit uncomfortable to state, which is that the community should stop treating a passing Lean check as evidence that the natural-language result is correct, and instead require a separate, human or model-assisted check of the translation, and that requirement is expensive and the paper does not offer a cheap substitute.

Bottom line: this paper makes a clean, well-grounded case that a compiling Lean proof is not a verification of the natural-language theorem it was meant to formalize, because semantic faithfulness of the translation is a generally undecidable step that the formal checker has no access to, and the Navier-Stokes example shows the divergence is not hypothetical but is already present in a real, high-profile announced result. If your workflow treats autoformalisation as a correctness gate for AI-generated mathematics, this is the paper to read before you trust the next one, because the gate it describes does not check the thing you think it checks, and the fix, a separate human or model check of the translation, is one the current pipelines do not have.
