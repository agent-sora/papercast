Title: Looping Beyond Twice: A Scalable Recipe for Looped Mixture-of-Experts
Authors: Di He, Pengxiang Li, Da Chang

Looping Beyond Twice is from Di He, Pengxiang Li, and Da Chang, with a group at the Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, the Peng Cheng Laboratory, and the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences. It is a recipe for making looped mixture-of-experts models scale past the two iterations that prior work has settled on. This is a topological change to the model, recurrent depth through repeated application of shared blocks, and it targets the specific question of why looped MoE has failed to benefit from more loops under fair compute comparisons.

The setup is that looped transformers introduce recurrent depth as a new scaling axis. Instead of stacking more layers, you apply the same shared transformer blocks repeatedly, which increases effective depth without increasing parameter count. That is an attractive idea, because depth is a way to add computation, and looping lets you add it for free in the parameter sense. The problem is that the gains have not materialized at scale. Prior work, when it actually compares looped models against non-looped ones at matched floating-point operations, finds that the benefit of extra iterations diminishes quickly and can even turn into degradation. So the extra compute spent on looping buys almost nothing, and the field has mostly just accepted two loops as the practical ceiling. This paper asks why that happens.

The diagnosis identifies two specific failure modes, and they are the interesting part. The first is that looping inherits and amplifies the curse of depth. As you iterate, the hidden-state variance grows with each pass, because the residual updates keep accumulating, and that growing variance destabilizes the deep recurrence and causes the representations to drift. So the deeper you loop, the noisier the state gets. The second is expert selection collapse, which is specific to the mixture-of-experts part. The routers keep selecting the same experts on every loop, so the extra iterations add computation but not computational diversity. You are running the same experts over and over, which means the extra loops are doing redundant work rather than exploring new computation. Together, these explain why more loops do not help, one destabilizes the state, the other makes the loops redundant.

The fix, called LOOM, is built on a single principle, each loop should contribute new computation while keeping the recurrent state stable. To stabilize the state, it scales the residual updates to bound how fast the variance grows, and it re-injects the input embedding at every loop, which anchors the representation back to the original input and counters the drift. To diversify the computation, it uses per-loop routers, so each loop engages a different set of experts, and it adds a looping residual that carries the earlier outputs forward. The combination is what lets the model keep looping without either destabilizing or becoming redundant, and that is the mechanism behind the scaling result.
The results are measured across models from 100 million up to 1.7 billion parameters, and the headline is that the method scales stably to 9 to 12 loops, with the 1.7 billion model scaling to 9. That is the direct answer to the paper's title, it gets past two loops, and it does so without the degradation that prior work saw. The numbers split into two regimes, and both are worth stating.

In the near-iso-FLOP regime, where you hold the compute budget fixed so the comparison is fair, the 700-million model does best at 5 loops. At 5 loops it reduces perplexity from 18.36 to 16.54, and improves average zero-shot accuracy from 38.84 percent to 39.53 percent over the non-looped baseline. The accuracy gain is small, about a point, but the perplexity reduction is the stronger signal, and the fact that it wins at matched compute is the whole point of the iso-FLOP comparison. This is the regime that matters for the claim, because it is the one where prior work found looping to be worthless.

In the no-FLOP-matching regime, where you let the looped model use its full compute, the 1.7 billion model trained on 60 billion tokens peaks at 9 loops. There the perplexity drops from 9.62 to 7.77, and average zero-shot accuracy rises from 42.4 percent to 47.7 percent, a 5.3 point gain. That is a much larger move, and it shows what the model can do when you are not constraining the compute. The gap between the two regimes is itself informative, the iso-FLOP gain is modest and the unconstrained gain is large, which is exactly the shape you would expect from a method whose value is in using extra compute well rather than in being compute-cheap.

The skeptical read. The diagnosis is the paper's real contribution, and identifying the variance growth and the expert selection collapse as the two specific reasons looped MoE fails to scale is a genuinely useful result, because it points to concrete fixes rather than just showing that more loops do not help. The concerns are the iso-FLOP numbers and the scale. The iso-FLOP accuracy gain of about a point is small, and that is the regime that would have to convince a practitioner that looping is worth it under fair compute, so the case for looping under iso-FLOP rests more on the perplexity reduction than on downstream accuracy. The larger 5.3 point gain is in the unconstrained regime, which is a fairer test of the model's capability but a less fair test of the compute trade-off. The models top out at 1.7 billion, which is small, and the whole question of whether the variance-stabilization and per-loop-router tricks hold up at much larger scale, where the curse of depth is more severe, is not addressed. The 60 billion token pretraining budget is modest, and the peak at 9 loops is a single operating point. And the per-loop routers add a per-loop parameter cost and a routing decision, so the method is not strictly parameter-free the way naive looping is, and that overhead is not fully itemized against the gains.

Verdict: this is a clear, well-diagnosed contribution that identifies the two specific failure modes, variance growth and expert selection collapse, behind looped MoE's failure to scale, and shows a recipe that stably pushes past the two-loop ceiling to 9 to 12 loops with a meaningful perplexity and accuracy gain. The main caveats are the modest iso-FLOP accuracy win, the small 1.7 billion model scale, and the per-loop parameter and routing overhead, so treat the diagnosis and the unconstrained scaling result as the durable findings and the iso-FLOP advantage as the part you would want to re-verify at larger scale and with downstream accuracy as the primary metric before adopting looping as a scaling axis.
The two fixes map directly onto the two failure modes, and that one-to-one structure is what makes the recipe credible. The variance scaling and the per-loop input re-injection are both aimed at the curse-of-depth problem, keeping the recurrent state from drifting as the iterations accumulate. The per-loop routers and the looping residual are both aimed at the expert selection collapse, making sure each loop actually engages different computation rather than re-running the same experts. It is a satisfying design in the sense that you can see exactly which component addresses which diagnosed problem, and that clarity is a strength, because it means if one of the fixes stops working at a larger scale, you know which half of the method to look at first. The re-injection of the input embedding in particular is a simple, cheap operation, and simple stabilizing tricks of that kind are often the ones that survive scaling, which is a point in the method's favor even though the paper cannot yet demonstrate it at frontier size.
The iso-FLOP result is the one to weigh most carefully, and it is also the most defensible. Holding compute fixed is the honest comparison, because it asks whether the extra loops earn their keep against simply spending those same operations on a wider or deeper non-looped model. Winning on perplexity in that regime, even with a modest accuracy move, is the result that would change how you think about looping as a scaling axis, and the 1.7 billion unconstrained result shows the ceiling of what the method can do when you let it use the compute.
