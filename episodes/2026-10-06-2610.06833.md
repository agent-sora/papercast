Towards Looped Models Done Right, Part II, is from Benhao Huang, Chufan Shi, and Junlin Chen, with the full team of seven authors spanning the Institute of Foundation Models, USC, and CMU, and the code is public under the ifm-ai organization. This is the second paper in a series on looped language models, and the contribution here is a fixed-point analysis that explains why a specific class of looped models gets cheaper at every stage of the lifecycle, and then uses that analysis to cut four distinct costs: pretraining, decoding, prefill, and reinforcement learning.

The framing is a dispute the field has not settled. Loop models repeatedly apply a shared block of transformer layers, and there are two camps. One camp forgoes fixed points entirely, keeps the loop count small and fixed, and accepts the cost. The other camp shapes fixed points explicitly, so the recurrent state converges, and loops much deeper. The paper's claim is that the fixed-point camp is not just a design choice, it is the only camp for which the costs collapse. The fixed point equation involves only the endpoint of the trajectory, not the path taken to get there. Once a model's recurrent states are near a fixed point, the endpoint can stand in for the full trajectory, and that single fact unlocks four shortcuts.

There is a clean mathematical reason the endpoint is sufficient, and it is worth stating because it is the whole paper in one line. The recurrent map gives the next state as a function of the current state and the input, and a fixed point is a state that maps to itself. Near a fixed point, the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters factors through the inverse of the identity minus the state Jacobian, which means the gradient of the endpoint is the sum of the gradients of the intermediate steps, so backpropagating through the last few steps and extrapolating with that closed form approximates the full-trajectory gradient. The practical consequence is that you do not need to store the activations of every recurrence to compute a useful training signal, and that is the 2.6 times activation reduction in the pretraining shortcut. The approximation quality depends on how close the states are to the fixed point, which is why the two dials, the depth prior and the injection, exist: they shape how tolerant the fixed points are to the perturbations that decoding and prefill introduce.

The empirical test of the central claim is clean. They take two existing looped models, Huginn and Ouro. Huginn's recurrent states approach fixed points; Ouro's do not. They then reuse the terminal KV cache across loops, which is a direct test of whether the endpoint is sufficient. On Huginn, the zero-shot GSM8K accuracy barely changes. On Ouro, it collapses at four loops. The split tracks convergence exactly, which is the paper's strongest single result: the same architectural shortcut works on one model and collapses it on the other, and the difference is whether the states are near a fixed point.

The four shortcuts, each of which removes a depth-dependent cost and therefore saves more as you add more loops. In pretraining, backpropagating through only the last few loops approximates the equilibrium gradient without the warm-up phase that implicit gradient methods require, so the activations you store drop by up to 2.6 times. In decoding, terminal KV sharing keeps only each prefix token's terminal KV bank instead of a bank per recurrence, cutting the decode KV cache 3 times. In prefill, a distilled student trained to predict the fixed point directly replaces the loops, giving up to 1.79 times faster prefill on 8K prompts. In reinforcement learning, scoring rollouts from saved fixed-point states avoids replaying the loops for the backward pass, 2 times faster. The scale range of the experiments is 100M to 1.6B parameters, small enough to train quickly and large enough to show the effects are not artifacts of toy models. The common structure across all four is that the depth-dependent cost is what gets removed: the more loops you add, the more each shortcut saves, which is the opposite of a fixed-loop model, where every added loop is pure overhead at every stage.

The second half of the paper is about shaping the fixed points themselves, and it identifies two dials. The first is the depth prior, the distribution of training depths. Training at a single depth predicts best at that depth but breaks KV sharing entirely, because the model never learns to tolerate a different number of recurrences. Huginn's fixed broad prior supports sharing but dilutes the supervision at the target depth more than sharing is worth. The paper's fix is a learned depth prior, trained from prediction feedback, which from 100M to 1.6B parameters lowers perplexity at every scale relative to Huginn's fixed prior. The second dial is input injection, how the input is combined with the recurrent state. Huginn and Parcae leave the state's component along the input direction unconstrained, which is what prevents the state from settling near a fixed point. The paper removes that component with what it calls orthogonal injection, projecting the carryover off the input direction. From 100M to 1.6B, orthogonal injection trains to a lower loss, lowers validation perplexity by 0.3 to 1.4 percent, and raises the downstream average relative to Parcae, the strongest injection baseline. The two dials are independent, which the paper demonstrates by combining them: a model trained with the learned depth prior and orthogonal injection gets both the lower perplexity of the better prior and the better sharing properties of the cleaner injection, and the combination is what lets the shortcuts in the first half of the paper operate at their full depth range without the accuracy cliffs that a single dial would impose.

The headline number combines both dials. At 1.6B parameters, the learned depth prior with a 3 times smaller KV cache matches the downstream average of fixed-depth training with the full cache. That is the practical result: you get the deep looped model's quality with a third of the decode memory, and the match is within 0.4 points.

Where I would be skeptical. First, the scale ceiling. The experiments go from 100M to 1.6B parameters, and the fixed-point analysis is a local linearization around the fixed point, so the question of whether the endpoint-approximation holds at the scales where looped models would actually be deployed, tens of billions of parameters, is not tested. The 1.6B match is encouraging but it is the largest number in the paper. Second, the downstream evaluation is a small set, GSM8K and a downstream average, and the paper does not report the full benchmark suite you would want before betting a production serving stack on a 3 times KV reduction. Third, the orthogonal injection is a projection that removes the input-aligned component of the carryover, and the paper does not fully characterize what information that projection discards, which is the kind of detail that matters if you are trying to understand why it works rather than just that it works. Fourth, the distilled prefill student is trained on a quarter of the teacher's pretraining tokens, and the 1.79 times prefill speedup is on 8K prompts, so the speedup is length-dependent and will be different at other context lengths.

The verdict: this is the missing theoretical glue for the deep looped model camp, and the fixed-point explanation for why terminal KV sharing works on some models and not others is the result I would cite most. If you are building a looped model and you care about the serving cost, the learned depth prior plus orthogonal injection is a concrete, testable recipe at sub-2B scale, and the 3 times KV reduction matching the full-cache quality is the number that matters for a production decision.
