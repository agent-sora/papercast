ExplorationBench: Measuring AI Systems' Exploration in Verifiable Alien Worlds. The first three authors are Ming Zhang, Zhenghao Xiang, and Peizhong Gao, with the team spanning Fudan University, the Hunyuan team at Tencent, and Tsinghua University.

The problem this paper sets out to solve is one that most of the evaluation literature fudges, and it is stated cleanly here. Static benchmarks test whether a model can retrieve and reason with knowledge it already has. Exploration asks for something different: the system must decide what evidence to collect, accumulate that evidence across its interaction history, distill new knowledge from the outcomes, and then apply that knowledge to problems it has not seen. The tension that makes this hard to measure is that the tasks must be new to the system, so success cannot come from pre-training, yet the answers must be fully known to the evaluator, so success can be verified. Established domains like math and coding satisfy the second requirement but not the first, because the model may just be reproducing what it memorized. Genuinely novel outputs, like a new theorem, satisfy the first but not the second, because verifying them may require expert proof-checking or years of observation. The authors' move is to build worlds that satisfy both: deterministic, executable environments whose hidden rules deliberately conflict with familiar semantics, so that recall alone cannot solve the tasks and every answer is checkable exactly.

There are two sandboxes. AlienCode is a small programming language with 31 discovery targets and 70 tasks, and AlienLogic is a natural-deduction logic system with 24 discovery targets and 70 tasks. The design is the point, so here is a concrete example from AlienCode. Integer literals are silently XORed with 27, so the statement that emits the number 100 actually prints 127. A function called PLUCK counts list positions from one even though the manual says zero. Each system starts from the same flawed manual and the same fixed worked examples, then runs four exploration rounds in which it chooses its own probes, runs them in the environment, and adds the results to its growing exploration history. Nothing else enters the context. At the end of each round, at milestones M-zero through M-four, the system is tested without any tool access: it states the rules it believes hold and solves the 70 held-out tasks, which an interpreter or a proof-checker grades exactly. A trajectory is scored on where it ends, and a system is scored on its best complete trajectory out of three.
They evaluate ten frontier systems, each at its highest reasoning setting, with three independent trajectories per sandbox, and each held-out task is answered three times per milestone so a score is a mean rather than a single sample. The findings are organized around three questions: can systems acquire an alien world by exploring it, how does discovering a rule relate to using it, and how reliably does exploration succeed.

The headline result is that the strongest systems can genuinely acquire unfamiliar rules and apply them, but the trajectory-to-trajectory variance is large and continued exploration can stall or even reverse earlier gains. One finding I would underline is the one about being told versus finding out. In AlienCode, discovering the rules beats being handed the rule set: the best trajectory outscores the open-book condition, where the complete rules are supplied before exploration, for 7 of the 10 systems. GPT-5.6 Sol reaches 87.1 percent against 68.1 percent under the open-book condition, and one of the Tencent models reaches 62.4 against 27.1. The explanation they give is that exploration supplies practice in using the rules, not just the rules themselves. AlienLogic inverts the pattern completely: the open-book condition alone reaches 93 to 97 percent, no explored trajectory matches it, and exploring first adds nothing, with a median difference of zero points. The authors distill this into a clean diagnosis: AlienLogic is limited by discovery, while AlienCode is limited by use. That is a genuinely useful separation of the two failure modes, and it is the kind of result that is hard to get from a benchmark that does not control for recall the way this one does.

A second finding is that AlienCode hinges on two keystone rules, both positional, that together enter 51 of the 70 tasks: the index shift of PLUCK and the slice shift of CARVE. Of the 15 trajectories that end at or above 50 percent, 13 state both correctly in their rule reports; of the 9 that end below 25 percent, 6 state neither. That means a large chunk of the score is gated on the system catching two specific off-by-one conventions, which is a reminder that the benchmark, for all its cleverness, still has a small number of high-leverage facts that dominate performance. And a third, more uncomfortable finding: exploration gains arrive in leaps, at different times, and can reverse. In each best trajectory in AlienCode, the largest single step contributes 45 to 92 percent of the retained gain, and which round that step lands in differs by system. It is the first round for GPT-5.6 Sol, the second for Claude Opus 5, Kimi K3, and Gemini 3.8 Flash, the third for Grok 4.6, and the last round for two of the weaker systems that are still climbing at the end. So the M-four score is a snapshot of a noisy, non-monotonic process, and the best-trajectory-of-three scoring is doing real work in smoothing over that.

The paper also runs five exploration conditions that I think deserve more attention than they get in the abstract. Besides the autonomous condition, where the system designs its own probes, there is a fixed-probe condition that issues model-independent probes at matched volume, a hindsight condition that replays the probes of the system's own best trajectory, a without-tool condition, and a no-environment-feedback control. The point of the fixed-probe and hindsight conditions is to decompose how much of the gain comes from the quality of the probes the system chose versus the raw volume of evidence it collected. The result is that autonomous, self-designed probes beat fixed-probe exploration for most systems, which supports the finding that exploration works best when the system designs its own experiments rather than consuming a canned evidence stream. The no-feedback control, where the environment returns no results, drops scores sharply, confirming that the gains come from reading the environment and not from the model's internal prior about the rules. That decomposition is what makes the benchmark more than a leaderboard, because it isolates the specific capability, active evidence collection, from the adjacent ones, like following a provided rule sheet or just reasoning harder within a fixed context.

What I would trust here is the construct validity. This is the cleanest operationalization of exploration-versus-recall I have seen, because the worlds are executable, the answers are checked exactly, and the hidden rules are engineered to contradict pre-training priors so that a model cannot succeed by remembering. The flawed-manual-plus-worked-examples setup is a nice touch that mirrors how a researcher actually encounters a new domain: with a partially wrong document and a few examples to probe against. What I would be cautious about is the generalization. Two hand-built sandboxes with a known, small rule set are a far cry from open scientific discovery, and the fact that two keystone rules gate half the tasks means the benchmark may be measuring a narrow kind of inductive pattern-matching more than open-ended hypothesis formation. The Best@3-of-three scoring is also a choice that flatters the top of the leaderboard; the mean and worst-trajectory columns show how much variance is being smoothed away, and I would look at those before trusting any single ranking. The open-book ablation is the part I would re-run first, because it is the strongest evidence that the benchmark is actually measuring exploration and not just instruction-following over a provided rule list.

Bottom line: ExplorationBench is a well-constructed, contamination-resistant benchmark for a real gap in evaluation, and its discovery-versus-use separation is the most useful takeaway. But with only two sandboxes and a handful of high-leverage rules, it measures a specific, narrow slice of exploration, so treat its leaderboards as a first signal rather than a definitive ranking of which systems can actually do science.
