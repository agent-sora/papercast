The paper is MJev-Spatial: System One Spatial Intelligence via Native Attention Matching, from Luozheng Qin, Yuhan Zhu, and Changhai Zhou, with Pony Ma listed among the twelve authors, out of Xiaomi. The idea is that most spatial questions a vision-language model has to answer are not reasoning problems, they are lookups, and treating them as slow autoregressive generation is the wrong shape for the job. Instead of making the model emit a coordinate as a string of tokens, one digit at a time, MJev-Spatial turns each spatial judgment into a compact classification over a small candidate set, and it scores those candidates by reusing the backbone's own pretrained attention, so there is no new prediction head and no added parameters. That last detail is the part I want to hold onto.

The model is built on Qwen3.5-4B. The vision encoder and its merger are frozen, and the language backbone is fine-tuned with all of its parameters. A spatial question, the image, and the list of candidates are run through the causal sequence in one pass. Each candidate description ends with an end marker, and the whole list ends with a decision marker. After the first 31 language layers, the hidden state at each end marker is that candidate's representation in context, and the state at the decision marker is the decision representation. Here is the trick: instead of passing those states through the final layer's value and feed-forward paths, which a recent result called FreeRet found can drift representations toward predicting the next word rather than matching semantics, they score the candidates using the final attention layer's pretrained query and key projections. The decision state supplies the query, each candidate supplies a key, and the average scaled inner product across the query heads becomes the logit. A single softmax over the current candidates gives the class probabilities. So the classifier is pretrained from the start, and it is scoring by the exact machinery the backbone already uses to attend.

There are five output types, and each has its own candidate structure. Multiple choice keeps the option contents as candidates. Lengths use a two-stage codebook, 32 coarse roots and then a fine split into cells, where the interval edges are spaced by a log-one-plus rule so that a few centimeters and a few kilometers share one meter-based codebook, and choosing the right cell bounds the quantization error by about a centimeter or five percent of the value, whichever is larger. Counts are two stages of 32, which reconstruct any integer from zero to 1023 exactly. Angles use 14 roots over 30-degree intervals. And pointing is the interesting one, because a single fine region needs local detail, so it is three rounds of a nine-way grid decision, and after each of the first two selections the model crops the chosen region from the original pixels, encodes it, and uses it as evidence for the next decision. After three decisions the answer is the center of the final cell, which is effectively a 27 by 27 grid.

To get multiple points out of the same model, they expand several paths through the tree and assign each final cell the product of its conditional probabilities along the way. Those probabilities form a spatial map, and wherever there are separated high-probability regions, each one yields a prediction. They keep the cells above a fixed threshold relative to the peak, group the connected ones, and return the center of the strongest cell in each group, so the number of points comes out of the map itself, with no learned stop decision.

The training converts existing spatial annotations into 12.3 million question-answer pairs, with 7.1 million for multiple choice, 3.5 million for point localization, 1.6 million for length, 120 thousand for angle, and 38 thousand for counting, converted from five public spatial datasets. The key efficiency is joint stage supervision. Every stage of a question is supervised as categorical cross-entropy, and because the stages are serialized in causal order with teacher forcing, all of a question's stage losses are evaluated in a single language-backbone forward pass, which removes the separate training pass that each refinement stage would otherwise need. A subtle detail in the loss is that each stage is averaged per question, so a three-stage pointing example contributes no more total loss than a one-stage multiple-choice example, which keeps the training balanced across task types even though they have very different numbers of decisions. They also do length grouping and a longest-work-first assignment across GPUs to balance the load, since point examples carry two image crops and choice examples are short, and the whole run is a full-parameter fine-tune of the 4B language backbone on eight GPUs.

The result, on 12 spatial benchmarks against seven autoregressive vision-language models, is that MJev-Spatial leads the overall column at 65.64, ahead of the strongest generative baseline at 62.79, and it is the best or second-best on most of the individual benchmarks, including RefSpatial at 58.50 and Where2Place at 67.96, where the generative models fall well short. The point-prediction benchmarks are where the classification formulation does its most visible work.

The efficiency claim is the second half of the pitch, and it follows almost directly from the architecture. Because every candidate for a decision is scored in the same forward pass, and the unused value, gate, output, and feed-forward paths of the final layer are dropped entirely, the model is not running a long autoregressive loop to emit a coordinate as text. There is no parsing step, no token-by-token decode, no chance that a generated coordinate comes out malformed or points outside the image. For a pointing question the cost is the backbone pass plus two extra image encodings for the crop refinement, which is a fixed and small number of operations rather than a variable-length decode. That is the system one versus system two distinction the title is making, the judgment is computed the way a fast reflex is, in one shot, instead of reasoned out step by step.

What I would trust is the core design decision, which is well motivated and well executed. The FreeRet observation about the final MLP pushing toward lexical prediction is a real and underused result, and scoring with the pretrained query and key projections is a genuinely elegant way to get a classification head that is already semantically aligned, for free. The codebooks are careful, the multi-point decoding is principled rather than threshold-tuned, and the joint-stage supervision is a real engineering win.

What I would re-verify is the generality. This is one backbone, one family of 4B models, and the benchmark list is a spatial-understanding suite, so the claim of system one spatial intelligence is being tested on exactly the tasks it was designed for. I would want to see it hold against a much larger autoregressive model on the same set, and I would want to see the latency numbers, because the whole pitch is that a single forward pass and a softmax should be far cheaper than autoregressively decoding a coordinate, and the crop re-encoding for points adds two image encodings per point, which is a cost that has to be measured, not asserted. The codebooks also mean the model is committing to a fixed set of intervals, and I would want to see where it fails when the true value sits near a boundary.

Two-sentence verdict: MJev-Spatial is a clean and well-motivated reframing of spatial prediction as classification, and its use of the backbone's own pretrained attention to score candidates is the kind of parameter-free design that earns its elegance. It leads a 12-benchmark spatial suite over strong generative baselines, though the evidence is one model family on its home turf, so treat it as a compelling architecture result that still needs broader and latency-verified generality.
