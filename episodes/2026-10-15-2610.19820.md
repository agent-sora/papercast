The paper is ActionOPD: Distilling Decisions, Not Tokens, from Huining Yuan, Xiangmin Yi, and Zelai Xu, all at Tsinghua University. It is a distillation method for language-model agents, and its central argument is about granularity. When you are distilling a capable but expensive agent into a smaller one, the question is not just which states to supervise at, but what unit of behavior to align. The paper's claim is that the natural unit is not the token, and not the final trajectory, but the semantic action: a tool call with its name and arguments, or a natural-language response with its communicative intent.

The problem is the one that motivates all on-policy distillation. In offline imitation, the student learns from sequences the teacher generated, so the prefixes it trains on are the teacher's, not the student's. At inference the student continues from prefixes it wrote itself, and those are a different distribution. That occupancy mismatch means the student's own mistakes are underrepresented in training, and once it errs at inference it can walk into a prefix it has never seen, and the error compounds. The paper's framing sharpens the question into two separate axes. The first is the state axis: at which states should the teacher supervise, the teacher's own states or the student's. The second is the granularity axis: at what level of description should the behavior be aligned, the token, the trajectory, or something in between. Offline imitation supervises at teacher states and token granularity. Token-level on-policy distillation supervises at student states and token granularity. Both get the state axis right or wrong in different ways, but neither touches the granularity axis, and the paper's claim is that the granularity axis is the one that matters for agents, because an agent's behavior is decided by its actions, and actions are coarser than tokens. On-policy distillation fixes the distribution problem by supervising the student on its own rollouts, using the teacher's next-token distribution at each student-generated prefix as the target. But standard on-policy distillation is token-level, and that carries two costs. It requires the teacher's token probabilities, which a closed or black-box teacher does not expose, and it typically requires the teacher and student to share a tokenizer so that the token distributions are even comparable. And even when those are available, token-level supervision never actually checks whether the student made the same decision. The same tool call can be phrased a hundred ways, and the same decision can be reached through different reasoning, so matching tokens is a proxy for matching decisions, and a loose one at that.

ActionOPD reframes the objective around actions. A tool call is a decision expressed through its name and its arguments. A natural-language reply is a decision expressed through its intent and its information content. The goal becomes maximizing the expected likelihood of the teacher's actions at states the student actually visits. Concretely, at each student-encountered state, the method samples actions from both the student and a black-box teacher. It then rewards the student's sampled actions according to their semantic agreement with the sampled teacher actions. A separate matcher, instructed to judge agreement rather than correctness, decides whether a student action and a teacher action express the same decision. The student is then trained by a policy-optimization step that pushes up the probability mass of student outputs that match teacher actions. Critically, the student never has to reproduce the teacher's written reasoning, and the teacher never has to hand over token probabilities. The supervision is action-level, and it flows through a semantic matcher instead of a shared tokenizer.

That last point is the practical unlock. Because the teacher is treated as a black box that just samples actions, ActionOPD works across model families and across tokenizers. The student and teacher do not need to share a model family, and they do not need compatible tokenization, which rules out a large class of distillation setups where the teacher you want to copy is a proprietary or differently-tokenized model. The objective is also a policy gradient on sampled actions, not a distribution-matching loss, so it composes with the way the student is already being rolled out. In the self-distillation variant the context budget is explicit: a 32,768-token context window with a 4,096-token response limit, the student prompt capped at 24,576 tokens and the teacher prompt at 28,672, the difference being the space for teacher-only privileged inputs, and the teacher's own output tokens and private prompt tokens are excluded from the student's loss, so the gradient only ever touches student-generated candidate tokens. The privileged context in Self-ActionOPD is a reviewed summary of the private goals, constraints, and known-versus-unknown information in a task, plus reference tool names and arguments, and it is withheld from both the student and the matcher even though it can influence which student actions get rewarded.

The results. They train Qwen3 students of 4B and 8B parameters, using two different teachers, on a set of curated tool-use tasks, and report average gains of 3.3 to 6.9 percentage points across four out-of-distribution benchmarks. The gains scale with both student size and teacher capability, which is the scaling behavior you want to see from a distillation method. On tau-squared-bench, an interactive agent benchmark, ActionOPD is competitive with both offline distillation and token-level on-policy distillation, which is the comparison that matters, because those are the baselines a reader would reach for first. They also explore a self-distillation variant, Self-ActionOPD, where the teacher is the same model as the student but given privileged context, and use a 32,768-token context window with a 4,096-token response limit for both roles, giving the teacher an extra 4,096 tokens for private inputs while excluding the teacher's own output tokens from the student's loss. That privileged-context design is the interesting one, because it means the method can close part of the student-teacher gap without requiring a strictly larger model, and it also means the student is being trained on a distribution of decisions that the student could not have reached on its own, which is a different and more aggressive form of distillation than matching an external teacher's behavior.

Now the skeptical pass. The improvements are real but small, a few points on average, and they are measured on four out-of-distribution benchmarks that the paper should be fully explicit about. A 3 to 7 point gain is meaningful in agent work, but it is exactly the size where benchmark selection and task curation can move the number around, so I would want the full per-benchmark table and the task list before treating the average as stable. The semantic matcher is doing a lot of quiet work. It is the thing that decides whether two actions count as the same decision, and if it is too lax it rewards superficially similar actions, and if it is too strict it gives sparse, noisy signal. The paper should report the matcher's agreement rate and its sensitivity, because the whole method inherits the matcher's errors. The black-box framing is the selling point, but a black-box teacher means you are paying for teacher samples at every student state, which can be a substantial inference cost at scale, and the paper is light on the compute comparison against token-level on-policy distillation, which at least reuses log-probabilities in one forward pass. I would re-run the tau-squared-bench comparison, confirm the four out-of-distribution gains hold across seeds, and audit the matcher on a hand-labeled slice of action pairs, because that is where the method's ceiling is set.

Bottom line: ActionOPD is a clean reframing of agent distillation that moves the supervision unit from the token to the semantic action, and that buys you black-box, cross-tokenizer distillation for free. The reported gains are modest and the method inherits the quality of its semantic matcher, which is the component to scrutinize before adopting, but the action-level objective is a sensible primitive that should carry over to other agent training setups.
