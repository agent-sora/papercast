A Broader Look into MLLM's Retrieval Heads, by Jia Wang and Yuqing Li at the Institute of Information Engineering of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Jiangnan Li at Tencent, with corresponding author Zheng Lin also at the Academy. The paper asks two questions about retrieval heads, the small set of attention heads in a large language model that move information from context to output: does that mechanism survive as models gain new input modalities, and can it be used to make models better rather than just to describe them.

The starting point is the retrieval-head literature from text and image-text models, which has established that a sparse set of heads does the copying from context to output, that ablating them collapses long-context accuracy, and that the set is detectable from attention patterns alone. The gap this paper fills is twofold. Most analyses stop at vision-language models, so it is unknown whether audio enters the same circuit at all. And nearly all of the work is descriptive, so the authors take the extra step of asking whether the detected heads can be trained on directly.

The first question is answered with a controlled experiment. The authors take a background video and insert a six-digit secret number into it in exactly one of two ways: either synthesized speech says the digits while the video track stays unchanged, or the number appears in a one-second centered banner while the audio track stays unchanged. Everything else, the video, the question, the insertion point, is held fixed, so the only thing that changes between the two conditions is which sense carries the answer. They run this paired needle-in-a-haystack setup, which they call AV-NIAH, through omni-modal models and score every attention head by how much its question-to-needle attention rises when a real question replaces an uninformative placeholder on the same input. A head is a retrieval head if it lands in the top five percent of that score ranking.

Across 18 checkpoints spanning plain language models, vision-language models, and omni-modal models, including Qwen2.5-Omni, MiniCPM-o, and SALMONN in the 3B through 8B range, the pattern is consistent. Retrieval heads stay sparse: fewer than one in ten heads carries half of all the positive retrieval score in every model, and the high scores cluster in middle and later-middle layers. They are also shared across the senses: between 69 and 83 percent of the top-5-percent heads are the same whether the answer is spoken or displayed, while two random sets of that size would share fewer than three heads. The full ranking of all heads agrees across the two conditions with a Spearman correlation between 0.88 and 0.92 in all eight omni-modal models. The authors also show the mechanism survives adaptation: every omni-modal checkpoint stays strongly correlated with the language or vision-language backbone it was built from, with the SALMONN models at 0.97 or better.

The detection criterion is worth stating precisely because the rest of the paper leans on it. For each example, a head's evidence attention is the average attention it pays from the question-token positions to the evidence-token positions. But some of that attention is a positional or content preference that has nothing to do with the question. So the authors recompute the same quantity with an uninformative question on the identical context, and the calibrated retrieval score is the difference, averaged over examples. Heads are ranked by that score, and the retrieval heads are the top five percent. The ablation protocol is matched in the way that makes the causal claim defensible: a head set is zeroed before the attention output projection, and the control is the same number of heads drawn at random from outside the retrieval set, at the same layers, across three seeds. The reported effect is the additional drop beyond that random control, tested with paired cluster bootstrap and Holm correction, so a small effect that could be noise does not get dressed up as a finding.

The causal test is where the claim becomes hard. They ablate the top-5-percent heads by zeroing their outputs and measure the additional drop compared to ablating the same number of randomly chosen heads at the same layers. In Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, audio retrieval exact match falls from 98.3 percent to zero, while matched random ablation leaves it at 96.9 percent. The same collapse happens in all four models they test, for both spoken and displayed evidence, at 100-second and 300-second background lengths. A more interesting subset is the cross-modal shared heads, the roughly a dozen heads selected under both the audio and the visual condition. They make up under 2 percent of all heads, and ablating them also hurts tasks the heads were never selected for: in the 8-task suite of document question answering, spatial perception, and visual mathematics, the shared-head drop beats the random control on every task and beats an image-retrieval-head control in 15 of the 16 model-task pairs, most strongly on visual math. In other words, a tiny shared set of heads does double duty as the model's evidence-locating hardware.

The second question turns these heads into an optimization target. For multimodal document retrieval on MMDocIR, the model scores candidate pages by summing the attention from the question to each page over a fixed set of selected retrieval heads. The head set is detected once on the base model and frozen for both training and inference, with 16 heads pooled from four detection conditions. Because raw attention scores live in small, variable ranges, each candidate's score is rescaled per example to a fixed logit range with detached endpoints, so the gradients still flow through every candidate while the scale stays stable. They train that scorer with a pairwise ranking loss, one relevant page against one hard negative from the same document presented in random order, with the gradients updating LoRA adapters on the attention projections while the head set itself stays frozen. On Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct with 16 heads, page Recall at 1 goes from 56.45 to 62.48, a gain of 6.03 points, and the gain holds in all ten document domains. The same training also improves layout-level retrieval even though the training data contain no layout labels at all: layout Recall at 1 moves from 47.14 to 48.60. The effect is not a Qwen quirk. Repeating it on Qwen2.5-VL-7B gives a page gain of 5.69 points and on Gemma3-12B a gain of 2.17, and out-of-domain scores on MMMU, MathVista, and MMLongBench-Doc move by under one point either way.

Where I would push back is on scale and on transfer. A 6-point Recall at 1 on a single document-retrieval benchmark is a real but modest win, and the method is a scorer, not a full retrieval system: the paper itself notes that its trained model exceeds some published retrievers only under incomparable settings, and the controlled comparison is against the untrained heads. The bigger interpretability claims rest on one needle format, a spoken or displayed digit string, and while the authors do show the results persist across background lengths and detection material, whether retrieval heads for natural-language evidence are the same heads as for a digit string is only partially addressed. Still, the paired-modality design is a clean one, and the finding that modality adaptation barely touches the retrieval circuit, that omni-modal models reuse the backbone's heads and extend them to new senses, is the kind of structural result that is hard to get any other way.

My verdict: the paper is a careful causal analysis of a small mechanism with a modest but real optimization payoff, and its strongest contribution is showing that a sub-2-percent set of cross-modal shared heads is load-bearing for document understanding and visual math, not just for retrieval. The ranking-training result is a sensible first step, and I expect this head-scoring idea to show up in more document-retrieval pipelines next year.
