Paint What You See: Benchmarking Dexterous Visual Tool Use in Multimodal Agents, by Shudong Liu of Peking University and SenseTime Research, Dongyang Chen of Tsinghua University and SenseTime, and Enci Zhang of Peking University, with further authors from SenseTime. The capability being tested is one those benchmarks cannot see: fine-grained, closed-loop, parameterized visual action. The agent looks at a reference image and a canvas, emits a brush stroke as thirteen continuous parameters, a quadratic Bezier path with start, control, and end points, per-endpoint radius and opacity, and a shared color, the renderer applies it, and the updated canvas comes back as feedback. Every parameter directly shapes the result; nothing absorbs imprecision. The authors call it dexterous visual tool use, by analogy with robot manipulation, and contrast it with everything current benchmarks measure: web and GUI agents succeed inside generous tolerance regions, and visual editing agents delegate execution to external generative models. Here there is no tolerant region and no delegating.

The benchmark, EASEL, centers on reference-guided reconstruction: one hundred ten samples across four difficulty tiers, from programmatic shapes at sixty-four by sixty-four with a thirty-two-step budget up to natural images at one hundred twenty-eight with one hundred twenty-eight steps, plus five semantic tasks, two outline-annotation tasks scored by Dice coefficient, a handwriting task, and two mazes scored by path progress, all told up to eleven thousand three hundred ninety-two interactions for a full evaluation. Similarity is a weighted composite: half structural SSIM, thirty percent color fidelity, twenty percent edge overlap, with trajectory diagnostics, similarity at the halfway step, area under the curve, best-achieved similarity, and the final-best gap, designed to expose how models use feedback rather than just where they end up.

The results should puncture some assumptions. Twenty-five models evaluated, six closed APIs and nineteen open weights from four to ninety billion parameters. The best agent, Gemini three point one Pro, reaches zero point five three five final similarity, which the score anchors show is exactly the level of filling the canvas with the reference's mean color, zero point five three four, though Gemini does capture some edge structure where fills score zero. Claude Opus four point seven follows at zero point four seven two, open-source models cluster in the zero point four zero to zero point four five band with small variance and no correlation with parameter count, and the specialist painting policy that generated the training data reaches zero point six six five within agent budgets and zero point seven one five with its native two hundred fifty steps, so the task is demonstrably solvable and the metric has headroom; the models are simply far from it. The trajectory diagnostics split closed-source behavior into two failure modes: plateau models, Gemini and the Claudes, rise sharply within the first five percent of steps and then effectively stop making meaningful changes; degrading models, GPT-five point five, GLM-five V Turbo, and Qwen three point five plus, keep acting and make the canvas worse, with GPT-five point five showing the largest final-best gap at zero point zero seven three. Across every closed-source model the effective peak arrives within the first ten percent of the step budget, meaning none of them exploit the remaining ninety percent of corrective feedback. One measurement subtlety the paper handles well: small final-best gaps can indicate either genuine preservation or near-total inaction, and the five open-weight models with gaps under zero point zero one have exact-repeat rates of eighty-two to ninety-two percent, so their stability is just repetition.

The semantic tasks draw sharper boundaries than reconstruction does. Gemini three point one Pro outlines a circle at zero point nine one nine Dice and writes hello perfectly, but manages only zero point one eight three on the simple maze and essentially zero on the harder one. GPT-five point five is the inverse in part: perfect score on the simple maze, achieved through the single clearest example of closed-loop self-correction in the whole evaluation, twenty-five undo actions correcting a path before submitting, while Gemini uses undo almost exclusively for maze collision recovery. Claude Opus four point seven fails circle outline entirely with a draw-undo loop that erases its own work, one hundred twenty-four non-maze undos against three maze undos, showing that action-strategy stability is a prerequisite for any cumulative-canvas task. Claude Sonnet almost never uses undo yet posts near-maximal invalid-action rates on mazes, proceeding confidently through constraint violations, a third failure mode. And the harder maze remains unsolved by every model evaluated.

The training probe asks whether supervision can move this needle. EASEL-Data converts stroke-based rendering trajectories into four hundred forty thousand next-action prediction samples through a two-stage curriculum: three hundred eight thousand samples covering early strokes densely, where structure and color get established, then one hundred thirty-two thousand spanning full trajectories for mid-to-late refinement, with ten percent of references additionally annotated with reasoning traces. EASEL-nine B, fine-tuned from Qwen3.5-nine B with LoRA at rank sixty-four on all linear layers, gains twenty-seven thousandths of similarity over its base, a relative six point three percent that ranks it third overall and first among non-proprietary models, and the stage-one curriculum accounts for nearly all the gain while stage-two completion adds only three thousandths. Perception is preserved, with small improvements on standard vision benchmarks, MMVP, POPE, HallusionBench. The honest reading in the paper itself: local action quality improved, long-horizon feedback correction did not, the final-best gap slightly worsened, and the remaining distance to the specialist policy is enormous.

The skeptical read. First, a painting canvas is a proxy, deliberately narrow, and the paper says so: it isolates parameterized visual action from confounds, but competence here does not certify anything about real design tools, medical annotation, or CAD, where the authors see the motivation. Second, reconstruction similarity as a composite metric embeds weighting choices; the ranking-sensitivity analysis is relegated to supplementary material, and edge overlap at these magnitudes, zero point zero five for the best model against zero point five six for the policy, is doing a lot of diagnostic work. Third, the closed-model evaluations are API snapshots with unknown system prompts, and in-context tool documentation heavily conditions agentic behavior; a different harness could shift these numbers, though the uniform failure pattern across twenty-five models suggests the ceiling is real. Fourth, EASEL-nine B is explicitly a diagnostic probe, not a solution: a relative six percent from four hundred forty thousand samples raises the question of whether next-action imitation is the right training signal at all, versus outcome-based reinforcement learning, which the authors flag as future work and which the benchmark's explicit action traces make feasible. What I would trust: the score-anchor methodology, placing model performance between trivial baselines and a specialist policy, which every benchmark should do and almost none do, and the trajectory diagnostics, which convert a single number into a behavioral diagnosis. What I would re-verify: whether the plateau-versus-degrade dichotomy survives different tool schemas, and whether reinforcement learning on the composite reward closes more of the gap than imitation did. One design detail worth stealing for any similar harness: all spatial coordinates are quantized to integers in the zero-to-one-thousand range to align with multimodal tokenization, and strokes composite by alpha blending, so the interface is JSON in, canvas out, cheap enough that a twenty-five-model evaluation stayed practical. Bottom line: the paper finds that multimodal agents can see remarkably well and cannot act precisely, that their closed-loop behavior peaks in the first tenth of an episode and either freezes or backslides, and that the gap between understanding and execution is now measured rather than assumed. Anyone building agents that must place, draw, or manipulate rather than merely click should treat this as the baseline that exposes what click-tolerant benchmarks have been hiding, and the benchmark's own training probe suggests the fastest route forward is outcome-based reinforcement on the composite similarity rather than more imitation data.
