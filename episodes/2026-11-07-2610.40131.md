VideoMemBench, How Well Do MLLMs Remember in Video Conversations, from the University of Technology Sydney, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, and Zhejiang University. The first three authors are Zhengdong Hu, Chao Wang, and Kaituo Feng. The premise is that multimodal large language models can now take in hour-long video, which makes a long multi-turn conversation over a single video a realistic way to use them, and the paper asks whether the model can actually carry information from one turn to the next. That ability, the paper calls in-context video memory, is something no existing video question-answering benchmark measures, because every question in those benchmarks is independent and answerable from the video alone, so nothing in them ever requires the model to remember what it saw or said before.

The distinction the benchmark draws is between two kinds of cross-turn dependency. An episodic dependency refers back to a visual episode that was described in an earlier turn, so it probes memory of what the model has seen. An answer dependency builds on the model's own earlier answer, so it probes memory of what the model has said. The paper is careful about the difference, because a model can remember the video content and still fail to remember its own prior output, or vice versa, and those are different capabilities with different failure modes. Every dependency in the dataset is verified against two properties. Necessity, meaning the question genuinely cannot be answered without the earlier turn, so you are not just testing single-turn video understanding in disguise. And non-leakage, meaning the question does not restate the information from the earlier turn, so the model cannot cheat by re-reading the dependency from the prompt itself.

The construction pipeline uses three language model roles, a planner that decides what dependencies a dialogue should have, a generator that writes the turns, and a judger that checks necessity and non-leakage, and every dialogue is then reviewed by human experts. The result is three hundred and twenty six videos, each paired with a long multi-turn dialogue, totaling two thousand four hundred and seventy six turns. Of those, one thousand three hundred and eighty seven depend on earlier turns, which includes multi-parent dependencies, where a turn depends on more than one prior turn, and long-range call-backs, where the dependency stretches back many turns. The remaining questions are independent, included so the benchmark can measure whether the model is doing well on the dependent turns specifically or just across the board.

The evaluation covers over twenty open and closed source multimodal models. The headline is that the best model tested, Claude Opus 5, achieves an overall score of only forty-five point seven percent. Broken down by dependency type, Opus 5 gets thirty-five point seven percent on the answer-dependent turns, the ones that require remembering its own prior output, and forty-nine point one percent on the episodic-dependent turns. So even the strongest model is far from reliable, and it is measurably worse at remembering what it said than what it saw. The paper also reports that performance declines as the dependency complexity grows, with larger drops the further the call-back stretches, and a consistent decline as the number of parent turns a question depends on increases, falling from the level with one parent to about twenty-nine point eight percent with three parents.

One design decision that separates this from a naive multi-turn benchmark is how the dependency structure is tracked. Each turn carries a structured dependency graph, and the graph distinguishes the two dependency types explicitly, records how many parent turns a turn depends on, and records the distance in turns back to each parent. That structure is what makes the reported slices possible, the split between episodic and answer dependencies, the decay as the number of parents grows, and the decay as the call-back distance grows. Without the typed graph you would have an accuracy number and nothing you could diagnose from it.

A concrete example of the trend. For Gemini 3.8 Flash, dependent-turn accuracy drops from about forty-one percent at the shortest distances to about thirty percent at the longest, and for Qwen3.5-35B it drops more sharply beyond a certain range. For Qwen3.5-27B, dependent-turn accuracy falls from about thirty-six percent early in the dialogue to about twenty-eight percent later, while its independent-turn accuracy declines more modestly from about forty-six to forty percent. So the gap between the dependent and independent turns is not noise, it is a real and growing deficit as the conversation gets longer, which is exactly the regime where in-context video memory is supposed to matter. On the open models, the best dense Qwen3.5 model, the twenty-seven billion variant, scores about thirty-four point three percent overall, and the overall score scales with model size within the family, rising from about sixteen point two percent at zero point eight billion to thirty-four point three at twenty seven billion.

The design choices worth noting for implementation. The necessity and non-leakage verification is what makes this a memory benchmark rather than a harder video benchmark, and the two-property check is the part I would replicate if I built a similar eval for my own modality. The human review over every dialogue is expensive but it is what keeps the dependency graph honest, because a dependency that is only weakly necessary, where the model could answer from the video alone, would silently convert a memory question into a perception question and deflate the scores for the wrong reason. The typed dependency graph, distinguishing episodic from answer dependencies and tracking multi-parent and long-range structure, is also the reusable artifact. That graph is what lets you slice the results by dependency type and distance, which is where the interesting findings, the answer-versus-episodic gap and the distance decay, actually come from.

A skeptical read. The benchmark is well constructed and the finding that even the best model sits at under half on a task that should be well within its nominal capability is the headline that matters. The main caveat is that the numbers are low across the board, which is expected for a deliberately hard memory probe, but it also means the absolute scores are sensitive to the scoring protocol, and a benchmark where the best model is at forty-five percent is one where small changes in how you grade the dependent turns can move the leaderboard noticeably. I would want the per-model breakdown by dependency type and distance to be the primary reported result, not just the overall score, because the overall number hides the fact that the failure is concentrated in the answer-dependent and long-range cases. The other thing I would verify is the video source distribution. The benchmark uses a fixed set of three hundred and twenty six videos, and if those lean toward a particular genre or visual density, the memory scores could be entangled with how well each model handles that specific visual content. The distance-decay result is the most robust finding, because it is a relative trend within the same benchmark, and it is the one that would most directly inform how you design a long-video conversation system, namely that you should expect the model to lose track of earlier turns as the dialogue grows and plan for explicit re-grounding rather than assuming the context window carries it.

The bottom line. VideoMemBench is a clean, well-verified benchmark for a capability, in-context video memory, that existing video question-answering sets never test, and it shows that even the best current multimodal model manages only about forty-six percent overall, with a pronounced gap between remembering what it saw and remembering what it said. If you are building long multi-turn video dialogue, the assumption that a long context window is enough for the model to carry the conversation is not supported by these numbers.
