Surfing the Activation Wave: Analyzing the Information and Geometry of LLM Hidden States for Speculative Decoding, from Raghavv Goel, Sudhanshu Agrawal, and Mukul Gagrani at Qualcomm AI Research and Stanford University. This is a representation-geometry and interpretability paper, and it earns its slot on the show for that reason even though the upvote count is low. The setting is speculative decoding, where a small draft model proposes tokens that a large target model verifies, and methods like EAGLE3 feed the draft model a set of hidden states from the target model to condition on. The problem is how that set is chosen. In practice these layers are picked by simple fixed, position-based heuristics, for example the EAGLE3 rule of taking layers two, the middle layer, and N minus three. There is no explicit objective that balances how informative the selected layers are against how redundant they are with each other, and choosing an optimal subset is fundamentally a combinatorial problem. For a thirty-six-layer model, choosing only three layers gives you thousands of candidate sets, so exhaustive search is off the table.
The analysis is where the paper earns its interpretability slot. Working with Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, the authors collect hidden states across depth and measure two things: informativeness, through matrix entropy, and redundancy, through centered kernel alignment, or CKA. Three findings come out. First, the per-layer Shannon entropy is not flat across depth; it forms a non-uniform profile they call an activation wave, with three distinct regimes, an early region around layers zero to six, a long middle region from roughly seven to twenty-eight, and a late region from twenty-nine to thirty-five. Second, the entropy gain you get from concatenating layers is also non-uniform, which is the signal that some layers add little new information once their neighbors are in. Third, the pairwise CKA plots show clear transition boundaries that mark representational compartments across depth, consistent with the broader literature finding that many middle layers are partially interchangeable while early and late layers stay more functionally distinct. On top of this they add a calibration detail that matters for measurement quality: a token-entropy filter that drops hidden states from low-predictive-entropy tokens, because those uninformative positions inflate cross-prompt entropy variance. With the filter, the cross-prompt entropy variance drops by forty eight point four percent in the early layers, forty five point eight percent in the middle, and thirty five point six percent in the late layers.
The selector is ELSI, Entropy-based Layer Set Iteration. Because the search space is combinatorial, ELSI uses beam search to efficiently identify high-entropy layer sets by evaluating the entropy of the concatenated hidden states, and it scales to realistic model depths. A diversity-aware extension, ELSI-C, uses an entropy-initialized CKA-transition step so that the added layers are not just individually informative but also not redundant with each other. Concretely, at the three-layer size, ELSI picks layers five, twenty-three, and thirty-five for Qwen3-4B with a set entropy of zero point six two seven, versus the EAGLE3 default of layers two, eighteen, and thirty-three at zero point four four one; the same pattern holds on Llama-3.1-8B.
The results are evaluated on SpecBench by mean accepted tokens per forward pass, a metric that directly translates into higher tokens per second for a given number of chosen hidden states. The honest picture is more nuanced than the abstract suggests, and the paper is to its credit about it. On Llama-3.1-8B at greedy temperature, the EAGLE3 default rule actually posts the best average at four point six four accepted tokens, while ELSI at three layers comes in at four point five nine and the single-layer ELSI variant at four point five eight. So the learned selection does not uniformly beat the fixed heuristic. Where ELSI does clearly win is at higher temperatures and with the diversity-aware variant, where the selected sets separate more cleanly from the default, and the paper frames its contribution accordingly: the selected sets outperform the fixed baseline choices in the harder sampling regime, and more importantly, the analysis gives a mechanistic explanation for why a simple default like layer two, the middle, and N minus three can still be competitive, namely that it happens to span the early, middle, and late compartments, so it captures complementary information even without being optimized. The selected sets do land in different compartments than the default, which is the explanation for the EAGLE3-over-EAGLE2 improvement the paper walks through.
The beam-search mechanics are the part that makes the selector practical rather than a toy. A naive exhaustive search over the combinatorial space is infeasible, so ELSI grows candidate layer sets incrementally: it starts from the single most informative layer, measured by the entropy of its hidden state, and at each step extends the set by adding the layer that maximizes the entropy of the concatenated hidden states, keeping only the best few partial sets at each depth to control the search. The entropy of the concatenation, rather than the sum of individual entropies, is the key design choice, because it is the quantity that penalizes redundancy: if a candidate layer is already well-predicted from the layers in the set, adding it barely raises the joint entropy, and the beam search naturally discards it. That is how the informativeness criterion and the redundancy criterion get unified into a single objective without a hand-set trade-off weight. The diversity-aware extension, ELSI-C, layers the CKA transition structure on top of this entropy initialization: instead of only asking which layer adds the most new information, it also asks whether that layer sits in a representational compartment that the current set has not yet sampled, so the final three-layer set is pushed to span the early, middle, and late regions of the activation wave rather than clustering in the most entropic part of depth. This is why the selected sets land in different compartments than the fixed default, and it is the mechanism behind the EAGLE3-over-EAGLE2 improvement the paper walks through, since EAGLE3's default of layers two, middle, and N minus three happens to already span those compartments, which is why it remains competitive even though it was never optimized. The beam search itself is cheap relative to the models it selects for: the entropy evaluations are computed on the target model's cached hidden states rather than on fresh forward passes, so the calibration is a one-time analysis pass over the model's activations, and once the selected layer set is fixed the speculative-decoding loop runs exactly as it would with any hand-chosen set, with no per-token change to the serving path.
The skeptical read. First, the headline gain in accepted tokens over the strongest fixed baseline is small and, at greedy decoding, the fixed rule wins, so the practical upside of adopting ELSI is modest and regime-dependent, and the paper does not report wall-clock speedup or the cost of the beam-search calibration step, which is a one-time overhead but a real one. Second, the evaluation is on two mid-size instruct models, Qwen3-4B and Llama-3.1-8B, and the combinatorial-selection argument gets stronger, not weaker, at frontier scale, so the method's value proposition is actually untested where it would matter most. Third, the entropy and CKA analyses are clean and reproducible in principle, but the compartment boundaries are model-specific, so the recipe is a calibration procedure rather than a transferable rule, and you re-run it per model. Bottom line: the durable contribution is the analysis, the activation wave, the CKA compartments, and the token-entropy filtering, which together give a principled account of why layer selection for speculative decoding works the way it does. The ELSI selector itself is a reasonable, if incremental, tool that wins in the harder sampling regime and explains the default's competitiveness, and that explanation is the part I would actually reuse. I would want a frontier-scale evaluation and a calibration-cost breakdown before calling it a production replacement for the fixed rule.
