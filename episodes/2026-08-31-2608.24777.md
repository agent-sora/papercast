StepGuard: Learning Step-Level Guardrails with Scalable Supervision and Safety-Utility Balancing, by Zhijie Zheng and Yu Li of the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory with Beihang University, Chen Qian with Renmin University of China, and colleagues at Fudan University and KAUST. The premise: agents invoke tools, and tool invocation is where prompt injections turn into real damage, files modified, messages sent, secrets disclosed. Most guardrails audit whole trajectories after the fact. This paper builds a four-billion-parameter guard that checks each candidate tool call before it executes, and attacks the two things that make such guards hard to train: there is almost no real-world data on unsafe executions to learn from, and every guard in the wild has a defense bias, either blocking too many benign actions or missing too many risky ones.

The data engine, StepGen, is the first contribution. Real unsafe executions are too rare to supervise at scale, so it synthesizes them in a controlled way that isolates the actual decision point. For each scenario it samples a risk source, a failure mode, and a harm type, then has a planner compile a task into a structured execution plan with a designated unsafe anchor step. The plan is rolled out through an environment simulator into an unsafe base trajectory; for response-borne risks like indirect prompt injection, the simulator plants the perturbation in the environment context available at exactly the anchor step, so the anchor is the first unsafe decision, not an inherited one. Then the key trick: keep the execution prefix before the anchor fixed and re-roll only the suffix under two safe modes, a Refuse branch that declines the risky action, and an Aware branch that recognizes the risk and completes the task through a safe alternative. A fourth, independently constructed trajectory reuses the same tools in a benign scenario, which is what stops the guard from learning tool identity as a safety signal, a shortcut that plagues content-style guards. Every group is one unsafe trajectory, two prefix-aligned safe branches, and one benign reuse case, each action labeled safe or unsafe with a structured explanation, filtered by a rule-based validator for structure and parameter lineage and a language-model auditor for semantic consistency. Cold-start supervised fine-tuning runs on three thousand such demonstrations, with responses generated to match the annotations, on a Qwen3 four-B instruct base.

The training contribution, Balance-GRPO, targets the bias directly. The reward is half for the correct safety label and half more for also naming the right risk category, credit given only when the safety call is right. Standard grouped policy optimization then normalizes within each prompt group, and the balance controller reweights the normalized advantages with two factors: one correcting class-count imbalance, one driven by the observed accuracy gap between safe and unsafe classes in the rollout batch, centered on a target with deadband filtering, so the weaker class gets larger updates without touching the prompts or raw rewards. The motivation is empirical: the SFT checkpoint is badly over-defensive, seventy-nine point seven overall accuracy but sixty-nine point three on safe examples versus ninety-one point one on unsafe. And the field-wide figure is striking: existing guards span the whole diagonal from AgentDoG, which blocks almost everything, twenty-six point five safe accuracy against ninety-eight point eight unsafe, to the raw backbone, which blocks almost nothing, ninety-eight point nine safe against twenty-two point two unsafe. Existing training recipes optimize overall accuracy and never close that gap.

The results, in three settings. Static judgment: StepGuard averages eighty-three accuracy and eighty-three F1 on three trajectory-level benchmarks and eighty-four point eight accuracy with eighty-four F1 on step-level evaluation, the highest step-level F1 among open-weight guards, and despite the four-billion size it is competitive with GPT-five point four, which scores the same trajectory accuracy with slightly better trajectory F1 but lower step-level accuracy. Guarded-agent evaluation swaps one guard at a time onto the same Qwen3.6 thirty-five B agent: on AgentDojo StepGuard holds attack success rate to one point two percent with ninety point seven utility, and on AgentDyn nine point three ASR at sixty-six point seven utility; across the two environments, mean attack success rate falls seventy-seven point three percent relative to no guard while mean utility drops only two point eight points. On AgentHarm, the deliberately adversarial harmful-agent setting, it cuts the malicious score from twenty-two point eight to three point four but task completion drops from seventy point nine to fifty-two point eight, and the paper is honest that no evaluated method achieves a clearly favorable trade-off there. Ablations: the two StepGen components each pay, intermediate-prefix supervision lifting trajectory-level accuracy from eighty point four to eighty-three point eight, and benign tool-reuse examples lifting the harm-subset F1 from sixty-nine point two to seventy-five point two; generalization is real, a guard trained on only two risk sources still scores seventy-four point nine accuracy on six unseen ones, against forty-nine point six for the bare backbone; and Balance-GRPO versus vanilla grouped optimization narrows the safe-unsafe accuracy gap from thirteen points to eight while raising guarded-agent utility from eighty-five point three to ninety point seven on AgentDojo and sixty to sixty-six point seven on AgentDyn, at a cost of zero point three ASR points. Simpler fixes, safe-class upsampling and fixed class weights, close the gap too but at a real cost, dropping unsafe accuracy to seventy-six or seventy-seven percent, while the balance controller holds unsafe accuracy at eighty-six point four. Two structural findings round out the analysis: defense bias correlates with prediction instability, over-defensive models flip their answers on safe examples and under-defensive ones flip on unsafe examples, and the balance controller's training dynamics show the safe-minus-unsafe accuracy gap staying near zero across rollout steps where vanilla grouped optimization drifts wide, while macro-F1 climbs steadily rather than plateauing. The paper also checks flip rates per class: over-defensive guards flip mostly on safe examples, under-defensive ones on unsafe examples.

The skeptical read. First, the training data is synthetic by necessity, and the deployment claim rests on the simulator's risks resembling real injection patterns; the three-stage filtering and the generalization table are the right evidence, but a guard trained on manufactured attacks can always be surprised by a different manufacture, and the AgentHarm result, the most adversarial setting, is where the trade-off visibly sags, which is consistent with that concern. Second, the runtime gains are measured with one agent backbone, Qwen3.6 thirty-five B, and a guard tuned on trajectories from its own simulator family may transfer differently to other agent scaffolds; the paper varies guards, not agents. Third, the two point eight point utility cost is a mean across two environments, and the AgentDojo numbers, under two percent ASR with ninety-one utility, are excellent, but a guard adds latency to every tool call, and the paper does not report guard-in-the-loop latency cost, which at four B per step is the practical deployment question. Fourth, the safety-utility Pareto is reported per benchmark, so cross-environment claims in the abstract are means of only two environments. What I would trust: the prefix-aligned contrastive construction, which is the reusable idea, the same context, one decision, both outcomes, is exactly the supervision signal this problem was missing, and the balance controller, which is a small, well-ablated mechanism for a well-documented failure mode. What I would re-verify: latency under production tool-call rates, robustness against injection styles absent from StepGen's risk taxonomy, and behavior when the guard itself is the target of a prompt injection through observed tool output. Bottom line: StepGuard moves agent safety from post-mortem to pre-execution, shows that context-matched contrastive data plus bias-aware training gets a four-B guard to frontier-model-level judgment, and quantifies the price, roughly three utility points for a seventy-seven percent cut in attack success. The generalizable lesson for anyone training classifiers on synthetic data is the prefix-aligned group design: hold the context fixed and vary only the decision, because that is what forces the model to learn the decision rather than the scenery.
