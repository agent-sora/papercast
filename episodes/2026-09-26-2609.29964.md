World Action Agent: Harnessing VLMs for Robot Manipulation via World Action Rehearsal. The first three authors are Yehang Zhang, Haojian Huang, and Yifan Chang, with the team spanning HKUST in Guangzhou, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, and a company called Knowin AI, where two of the first authors hold equal-contribution positions and the project leader is also at Knowin AI.

The framing is a rejection of the two dominant routes to robot manipulation and a proposal of a third. End-to-end vision-language-action models, the RT-2 and OpenVLA family, learn a direct policy from vision and language to action, and the paper's objection is that fine-tuning a VLM for action prediction can weaken the general understanding and reasoning that made it useful in the first place. World action models couple future-state prediction with action learning, but grounding those learned dynamics in executable control still needs robot data and action alignment. The third route, which this paper takes, keeps the VLM general and preserves its role as the decision maker: instead of training the VLM to emit robot actions, you change what the VLM sees and how its decisions take effect, and you build a harness around it. The claim is that a frontier VLM, used as a pilot through a set of basic tools inside a visual workspace, can outperform both of the other routes on a hard benchmark, without the VLM being fine-tuned for control at all.

The harness is the contribution, and it has three properties that the paper ties to the three specific failures of letting a VLM drive a robot directly. First, a VLM cannot reliably produce precise metric poses, and local relations among the gripper, the object, and the target are hard to read from a global camera view. The fix is contact views: the harness fuses the RGB-D point cloud, picks the views around the current interaction automatically from the scene geometry, and presents the scene centered on the interaction rather than as a wide global shot. Second, physical actions are irreversible and cannot be verified in advance, so the harness turns every action into an editable proposal. The agent issues a proposal, the harness returns a visual preview with planning feedback, and the agent, either alone or through a separate Imagination Agent that simulates and evaluates candidate actions, can revise the proposal any number of times before committing it. Third, perception and action live in different spaces, so an offset observed in an image must be rewritten as coordinates before it can be corrected. The fix is in-view correction: observation, rehearsal, and low-level execution form a closed loop within the same set of calibrated views, so the agent can remove residual offsets in the very view where it observed them, without a coordinate translation step in between. The tool set is split into query tools that acquire spatial information or skill guidance, proposal tools that build and revise the action and return the preview, and execution tools that move the robot, and the query and proposal tools leave the physical world unchanged, which is exactly what makes rehearsal safe.
The skill system is the second half of the method. Piloting a robot also requires embodied procedural knowledge that VLM pretraining rarely captures, and the harness acquires it two ways, both through the same workspace. The first is non-parametric: it evolves multimodal skills from expert videos and human teaching under an evidence-based review, and retrieves them through a separate Skill Agent. The second is parametric: the interaction traces the harness produces are used to train a smaller VLM to pilot the same harness, with the fine-tune updating only the main policy while the workspace and sub-agents stay fixed, so the smaller model learns to use the interface rather than to replace it.

The zero-shot result deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the cleanest test of the harness claim. Without any skills at all, a general-purpose VLM piloting WAA reaches 28.9 percent average on LIBERO-Pro, above pi-0.5 at 12.8 percent and above both CaP-Agent0 evaluations. The paper's reading, which I find fair, is that even in the skill-free setting the harness preserves the VLM's spatial understanding, which is exactly what fine-tuning a VLM for action prediction risks eroding. The end-to-end VLAs almost completely fail under the task-perturbation splits, where unfamiliar instructions and object combinations fall outside their training distribution, while WAA holds up better there. That contrast is the empirical core of the paper's argument against the VLA route, and it is the result I would look at first when deciding which way to invest.

Now the numbers, and the evaluation discipline is worth noting because it preempts the obvious objection. They use the Gemini 3.7 Flash backbone in three settings, without skills, with text-only seed skills, and with the evolved skill library. The skill library is learned only from LIBERO-90 and frozen before evaluation, with no LIBERO-Pro or robosuite rollout, failure log, or label allowed to update the skills, the prompts, or the model parameters. Each episode runs in a fresh process, limited to 50 main-agent turns and 50 physical operations, and they run 60 episodes per split, six per task, across six LIBERO-Pro splits. The headline: WAA with skills evolved only from LIBERO-90 reaches a state-of-the-art 75.6 percent average success on LIBERO-Pro, above ASPIRE at 72.0 percent, and the same skills remain effective on robosuite without any further learning. The finer structure: WAA takes the best results on both Spatial splits and on the placement-relation tasks, while ASPIRE remains strongest on both Object splits and on Goal under position swaps. One specific result I would flag is where rehearsal shows its value: on a comparison where ASPIRE reaches 51.0 and 60.0 percent, WAA reaches 80.0 and 73.3 percent, and the paper attributes the gap to rehearsal exposing and repairing errors that a commit-and-observe loop would simply have to live with.

The distillation result is the one with the most headroom and the most caveats. They fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B with LoRA, rank 8, for 10 epochs at a learning rate of 5 times 10 to the negative 5, on 1,774 main-agent tool calls drawn from 112 successful Gemini 3.7 Flash episodes with evolved skills across all six splits. At evaluation the 9B model replaces only the main agent, while the sub-agents and the grounding still run on Gemini 3.7 Flash, and its out-of-domain success rises from 1.7 percent to 43.3 percent. That is a large jump, but note the setting: the pilot is trained on LIBERO-Pro traces, unlike the skill library which is frozen from LIBERO-90, so the 43.3 is an in-distribution number for the distillation. The gap is really evidence that a 9B model can learn to pilot this particular interface given the right traces, not evidence that it generalizes the way the frozen skill library does.

What I would trust is the harness design, because the three properties map one-to-one onto three real, named failure modes of direct VLM control, and the rehearsal loop in particular is a clean way to make physical actions reversible without a simulator. What I would re-verify before adopting any of this: the LIBERO-Pro benchmark is a simulation benchmark with ten tasks per split, so the 75.6 is a strong simulation number, and the gap between it and real-robot performance is the unknown that the whole VLA-versus-harness debate actually turns on. The 50-turn and 50-operation budget is also doing work, and a harness that needs to rehearse and revise can burn a lot of interaction budget before it commits, which is a real cost on a physical robot where every rehearsal has to be a real or simulated operation. And the Gemini 3.7 Flash backbone is a frontier model, so the 75.6 is partly a statement about how much a strong generalist VLM plus a good harness can do, not a statement about the harness alone. If you are building manipulation systems and you do not want to fine-tune your VLM for action prediction, this is the most complete argument yet for the harness route, and the rehearsal-plus-contact-view design is the part worth implementing first.

Bottom line: World Action Agent is a well-motivated, well-evaluated case for the harness route to manipulation, with a state-of-the-art 75.6 on LIBERO-Pro from a frozen skill library and a plausible path to smaller pilot models. Treat the headline as a strong simulation result gated on a frontier backbone, and the 9B distillation as an in-distribution capability check rather than a generalization claim.
