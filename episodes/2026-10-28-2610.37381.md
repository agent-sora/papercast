PDRVLA: A Vision-Language-Action Model with Physical Dynamics Representations, by Hanqing Wang, Zhaotian Cai, and Ruichuan An, from Peking University and JD.com, with the remaining co-authors split across the same two institutions and several marked with equal contribution. The paper comes from a robotics group that has a real hardware and data pipeline behind it, which is the kind of paper where the benchmark numbers are at least partly reproducible because the data is public.

The problem is that vision-language-action models generate robot actions from visual observations and language instructions, but their internal understanding of physical dynamics is thin. The obvious fix is to lean on a video world model to predict the future, but that introduces a latency problem: generating future video frames on the fly inside the control loop is too slow for real-time robot control. PDRVLA's answer is a lightweight physical dynamics module that extracts physical tokens from the vision-language features and conditions action generation on those tokens, without doing any online video generation at inference time. The module is trained to predict future RGB, depth, and optical-flow features directly, and it gets an additional auxiliary supervision signal from a frozen pretrained video world model. That is the core trade-off: the world model is used only at training time to provide a supervision target, and at inference the module is small enough to run in the loop.

The architecture has three parts worth separating. The first is the vision-language model backbone, which produces the final visual-linguistic features. The second is the physical dynamics module, which takes those final features plus the robot state and produces physical tokens through a set of learnable queries. The module is deliberately small, about fifty seven point three million parameters, which is one point four percent of a four-billion-parameter backbone, so the inference overhead is minimal. It uses four attention heads and a feed-forward expansion ratio of one, and a final projection maps the query outputs back into the space the action expert consumes. The third part is the action expert, a small diffusion-style model of about one hundred fifty million parameters that regresses the action chunk. At inference the chunk is initialized with Gaussian noise and denoised in four Euler steps. So the whole action head is a four-step diffusion over the action vector, conditioned on the physical tokens, and that is the part that has to be fast.

The training has two stages, and the split is the interesting design choice. Stage one is physical dynamics pretraining. The module predicts future RGB, depth, and flow features with a direct mean-squared-error loss, and on top of that it gets an auxiliary supervision signal: the predicted future RGB latent clip is fed through a frozen video world model, and the gradient of the world model's own prediction target is back-propagated into the module. So the module is being trained to produce features that a strong video model would find plausible, even though it never generates the video itself. Stage two is the action policy training, where the action expert is trained to regress actions, with the physical tokens as conditioning. The data for both stages comes entirely from public sources, expanded through human-to-robot synthesis, subtask annotation, and what they call embodied chain-of-thought annotation, or ECoT.

The data scale is worth stating because it is the reason the benchmark comparisons are credible. After source-specific filtering the public sources contribute about one point two two million episodes and ten thousand two hundred fifty nine hours. The subtask annotation covers roughly one point eight eight million episodes, one point seven nine billion frames, and twenty thousand one hundred one hours. They automatically annotate about seventy eight million sampled frames, which is four point four percent of the total, and from that they build about one point one million single-turn vision-language examples across twenty two data families. That is a substantial corpus, and it means the model is not being tuned on a single benchmark's task distribution.

On the results. Across four simulation benchmarks, PDRVLA reaches a success rate of ninety nine point three percent on LIBERO, eighty nine point nine percent on LIBERO-Plus, ninety three point six percent on RoboTwin version two, and eighty one point five percent on SimplerEnv. On LIBERO it is the highest average among the compared methods, ahead of a Qwen robot-manipulation model at ninety nine point one percent and an InternVLA model at ninety eight point nine percent. It hits one hundred percent on the spatial subset and ninety nine point eight on object and goal, but on the long-horizon suite it is at ninety seven point six percent, below a LingBot-VA model at ninety eight point five percent, so it is not a uniform win. On LIBERO-Plus, which perturbs the initial state, it leads at eighty nine point nine against eighty nine point zero and eighty four point eight. On SimplerEnv its strongest single result is on a transfer task, ninety seven point six percent, but on another it drops to sixty four point six percent, below the InternVLA model's eighty five point four percent, so the per-task variance is real.

The ablations are where the method's claim is tested. The physical representation learning ablation compares direct prediction only against adding the world-model auxiliary supervision. Adding the auxiliary supervision reduces the mean normalized prediction error on held-out clips from zero point six four three nine to zero point six three five four, a one point three two percent relative reduction, with the largest gain in RGB at four point zero six percent and depth at two point one two percent, while the flow reduction is only zero point six two percent. Critically, the flow reduction's ninety five percent paired-episode bootstrap interval includes zero, so the flow improvement is not statistically significant at the confidence level they report. That is an honest negative result, and it tells you the auxiliary supervision is helping the appearance and geometry prediction more than the motion prediction. The subtask-conditioning ablation raises overall LIBERO-Plus success from eighty nine point nine three to ninety zero point two three, with the largest gains under robot-initial-state and camera-viewpoint perturbations, plus one point one six and one point zero zero percentage points.

Here is the skeptical read. The headline success rates are high, but they are on simulation benchmarks, and the long-horizon and per-task results show that the wins are not uniform, so the average is doing some work. The auxiliary supervision effect on motion prediction is not statistically significant, which undercuts the strongest mechanistic claim, that the module is learning real physical dynamics rather than just better appearance prediction. The data pipeline is large and public, which is a real strength, but the human-to-robot synthesis and the ECoT annotation are the authors' own constructions, and the per-task results on SimplerEnv show that the synthesis quality varies a lot across task types. The four-step diffusion action head is a design choice that trades off action precision for inference speed, and the paper does not ablate the number of denoising steps against success rate, so you cannot tell whether four steps is the right number or just the fastest one that still passes the benchmarks. The module being one point four percent of the backbone is a good thing for latency, but it also means the physical tokens are a low-dimensional bottleneck, and the flow prediction weakness is consistent with that bottleneck being too small to carry the motion information.

The bottom line: this is a well-engineered robotics paper with a clean latency argument, the world model at training time and a tiny module at inference time, and the public-data corpus makes the simulation results more credible than the average of the numbers suggests. The caveat is that the motion-prediction improvement from the auxiliary supervision is not statistically significant, and the per-task variance shows the wins are concentrated, so I would re-verify the long-horizon and SimplerEnv results on your own hardware before trusting the average. If you are adding physical grounding to a VLA and you need the control loop to stay real-time, the frozen-world-model-at-training plus lightweight-physical-tokens-at-inference split is a concrete architecture worth stealing, even if you replace the diffusion action head with something simpler.
