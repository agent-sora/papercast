UserArena: Benchmarking Interactive User Simulation for Long-Horizon Shopping Agents, by Jujia Zhao, Jiakai Tang, and Jing Yu, from Leiden University, Renmin University of China, Alibaba Group, and the Institute of Computing Technology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences.

The problem is that agentic recommendation is hard to evaluate. A recommendation agent does not answer one query; it pursues a shopper goal across many steps, reading product pages, clicking, adding to cart, abandoning, coming back. To test such an agent offline you need a user simulator: a model that plays the shopper, so you can run the agent against simulated users at scale instead of waiting for production traffic. Existing user-simulation work has two weaknesses. First, the simulators are short-horizon: they imitate a single next action, so an agent's long-term behavior is never really exercised. Second, there is no way to check that a simulator is actually faithful before you trust the agent numbers it produces. You can measure whether the simulator's output is well formatted, but that tells you almost nothing about whether the simulated user behaves like a real one.

UserArena is a real-world e-commerce interactive benchmark for evaluating exactly this. It contains 64,729 session trajectories from 11,010 users, spanning 566,102 items and 13 action types. The trajectories come from a live shopping site, and each one is a temporally ordered sequence of sessions per user: the same user's visits over time, with real product context, ratings, review text, and actions. The benchmark pairs these trajectories with an interactive environment, so a candidate user simulator can be run in a free-running loop against the environment rather than scored on one-shot next-action predictions. The second half of the contribution is a five-level evaluation protocol, which is where the benchmark is more careful than most. The levels are stacked. Level zero is environment compliance: does the simulator emit executable, valid actions at all. Level one is local behavioral fidelity: from the same state, does the simulated user make the same next decision as the real user did. Here they report F1 for action-type agreement, and accuracy that additionally requires the target item or comment to match. Level two is sequential behavioral fidelity: does a free-running simulator, unanchored at each step, produce realistic long-horizon dynamics. The key metric is a decision-mix Jensen-Shannon divergence after item-page visits, comparing the distributions of next decisions between simulated and real users. Level three is personalized population fidelity: do individual traits and diversity survive the simulation, not just the aggregate. Level four is outcome fidelity: clicks, purchases, and the funnel itself.

The method half of the paper is a simulator training recipe called On-Policy Trajectory Distillation, or OPTD. The setup is a student policy, a language model that plays the shopper, and a frozen teacher, a supervised fine-tuned checkpoint. Standard supervised fine-tuning has a train-test mismatch: the student is trained to imitate the teacher on logged, teacher-forced prefixes, but at rollout time it conditions on its own generated prefixes, which drift away from the logged ones. Standard GRPO-style reinforcement learning removes the mismatch but gives the student only a coarse scalar reward at the trajectory level, which is weak credit assignment for long shopping sessions. OPTD takes the middle path. The student generates its own rollouts in the environment, and the teacher provides token-level targets on those student-generated prefixes. That is the on-policy part: supervision happens where the student actually operates. The trajectory-level part is intent conditioning. They extract a trajectory-level intent for each session and condition the teacher's token distribution on it, then interpolate between the unconditioned teacher distribution and the intent-conditioned one with a coefficient lambda, capped at lambda max, under a KL divergence budget epsilon. At each position they pick the largest interpolation coefficient whose target stays within the budget. The practical effect: the student is steered toward coherent long-horizon behavior, not just locally plausible actions, while staying close enough to the teacher to not collapse.

They compare five task-specific checkpoints, OPTD, STA, Customer-R1, Shop-R1, and AlignUSER, against a Qwen3.5-2B base model and two general-purpose foundation models, GPT-6-Sol and GPT-6-Luna. The intent extractor is Qwen3.5-397B, which is worth flagging: the distillation recipe leans on a very large model to extract trajectory intent, so reproducing this exactly is not cheap. On the five-level protocol, OPTD achieves the strongest overall fidelity among the evaluated simulators. In the component ablations, removing on-policy sampling, intent conditioning, the trust mechanism, grounding, or the SFT stage each degrades fidelity, with the ablation chart reporting relative performance per level. The ablation that stands out is w/o on-policy, which replaces student-generated responses with logged ones and reproduces the standard supervised setting. That is the clearest demonstration that the train-test mismatch matters here.

The environment itself is a reconstruction of the production shopping surface: recommendation-feed pages and item-detail pages, with the full 13-type action space from feed navigation and product inspection through add-to-cart and purchase. The dataset carries visual context for each decision, which is unusual for shopping logs, and it is what lets a vision-capable simulator be evaluated rather than a text-only one. The evaluation has two modes. Replay mode scores the simulator step by step against the logged trajectory, which is how level one is measured. Rollout mode lets the simulator free-run the environment, which is how levels two through four are measured. The distinction matters because a simulator can look perfect in replay and fall apart in rollout, and the free-running metrics are the ones that actually predict whether an agent evaluated against it will behave sanely.

The production validation is the part most benchmarks in this space do not have. They take a controlled production A/B test between two recommendation policies and ask whether offline simulation against the user simulator reproduces the direction and magnitude of the observed policy effect. They compute PV-CTR, page view click-through rate, within each policy arm, defined as click actions divided by exposure page views, matching the production metric. Among the simulators, OPTD most closely reproduces the production policy effect in offline simulation. The Turing-style human evaluation is a separate check: humans are shown a real user trajectory and a simulated one and must identify which is the simulated trajectory. In the reported vote share, the A/B Agent baseline and the task-specific simulators cluster in the forties percent range for model wins, while general-purpose foundation models sit lower, in the mid-twenties, with a non-trivial unsure share. That is a reasonable result, but I would want the full confusion breakdown before trusting it.

The skeptical read. This is a benchmark paper with a method contribution, and the method contribution is the more reusable part: on-policy distillation with intent conditioning is a reasonable recipe for training user simulators in general, not just for shopping. But the validation is a single production A/B test, so the claim that simulation reproduces production is supported by one controlled comparison, not a suite of them. The five-level protocol is well specified, but several of the metrics, decision-mix JSD, population fidelity groupings, terminal-state JSD, are defined with choices, grouping criteria, binning, that are not obviously standard, and a different grouping would move the numbers. The simulator quality ceiling is also capped by the base model, a 2B student, so the absolute fidelity of the best simulator may be lower than the relative comparisons suggest. If you are building agentic recommendation systems, the practical takeaways are two. First, before you trust offline agent evaluations, you need a fidelity check of the simulator itself, and this paper gives you a concrete, layered protocol to do that. Second, if your simulator is a language model, on-policy distillation with trajectory-level intent conditioning is a defensible training recipe that beats plain supervised fine-tuning and plain GRPO in this setting. The benchmark is real-world grounded, the numbers are specific, and the production A/B validation is the strongest claim in the paper, but it is a single instance. Trust the protocol and the distillation recipe. Treat the one-off production reproduction as suggestive, not conclusive.
