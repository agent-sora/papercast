AVA-Encoder: Towards Agent-Native Video Representation Learning. First authors Chuyue Li, Jinpeng Yu, and Haozhe Wang, from the Qwen Business Unit of Alibaba, ShanghaiTech University, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, the Institute of Computing Technology, and Southeast University. The problem: video creation agents cannot learn filmmaking from finished films, because a rendered video is not an operable record of the decisions that produced it—scripts, characters, shots, camera language, pacing, and audio design are coupled across space and time in ways the finished pixels do not expose. The proposal is to turn films into structured knowledge graphs that agents can read, query, edit, and reconstruct from—and to use reconstruction error itself as the training signal.

The task formulation is an agentic auto-encoder. Three parts. An Agentic Video Encoder governed by a policy implemented as three distinct system prompts covering film-level, shot-level, and keyframe-level understanding, with inter-level context injection propagating information across granularities. A bottleneck that is not a neural embedding but a Film Knowledge Graph. And a fixed decoder: a static rendering pipeline calling frozen generation models as tools, taking reference keyframes and shot-prompt descriptions from the graph and rendering shots sequentially. Because the decoder never changes, any difference in reconstruction quality measures the representation. That is the entire evaluation philosophy—reconstruction fidelity as a proxy for representation faithfulness.

The encoder pipeline runs three steps per video: preprocessing partitions frames into temporally ordered cinematic shots; hierarchical understanding produces film-, shot-, and keyframe-level analyses under the three prompts, with inter-level context injection feeding higher-level summaries downward so keyframe analysis inherits shot context; assembly maps all records deterministically into the fixed graph schema with no additional model calls.

The Film KG schema is worth studying if you build agent-facing representations. Ten node types: nine structured-text nodes plus keyframe nodes. The text side holds the narrative hierarchy—Story, Event, Shot—plus six shot-level state nodes: Character, Scene, Object, Style, Camera, and Audio, with entity variants represented inside character or scene attributes. Eleven edge types in three groups: production edges—Contains for hierarchy, Binds connecting shots to their states, References linking keyframes to assets; temporal edges—Transition, Sequence, Jump preserving entity-state transitions, temporal order, and non-adjacent recurrences; semantic edges—SpokenBy, Rel, Similar, Features, Narrative capturing speakers, entity relations, visual similarity, scene-entity associations, and narrative dependencies like cause, setup, and callback. A keyframe node stores its textual description plus an image-generation payload, with the generated image connected through an asset-reference link; an Audio state describes spoken content, speaker identity, voice properties, music, effects, and audiovisual synchronization as text. One rule does heavy lifting: no frame, crop, or screenshot from the source film may enter the asset layer or reach the generators. Every asset must be regenerated from structured text. That forces the graph, not pixels, to carry all cinematic information—and it makes the graph directly editable through typed dependencies.

The optimization is dual-loop textual-gradient evolution, TextGrad-style but staged. Outer loop, data-agnostic encoding policy pseudo-training: across training videos, reconstruction failures are diagnosed into textual gradients—each gradient being natural-language feedback describing both the error and the requested revision—an evaluation item being one atomic source-grounded fact checked against a reconstruction and marked failed when missing or contradicted. Candidate revisions to the shot- and keyframe-level prompts are proposed by one model, scored by another, and accepted corrections accumulate into transferable encoding rules, with an anti-forgetting gate rejecting updates that regress previously processed clips. Inner loop, data-dependent refinement at test time: same gated cycle applied only to the current video's graph instance, guarded by an anti-degradation gate preventing gains on one scoring dimension from silently degrading others. The loops optimize different objects—the shared policy versus the per-video graph—are not nested, and can run independently or sequentially.

Experimental setup is small-scale and fully disclosed, which I appreciate even as it limits conclusions. Six clips for pseudo-training, eighteen non-overlapping evaluation clips spanning animation, human-directed AI shorts, and classic cinema, all open-source. Frozen models in five roles: Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview for both video understanding under the encoder prompts and for two separated evaluation protocols, optimization-reward versus final-evaluation; Qwen 3.7 Plus as the modification model—which notably never scores its own revisions; Nano Banana Pro for images; HappyHorse 1.0 for reference-to-video generation. Fairness protocol: every compared representation gets the identical interface to the fixed decoder—at most five newly generated reference keyframes and 1,200 shot-prompt tokens per shot—with source material available during representation construction but never passed to generators. Baselines VideoAnalyzer, Storyboard Studio, and soap2soap are adapted onto this shared budget. Baseline adaptation details are specified for fairness: identical input test videos, a shared fixed decoder, common source-pixel restriction—no baseline may feed source frames to its generators either—plus temporal alignment before comparison. Evaluation runs four comparison directions—direct video comparison, direct keyframe comparison, video back-captioning, keyframe back-captioning—each scored over eight dimensions including Character, Scene, Position, Motion, Audio, Camera, and Narrative, with audio dropped from keyframe-based directions. Crucially, the vision-language model produces factual QA judgments and checklist items under frozen prompts, and deterministic machine rules—not model opinion—aggregate those into final direction and overall scores.

Headline result: AVA-Encoder reaches 49.0 percent overall against 28.3 percent for soap2soap, the strongest external baseline. Per direction: 57.8 versus 36.7 on direct video, 73.7 versus 39.5 on keyframes, 29.7 versus 15.8 on back-captioned video, 34.6 versus 21.3 on back-captioned keyframes. That is a 20.7-point absolute overall gap, 73.1 percent relative. Large—but remember all systems share the same decoder, so this cleanly measures representation quality rather than generation skill, which is exactly what they intended to isolate.

The ablation table answers where the points come from. A naive single-level encoder manages 27.5 overall; hierarchical understanding alone lifts it to 42.4, an 18.3-point gain, so structure dominates everything else. Adding inner-loop graph refinement brings 45.4; policy pseudo-training instead brings 45.8; both loops together reach 49.0, a combined gain of 6.6 points over neither, with each loop contributing independently in both controlled orderings. Removing acceptance gates costs 5.5 points, dropping performance to 43.5—ungated self-improvement actively damages representations through cross-dimension regressions and historical forgetting. The sharpest finding for anyone writing system prompts by hand: the pseudo-trained policy beats an independently human-tuned policy 45.8 versus 44.4 while cutting the shot-level prompt from 31,336 to 8,052 tokens—a 74.3 percent reduction—and the keyframe-level prompt from 13,574 to 4,062, a 70.1 percent cut. Automated prompt evolution produced shorter prompts that outperform expert curation. That is a small margin on eighteen clips, so treat the direction, not the delta, as the finding.

Operability results are qualitative but concrete: identity replacement edits propagate through dependent shots consistently—change a registered character and the affected keyframes, dialogue, actions, and appearance update while unrelated characters, scenes, compositions, and event structure stay intact—with the affected subgraph computed from typed dependencies and re-rendered without rewriting unrelated shots. The registry description of a replacement character can be filled manually or completed automatically by an LLM before propagation. Visual-treatment edits follow style-state transitions similarly, applying consistent treatment across the linked sequence. Downstream reuse feeds the complete representation as plain text into four external frameworks—MovieAgent, FilmAgent, Anim-Director, VideoStudio—with only a one-sentence request to refer to it and no adapters or prompt tuning; every framework improves on every dimension, MovieAgent rising from 2.47 to 3.30 overall on a five-point scale and FilmAgent from 1.85 to 2.83.

Limitations deserve emphasis because the framing invites over-reading. Eighteen evaluation clips and six training clips is tiny, with no confidence intervals on headline gaps. All judgment flows through one evaluation model family despite deterministic aggregation. Reconstruction fidelity is a proxy; a graph can reconstruct well yet miss knowledge a downstream planner needs, though RQ4 partially addresses reuse. And the outer loop's pseudo-training operates on six clips, so the transferable encoding rules were learned from a sample far too small to trust for coverage of cinematic styles.

Bottom line: AVA-Encoder makes a credible case that structured, reconstruction-testable knowledge graphs beat dense captions as agent-native video representations, and its gated dual-loop evolution is a reusable recipe for any domain where you can define a fixed decoder. The 20.7-point margin rests on eighteen clips—replicate on your own footage before treating it as established.
