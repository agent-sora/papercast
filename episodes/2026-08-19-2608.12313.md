AVA-Encoder: Towards Agent-Native Video Representation Learning. First authors Chuyue Li, Jinpeng Yu, and Haozhe Wang, from the Qwen Business Unit of Alibaba, ShanghaiTech University, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, the Institute of Computing Technology, and Southeast University. The problem: video creation agents cannot learn filmmaking from finished films, because a rendered video is not an operable record of the decisions that produced it. The proposal is to turn films into structured knowledge graphs that agents can read, edit, and reconstruct from—and to use reconstruction error itself as the training signal.

The task formulation is an agentic auto-encoder. Three parts. An encoder governed by a policy implemented as three system prompts covering film-level, shot-level, and keyframe-level understanding. A bottleneck that is not a neural embedding but a Film Knowledge Graph. And a fixed decoder: a static rendering pipeline calling frozen generation models as tools. Because the decoder never changes, any difference in reconstruction quality measures the representation. That is the entire evaluation philosophy—reconstruction fidelity as a proxy for representation faithfulness.

The Film KG schema is worth studying if you build agent-facing representations. Ten node types: nine structured-text nodes plus keyframe nodes. The text side holds the narrative hierarchy—Story, Event, Shot—plus six shot-level state nodes: Character, Scene, Object, Style, Camera, and Audio. Eleven edge types in three groups: production edges—Contains for hierarchy, Binds connecting shots to their states, References linking keyframes to assets; temporal edges—Transition, Sequence, Jump preserving order and non-adjacent recurrences; semantic edges—SpokenBy, Rel, Similar, Features, Narrative capturing speakers, entity relations, visual similarity, and narrative dependencies like cause, setup, and callback. An asset layer stores generated keyframe images, character references, audio, and shot videos. One rule does heavy lifting: no frame, crop, or screenshot from the source film may enter the asset layer or reach the generators. Every asset must be regenerated from structured text. That forces the graph, not pixels, to carry all cinematic information.

The optimization is dual-loop textual-gradient evolution, TextGrad-style but staged. Outer loop, data-agnostic encoding policy pseudo-training: across training videos, reconstruction failures are diagnosed into textual gradients, candidate revisions to the shot- and keyframe-level prompts are proposed by one model and scored by another, and accepted corrections accumulate into transferable encoding rules, with an anti-forgetting gate rejecting updates that regress previously processed clips. Inner loop, data-dependent refinement at test time: same cycle applied only to the current video's graph instance, guarded by an anti-degradation gate preventing gains on one dimension from degrading others. The two loops share a gated accept-reject discipline but optimize different objects—the shared policy versus the per-video graph—and can be run independently or sequentially.

Experimental setup is small-scale and fully disclosed, which I appreciate even as it limits conclusions. Six clips for pseudo-training, eighteen non-overlapping evaluation clips spanning animation, human-directed AI shorts, and classic cinema. Frozen models in five roles: Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview for both video understanding and evaluation, Qwen 3.7 Plus as the modification model—which notably never scores its own revisions—Nano Banana Pro for images, HappyHorse 1.0 for reference-to-video generation. Fairness protocol: every compared representation gets the identical interface to the fixed decoder, at most five newly generated reference keyframes and 1,200 shot-prompt tokens per shot. Evaluation runs four comparison directions—direct video comparison, direct keyframe comparison, video back-captioning, and keyframe back-captioning—each scored over eight dimensions including Character, Motion, Audio, Camera, and Narrative. Crucially, the vision-language model produces factual QA judgments and checklist items under fixed prompts, and deterministic machine rules—not model opinion—aggregate those into final scores.

Headline result: AVA-Encoder reaches 49.0 percent overall against 28.3 percent for soap2soap, the strongest external baseline among VideoAnalyzer, Storyboard Studio, and soap2soap. Per direction: 57.8 versus 36.7 on direct video, 73.7 versus 39.5 on keyframes, 29.7 versus 15.8 on back-captioned video. That is a 20.7-point absolute gap, 73.1 percent relative. Large—but remember all systems share the same decoder, so this cleanly measures representation quality rather than generation skill, which is exactly what they intended to isolate.

The ablation table answers where the points come from. A naive single-level encoder manages 27.5 overall; hierarchical understanding alone lifts it to 42.4, an 18.3-point gain, so structure dominates everything else. Adding inner-loop graph refinement brings 45.4; policy pseudo-training instead brings 45.8; both loops together reach 49.0, a combined gain of 6.6 points over neither. Removing acceptance gates costs 5.5 points, dropping performance to 43.5—ungated self-improvement actively damages representations through regressions. The sharpest finding for anyone writing system prompts by hand: the pseudo-trained policy beats an independently human-tuned policy 45.8 versus 44.4 while cutting the shot-level prompt from 31,336 to 8,052 tokens—a 74.3 percent reduction—and the keyframe-level prompt from 13,574 to 4,062, a 70.1 percent cut. Automated prompt evolution produced shorter prompts that outperform expert curation. That is a small margin on 18 clips, so treat the direction, not the delta, as the finding.

Operability results are qualitative but concrete: identity replacement edits propagate through dependent shots consistently—change a registered character and the affected keyframes, dialogue, and actions update while unrelated content stays intact—with the affected subgraph computed from typed dependencies. Downstream reuse feeds the complete representation as plain text into four external video-generation frameworks with no adapters; every framework improves on every dimension, with MovieAgent rising from 2.47 to 3.30 overall on a five-point scale.

Limitations deserve emphasis because the paper's own framing invites over-reading. Eighteen evaluation clips and six training clips is tiny; no confidence intervals are reported for the headline gaps. All scoring flows through one evaluation model family despite the deterministic aggregation layer. The benchmark, dataset, and framework are released, which makes verification possible—so verify. If your agents consume video, the schema design and the no-source-pixels rule transfer immediately; the specific numbers do not.

Bottom line: AVA-Encoder makes a credible case that structured, reconstruction-testable knowledge graphs beat dense captions as agent-native video representations, and its gated dual-loop evolution is a reusable recipe. The 20.7-point margin rests on eighteen clips—replicate on your own footage before treating it as established.
